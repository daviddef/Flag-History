Flags of Cuba

Cuba's flag story is short but strange: the design flown today was drawn in exile in New York in 1849, carried ashore by a failed invasion the following year, fought and died under for three decades of rebellion, tolerated but denied official status through four years of US military rule, and only then — in 1902 — finally raised as a sovereign nation's own banner. It has not changed once since, through revolution, embargo, and Cold War confrontation alike.

Behind that single modern design lie four centuries of colonial Spain, marked here by the Cross of Burgundy that flew over the whole Spanish Empire rather than over Cuba specifically, and, deeper still, an indigenous Taíno and Ciboney Cuba from which no flag survives at all.

five blue & white bars, red hoist triangle, Lone Star — unchanged since 1902
1902 – present

Republic of Cuba

Republic

Who & why: the Republic of Cuba is formally inaugurated on 20 May 1902 as the US military government withdraws, and the rebel Lone Star flag of the independence wars is adopted as the official national flag. Cuba's early decades are shadowed by the Platt Amendment (repealed 1934), which reserved the US a right of intervention; the 1959 revolution led by Fidel Castro then overturns that republic's government entirely, installing a one-party socialist state that endures to this day — a sweeping change of government and ideology, but not, at any point, of flag.

What it represents: the same "Lone Star" design first drawn in 1849: three blue stripes for the island's original three military districts and two white stripes for the purity of the independence cause, a red equilateral triangle at the hoist for liberty, equality, and fraternity, and a single white five-pointed star standing for Cuban sovereignty among nations.

1898 – 1902

US military occupation of Cuba

Foreign military occupation

Who & why: Spain cedes control after its defeat in the 1898 Spanish–American War, and the United States administers Cuba as a military government under generals John R. Brooke and then Leonard Wood. The 1898 Teller Amendment had already forsworn US annexation, so occupation is framed from the start as temporary; Cubans nonetheless fly their own Lone Star flag symbolically throughout this period, even though it holds no official legal status until independence itself is proclaimed on 20 May 1902.

What it represents: the flag shown here is the flag of record for the administering power — the United States flag of the era, simplified — not a Cuban national flag, since Cuba had none with legal standing during these four years.

1868 – 1898

Flag of Cuban independence (Lone Star)

Independence movement / insurgency

Who & why: the design was drawn up in 1849 by exiled revolutionary Narciso López, with the poet Miguel Teurbe Tolón, in New York, and first raised on Cuban soil at Cárdenas in May 1850 during López's failed invasion attempt. It became the banner of Carlos Manuel de Céspedes's Ten Years' War (1868–1878) and, a generation later, of José Martí, Máximo Gómez, and Antonio Maceo's War of Independence (1895–1898) — the same flag, carried by rebels for three decades before any government of Cuba's own existed to fly it officially.

What it represents: the exact design later formalized as the national flag in 1902 — five blue-and-white stripes, red triangle, single white star — already carried the same symbolism of liberty and sovereignty as an insurgent banner, long before it had any legal standing.

1515 – 1868

Spanish colonial Cuba

Spanish colony

Who & why: Diego Velázquez de Cuéllar conquers and settles the island between 1511 and 1515, founding Havana and six other original villas; Havana grows into Spain's key Caribbean naval and treasure-fleet hub, the gathering point for convoys sailing home to Seville. "National flag" is an anachronistic concept for a colonial possession of this era — Spain's own home flags changed too, most notably to the red–yellow–red Bourbon naval ensign adopted in 1785 — but across nearly all of this span, the standard reference symbol used for Spanish territories including Cuba is the Cross of Burgundy: a red saltire of knotted branches on a white field, the maritime and military banner of the Spanish Habsburg and Bourbon empires alike.

What it represents: a red diagonal cross of stylized knotted tree branches (Saint Andrew's cross form) on white, used across Spain's colonial possessions as a military and naval banner rather than a single "national" symbol in the modern sense.

1492 – 1515

Taíno and Ciboney Cuba / early Spanish contact

Indigenous / pre-colonial

Who & why: Christopher Columbus sights Cuba in October 1492 during his first voyage, encountering an island organized into Taíno cacicazgos (chiefdoms) in the more populous east and center, with Ciboney communities in parts of the west. Spanish conquest and settlement is gradual rather than immediate, only completed when Diego Velázquez brings the island fully under Spanish control by 1515.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from Cuba's indigenous societies. Taíno and Ciboney political and religious life used cemi iconography, body paint, and other symbolic forms rather than heraldic banners in the European sense, so this entry is shown honestly blank rather than inventing a flag that never existed.
