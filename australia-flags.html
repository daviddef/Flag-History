Flags of Australia

Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples have lived on this continent for at least 65,000 years — home to the world's oldest continuous living cultures, organized into hundreds of distinct language groups and nations long before any European sail appeared on the horizon. This page's flag lineage proper begins where the documentary record of flags itself begins: British colonization from 1788. No flag is invented to stand in for the pre-colonial era; it is shown here honestly, without one.

From a single penal colony at Sydney Cove, six separate British colonies grew up across the continent over the following century, each self-governing by the 1850s–90s but never politically united until they federated themselves, peacefully and by referendum, into the Commonwealth of Australia on 1 January 1901 — the flag chosen that year, only lightly formalized since, is the one still flown today.

Scope of this page: the territory of present-day Australia, pre-1788 to present, three entries. No interactive map on this page — see the Pacific hub for the region-wide overview. Part of a larger atlas — see the linked pages at the bottom.

1901 – present

Commonwealth of Australia

Federation, independent

Who & why: the six British colonies — New South Wales, Victoria, Queensland, South Australia, Western Australia, and Tasmania — vote by referendum to federate, becoming the Commonwealth of Australia on 1 January 1901. A public design competition that same year produces the winning flag; the Commonwealth government formally fixes it in law as the Australian National Flag under the Flags Act 1953, effective 1954, with no change to the design itself.

What it represents: the Union Jack in the canton marks the federation's origin within the British Empire and Commonwealth; the large seven-pointed Commonwealth Star below it originally stood for the six founding states plus the territories; five white stars on the fly form the Southern Cross, the constellation visible only from the southern hemisphere.

1788 – 1901

British colonial Australia (six colonies)

Colony

Who & why: Britain establishes a penal colony at Sydney Cove on 26 January 1788, transporting convicts to what it names New South Wales. Five further colonies split off or are separately founded across the century — Tasmania (1825), Western Australia (1829), South Australia (1836), Victoria (1851), and Queensland (1859) — each gaining its own responsible self-government by the second half of the 19th century while remaining separate British possessions with no shared government until federation.

What it represents: the Union Jack, Britain's own flag, flew as the formal colonial ensign throughout; each colony later flew its own Blue Ensign variant with a distinct badge in the fly, but the shared Union Jack canton is the constant across the whole pre-federation era.

Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Australia

Indigenous nations, pre-contact

Who & why: archaeological evidence places Aboriginal peoples on the continent for at least 65,000 years, organized into several hundred distinct nations and language groups with their own law, land custodianship, and oral tradition — the oldest continuous living culture documented anywhere in the world. Torres Strait Islander peoples, culturally and linguistically distinct, inhabit the islands between Australia and New Guinea. No single continent-wide political entity ever existed in this era, and none is invented here to stand in for one.

What it represents: flags as a European heraldic form were never part of pre-contact Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander culture; this entry is left honestly blank rather than retrofitted with a symbol. The Aboriginal Flag and Torres Strait Islander Flag now in wide use were both designed in the 1970s–90s, well within the modern era, and are outside this page's pre-1788 scope.
