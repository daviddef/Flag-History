Flags of Peru

Peru was the heart of the Inca Empire, Tawantinsuyu, the largest pre-Columbian state in the Americas, expanding across the Andes from the 1200s and governed from its capital at Cusco — until Francisco Pizarro's conquest of 1532–33, in which he captured and executed the Sapa Inca Atahualpa at Cajamarca and took Cusco itself, ending independent Inca rule.

What followed was the Viceroyalty of Peru, organized by 1542 as the Spanish empire's major South American administrative and mining center, with Lima as vice-regal capital and the silver of Potosí (in modern Bolivia, but administered under this viceroyalty for most of the colonial period) funding much of Spain's imperial wealth. Independence came in stages: proclaimed by José de San Martín in Lima in 1821, but only secured militarily three years later, when Simón Bolívar and Antonio José de Sucre's victory at the Battle of Ayacucho in December 1824 effectively ended Spanish rule across South America.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Peru, c. 1200s (Inca era)–present, five political entities. No genuine internal governing split exists within Peru's own modern borders at any point covered here, so — like the atlas's other single-entity pages — this one carries no internal-partition map. 1825 – present

Republic of Peru (modern)

Republic

Who & why: the republic's political history includes real turbulence — a long 19th- and 20th-century run of military governments, coups, and short-lived constitutions, along with the War of the Pacific (1879–84), in which Peru, allied with Bolivia, lost southern territory to Chile. Democratic civilian rule has been the norm since 2001. Through all of it, the flag itself has been essentially unchanged since Simón Bolívar fixed its current design in 1825.

What it represents: a vertical red-white-red triband. A version bearing the national coat of arms in the white band is reserved for state and official use; the plain triband shown here is the civil flag flown by ordinary citizens.

1821 – 1824

War of independence

Revolutionary government

Who & why: José de San Martín, arriving from the south after campaigns in Argentina and Chile, proclaims Peruvian independence in Lima on 28 July 1821. Spanish royalist forces continue to hold much of the highlands for three more years, until Simón Bolívar and Antonio José de Sucre's decisive victory at the Battle of Ayacucho on 9 December 1824 effectively ends Spanish colonial rule across South America.

What it represents: San Martín's original 1820 red-and-white design, sometimes shown with a golden rising-sun emblem, is the precursor to the red-white-red triband that Bolívar fixes permanently in 1825.

1542 – 1821

Viceroyalty of Peru (Spanish colonial)

Colony

Who & why: established in 1542, the viceroyalty makes Lima the seat of Spain's major South American administration for nearly three centuries, overseeing at its peak most of Spanish-held South America before the 18th-century creation of the Viceroyalty of New Granada and the Viceroyalty of Río de la Plata carves off large portions of that territory. Potosí's silver mines, administered from Lima until 1776, fund much of the Spanish empire's wealth.

What it represents: no distinct Peruvian flag exists under colonial rule; ships and forts fly variants of the Spanish royal ensign, shown here in a simplified form representative of the period rather than any single fixed design.

1532 – 1542

Spanish conquest period

Conquest

Who & why: Francisco Pizarro lands on the Peruvian coast in 1532, capturing and executing the Sapa Inca Atahualpa at Cajamarca after collecting an enormous ransom in gold and silver. By 1533 Pizarro's forces take Cusco, ending independent Inca rule; a full viceregal administration is not formally organized until 1542, so this decade is a transitional conquest period under provisional Spanish military and colonial authority.

What it represents: no distinct flag was created for this transitional decade; Pizarro's expedition and the early conquest administration operated under the same Spanish royal banner later flown over the formal viceroyalty, shown here identically for that reason.

c. 1200s – 1532

Inca Empire (Tawantinsuyu)

Empire, indigenous

Who & why: the largest pre-Columbian state in the Americas, centered on Cusco, expands across the Andes from the 1200s–1400s to its greatest extent under Huayna Capac in the early 1500s, stretching along much of the Pacific coast and highlands of South America. A succession crisis and civil war between his sons Atahualpa and Huascar weaken the empire just before Pizarro's arrival in 1532.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from the Inca state. The colorful "rainbow flag" popularly associated with Cusco today is a 20th-century invention, not a genuine Inca-era banner, and is not shown here. Some reconstructions depict a stylized sun disc, tied to Inti worship at the heart of Inca religion, but this remains an informed, non-official device rather than a documented flag — shown here honestly blank instead.
