Flags of the Roman Empire

A single continuous state, by its own reckoning, for roughly twenty-two centuries: from a legendary 753 BCE founding on the Palatine Hill, through five centuries as the Republic of SPQR, the transition to one-man rule under Augustus in 27 BCE, a near-collapse and reinvention in the third century, a new capital at Constantinople, a permanent East/West split in 395 CE, the West's fall in 476 CE — and a continuing Eastern half, what historians call the Byzantine Empire, that outlived the West by nearly a thousand years before Ottoman conquest finally ended it in 1453.

Rome predates the vector flag by well over a millennium; its identifying symbol was the vexillum, a square banner hung from a horizontal crossbar, and above all the eagle (aquila) standard carried by each legion. This page traces that standard's evolution honestly — reconstructed where pre-heraldic, moving toward the double-headed Byzantine eagle that later became one of history's most copied imperial emblems.

Scope of this page: Rome and its direct continuation, the Byzantine/Eastern Roman Empire, c. 753 BCE – 1453 CE, told as one throughline rather than two separate stories, since Byzantine emperors and subjects called themselves Romans (Rhωmaîoi) until the very end. This page does not attempt to cover every modern country that once sat inside Roman borders — Italy, Greece, and Turkey are its clearest geographic heirs, each covered in this atlas's own regional hubs. 1453 CE

Fall of Constantinople

Conquered

Who & why: Sultan Mehmed II's Ottoman forces breach Constantinople's ancient land walls on 29 May 1453 after a seven-week siege, using massive siege cannon against defenses that had held for over a thousand years; Emperor Constantine XI Palaiologos dies fighting in the breach. This ends the Roman state for good, nearly 2,206 years after its traditional founding, and Constantinople becomes the Ottoman capital.

What it represents: the closing point of this page's arc. The Ottoman Empire that succeeds Byzantium in this same capital city is covered on its own page in this atlas.

330 – 1453 CE

Byzantine Empire (Eastern Roman continuation)

Empire, continuing

Who & why: Constantine the Great founds Constantinople on the site of Byzantium in 330 CE as a new eastern capital; after the West's fall in 476, the Eastern Empire endures, calling itself simply "Roman" throughout. Justinian I (527–565) briefly reconquers Italy, North Africa, and part of Spain and codifies Roman law; the empire survives crusader occupation (1204–1261), slow territorial loss, and repeated sieges before finally falling in 1453 — nearly a thousand years after the West.

What it represents: the double-headed eagle, looking both east and west, becomes the empire's definitive dynastic emblem under the Palaiologos dynasty (from the 13th century), evolving from the single-headed Roman aquila — shown here as an informed reconstruction of its later, most iconic imperial form.

395 – 476 CE

Permanent East/West split & the West's fall

Split empire

Who & why: Emperor Theodosius I dies in 395 CE and divides the empire between his two sons for good — earlier splits under Diocletian's Tetrarchy had been administrative, but this one never reunifies. The Western Roman Empire, weakened by repeated barbarian incursions, sees Rome itself sacked twice (410 by the Visigoths, 455 by the Vandals) before the Germanic general Odoacer deposes the last western emperor, Romulus Augustulus, in 476 CE — the conventional end date for "the fall of Rome."

What it represents: both halves continue to use Roman legionary and imperial symbolism, but they now answer to separate courts and separate fates — the West dissolving into Germanic successor kingdoms, the East persisting as what later historians call Byzantium.

284 – 395 CE

Crisis, Tetrarchy & Constantine's Christian turn

Empire, reorganized

Who & why: after the near-collapse of the Crisis of the Third Century (military anarchy, civil wars, economic breakdown), Diocletian restores order from 284 CE and splits administration into a four-ruler Tetrarchy to manage the empire's unwieldy size. Constantine the Great reunifies sole rule by 324, founds Constantinople as a new capital in 330, and, after his own conversion, sets Christianity on the path to becoming the empire's dominant faith — formalized as the state religion under Theodosius I in 380.

What it represents: the labarum, a military standard bearing the Chi-Rho Christogram, is adopted by Constantine after his reported vision before the Battle of the Milvian Bridge (312 CE) — the first explicitly Christian imperial symbol, replacing the old pagan legionary standards. Shown here as a reconstruction.

27 BCE – 284 CE

Roman Empire (Principate)

Empire, imperial rule

Who & why: Octavian, having defeated Mark Antony and Cleopatra at Actium (31 BCE), is granted the title Augustus by the Senate in 27 BCE, formally ending the Republic while preserving its institutions as a facade — the beginning of one-man imperial rule. Rome reaches its greatest territorial extent under Trajan (117 CE), spanning from Britain to Mesopotamia, before the mid-3rd-century Crisis brings the empire to the edge of collapse.

What it represents: the aquila (eagle) standard, carried by every legion since Marius's reforms (104 BCE), remains Rome's defining military and imperial emblem straight through the transition from Republic to Empire, still marked with the old Republican motto SPQR (Senatus Populusque Romanus — the Senate and People of Rome).

509 – 27 BCE

Roman Republic

Republic

Who & why: tradition holds that the last king, Tarquin the Proud, is overthrown in 509 BCE, replaced by a republic governed by elected magistrates and the Senate. Rome expands from a single city-state to control the entire Italian peninsula, then the wider Mediterranean through the Punic Wars against Carthage (264–146 BCE) and conquests in Gaul, Greece, and the East — while internal political strain, culminating in Julius Caesar's dictatorship and assassination (44 BCE) and the civil wars that follow, brings the Republic itself to an end.

What it represents: SPQR (Senatus Populusque Romanus) is the Republic's defining formula, stamped on standards, monuments, and later even manhole covers — a claim that sovereignty rests jointly with the Senate and the Roman people, carried by the vexillum banners of the legions.

753 – 509 BCE

Roman Kingdom

Kingdom, legendary founding

Who & why: Roman tradition dates the city's founding to 753 BCE, by Romulus (who becomes its first king after killing his twin brother Remus in the founding myth). Seven kings rule in the traditional account, ending with the tyrannical Tarquin the Proud, whose overthrow in 509 BCE establishes the Republic. Much of this era is legendary rather than securely documented history, though archaeology confirms a genuine early settlement on the site.

What it represents: no flag or fixed banner is attested from this earliest, semi-legendary period — shown here honestly blank rather than inventing one, consistent with this atlas's treatment of other pre-heraldic eras.
