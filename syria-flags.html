Flags of Syria

From ancient Levantine city-states and a Roman-then-Byzantine province, to the century when Damascus itself was the capital of an empire stretching from Iberia to the Indus, through Mamluk and four centuries of Ottoman rule, a French Mandate, independence, a brief union with Egypt, more than five decades of Assad family rule, and the government's fall in December 2024 — twelve flags across roughly 2,600 years of documented history.

This page sticks to dates, names, and documents throughout, including for the most recent entries. Where the record reaches into events still unfolding or freshly settled, it states only what is widely reported and avoids characterizing anything still contested.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Syria, antiquity–present, twelve political entities. No interactive map or internal-region view is included here — that layer is deferred project-wide to a later phase. Part of a larger atlas — see the linked pages at the bottom.

2024 – present

Transitional government

Transitional government

Who & why: in early December 2024, an opposition offensive led by Hayat Tahrir al-Sham advanced rapidly across the country and captured Damascus, ending 54 years of Assad family rule. A transitional authority took over administration of the country. This is very recent history; this page sticks to what has been widely reported — the change of government and the change of flag — without characterizing events still unfolding.

What it represents: the transitional authorities readopted the green-white-black tricolor with three red stars, the flag first used at independence in the 1930s–40s, in place of the red-white-black two-star flag flown by the Ba'athist government since 1980.

1980 – 2024

Ba'athist Syrian Arab Republic

Ba'athist republic

Who & why: the Ba'ath Party had held power since a 1963 coup; Hafez al-Assad, already the dominant figure since a 1970 internal takeover, formalized his presidency in 1971, beginning Assad family rule. His son Bashar al-Assad succeeded him in 2000. A civil war began in 2011 and continued for thirteen years, ending with the government's fall in December 2024.

What it represents: a red-white-black tricolor with two green stars, adopted in 1980 — visually similar to, but a historically distinct design from, the 1958–61 United Arab Republic flag below.

1961 – 1980

Syrian Arab Republic (post-union)

Republic, independent

Who & why: Syria seceded from the United Arab Republic in September 1961 and restored its own institutions and flag. The 1963 coup that brought the Ba'ath Party to power falls within this span; the flag itself did not change again until 1980.

What it represents: a return to the 1932 green-white-black tricolor with three red stars, the same design flown at independence in 1946.

1958 – 1961

United Arab Republic (union with Egypt)

Union

Who & why: Syria and Egypt merged into a single state, the United Arab Republic, in February 1958, under President Gamal Abdel Nasser. The union dissolved in September 1961 after a military coup in Damascus.

What it represents: a red-white-black tricolor with two green stars representing the two constituent republics — a design visually close to, but a historically distinct instance from, the 1980 Ba'athist-era flag above.

1946 – 1958

Syrian Republic (independence)

Republic, independent

Who & why: full French military withdrawal was completed on 17 April 1946, still marked annually as Evacuation Day. The Syrian Republic, whose institutions had already existed under the French Mandate since 1930, became fully sovereign.

What it represents: the green-white-black tricolor with three red stars, originally adopted by the Mandate-era Syrian Republic in 1932 and carried forward unchanged into full independence.

1920 – 1946

French Mandate

Mandate

Who & why: Emir Faisal, who had led the Arab Revolt, proclaimed an independent Arab Kingdom of Syria in Damascus in March 1920; it lasted four months, ending when French troops defeated his small army at the Battle of Maysalun that July. The April 1920 San Remo Conference formalized a League of Nations Mandate for Syria and Lebanon under French administration, which continued until full withdrawal in 1946.

What it represents: the French tricolor flew as the mandatory power's flag; the Mandate-era Syrian Republic, established in 1930, flew its own green-white-black tricolor with three stars alongside it from 1932 on — the same flag independent Syria later kept.

1517 – 1918

Ottoman Empire (Damascus and Aleppo eyalets)

Empire province

Who & why: Ottoman Sultan Selim I broke Mamluk power at Marj Dabiq near Aleppo in 1516, absorbing the region into Ottoman provinces run by appointed pashas answering to Constantinople. Four centuries of continuous Ottoman rule followed, the longest single-power era the region experienced since the coming of Islam, ending with the Ottoman collapse at the close of World War I in 1918.

What it represents: the Ottoman state flag, a red field with a white crescent and star, flown across all Ottoman provincial territory including the eyalets of Damascus and Aleppo.

1260 – 1517

Mamluk Sultanate

Sultanate

Who & why: the Mamluks, a slave-soldier caste that had seized the Egyptian sultanate from the Ayyubids in 1250, crushed a Mongol invasion at Ain Jalut in 1260 and absorbed inland Syria the same year, ruling the region through a network of Cairo-appointed governors until the 1516–17 Ottoman conquest.

What it represents: Mamluk heraldry used gold and other emblematic blazons tied to individual sultans and officers rather than one fixed state flag; the design here is an informed reconstruction of the era's common gold-field, dark-emblem convention, not a documented single national banner.

1174 – 1260

Zengid & Ayyubid Damascus

Sultanate

Who & why: Nur ad-Din's Zengid dynasty took Damascus in 1154 and led a Sunni military revival against the Crusader states; his general Saladin succeeded him, founded the Ayyubid dynasty, and retook Jerusalem in 1187. Ayyubid rule over Damascus and inland Syria continued until the Mongol invasion of 1260, which the Mamluks then repelled and replaced with their own rule.

What it represents: no single fixed state flag is documented for this era; the dark field with a plain emblem here is an honest placeholder representing the period's Sunni Turkic and Kurdish-founded dynastic rule rather than a specific attested banner.

750 – 1174

Abbasid suzerainty and local dynasties

Sultanate

Who & why: the 750 Abbasid revolution shifted the caliphate's capital to Baghdad, and direct Abbasid control over Syria gradually loosened over the following centuries as regional governors and successor dynasties — including the Tulunids, Ikhshidids, Hamdanids, and Fatimid-appointed administrators — exercised effective local rule, formally or nominally under Abbasid or Fatimid suzerainty. This long, genuinely fragmented span is treated here as one honest bridging entry rather than a set of invented precise sub-periods. It ends when the Zengid dynasty under Nur ad-Din unified Damascus and much of inland Syria in 1174.

What it represents: plain black is the color consistently associated with Abbasid banners; local and successor dynasties across this span flew their own emblems, none of which is treated here as the single flag of Syria as a whole.

636 – 750

Umayyad Caliphate (Damascus as imperial capital)

Caliphate

Who & why: Byzantine Syria fell to Rashidun Arab armies in the 630s, the decisive blow landing at the Battle of the Yarmouk in 636. Mu'awiya, first governor of Syria under the Rashidun caliphs, founded the Umayyad Caliphate in 661 and made Damascus its capital — for nearly a century, the seat of an empire stretching from Iberia to the Indus ran through this one city, a real golden age for Damascus as an imperial center. The dynasty ended in 750, when the Abbasid revolution overthrew it and shifted the caliphate's center of gravity east to Baghdad.

What it represents: plain white is the color most consistently associated with Umayyad banners in the historical record, distinguishing them from the Abbasids' black and the Fatimids' green that followed. No single fixed design beyond the plain field is reliably documented.

antiquity – 636

Ancient & Byzantine Syria

No flag documented

Who & why: the lands of present-day Syria carried some of the ancient Near East's earliest urban civilizations — the Bronze Age kingdom of Ebla, the trading city of Ugarit, and later Aramaean kingdoms centered on Damascus — before falling successively to Assyrian, Babylonian, Achaemenid Persian, and then Alexander's Hellenistic conquest. Seleucid Greek rule gave way to Rome by the 1st century BCE, and the province continued under Byzantine (Eastern Roman) administration until the Rashidun Arab conquest that began in the 630s and culminated at the Battle of the Yarmouk in 636.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from this pre-heraldic span; national and civic flags as a concept postdate this entire era. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
