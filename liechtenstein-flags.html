Flags of Liechtenstein

A principality assembled on purpose: in 1699 and 1712 the wealthy Liechtenstein family, already prominent in the Habsburg court but landless in a way that mattered for imperial politics, bought two small lordships along the upper Rhine specifically because owning territory held directly of the Holy Roman Emperor — rather than as a vassal of some other prince — qualified a family for a seat in the Imperial Diet. Vaduz and Schellenberg were united into the Principality of Liechtenstein in 1719, named for a family that would not set foot in the place for decades.

What began as a legal instrument for court standing outlived the Empire that created it: Napoleon dissolved the Holy Roman Empire in 1806 and folded Liechtenstein into his Confederation of the Rhine, then the 1815 Congress of Vienna moved it into the German Confederation, and only that body's own 1866 dissolution left the principality fully sovereign for the first time — a status it has held, continuously and without invasion, ever since.

Scope of this page: the territory of the two former lordships of Vaduz and Schellenberg, now the modern Principality of Liechtenstein, 1719–present, four political entities. No internal governing split occurs within Liechtenstein's own borders at any point in this span, so this page carries no partition map. Part of a larger atlas — see the linked pages at the bottom.

1937 – present

Principality of Liechtenstein (modern, with crown)

Principality, independentFranz IRoman Catholic (constitutionally established, National Church)

Who & why: after discovering at the 1936 Berlin Olympics that Liechtenstein's plain blue-and-red flag was identical to Haiti's, the principality adds a gold princely crown to the canton in 1937 to distinguish it. Liechtenstein stays neutral through both World Wars, joins the UN only in 1990, and today runs one of the world's highest per-capita incomes on finance, industry, and (fittingly, given its 1719 origin) a still-reigning princely house.

What it represents: the same blue-over-red bicolor traced to the 19th century, now marked with a gold crown signifying the ruling prince, formally adopted by law in 1937 and unchanged since.

1866 – 1937

Principality of Liechtenstein (fully sovereign, plain bicolor)

Principality, independentJohann IIRoman Catholic (traditional, National Church)

Who & why: the German Confederation, which had absorbed the old Holy Roman Empire's successor states after 1815, dissolves itself in 1866 following the Austro-Prussian War. With no overlord left above it, Liechtenstein becomes fully sovereign for the first time in its history. It disbands its small army in 1868 as an economy measure and, having no plausible path to defending itself militarily, commits instead to permanent neutrality — a stance that keeps it out of both World Wars.

What it represents: the blue-over-red bicolor, drawn from the Liechtenstein princely house's own livery colors and in use since at least the mid-19th century, flown here plain, before the 1937 addition of the crown.

1815 – 1866

Principality of Liechtenstein (German Confederation member)

Confederation memberJohann IRoman Catholic (traditional, National Church)

Who & why: the 1815 Congress of Vienna, redrawing Europe after Napoleon's defeat, folds the former Confederation of the Rhine states into a new German Confederation, a loose association of 39 sovereign German-speaking states under Austrian presidency. Liechtenstein keeps its own prince and internal government throughout, as it had under Napoleon's arrangement, but remains bound into a larger confederal structure rather than standing fully alone.

What it represents: Liechtenstein flew no single fixed national flag distinct from its princely house arms during this period; shown here schematically as the princely red-and-gold heraldic coloring used on seals and court livery of the era, the direct ancestor of the blue-and-red flag adopted later in the century.

1719 – 1815

Principality of Liechtenstein (Holy Roman Empire, then Confederation of the Rhine)

Confederation memberAnton FlorianRoman Catholic (established, National Church)

Who & why: Emperor Charles VI unites the purchased lordships of Vaduz (bought 1712) and Schellenberg (bought 1699) into the Principality of Liechtenstein on 23 January 1719, elevating it to a status held directly of the Empire — the qualification the Liechtenstein family had been buying territory to secure. The principality remains a minor Imperial Diet member until Napoleon dissolves the Holy Roman Empire in 1806 and absorbs it into his new Confederation of the Rhine, under French protection, still with its own prince.

What it represents: no distinct Liechtenstein territorial flag is documented from this founding era; the principality was represented, when at all, by its ruling family's heraldic arms rather than a fixed national banner. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
