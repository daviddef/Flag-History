Flags of Cape Verde

Cape Verde starts this atlas from a genuinely different point than any of its West African neighbors: the archipelago was uninhabited when Portuguese navigators reached it around 1460, so there is no pre-colonial indigenous polity to cover here — the story begins with settlement itself. From that founding, five centuries of Portuguese rule turn the islands into a notorious waypoint of the Atlantic slave trade and, from that same mixing, one of the first Atlantic Creole societies. Independence in 1975 arrives peacefully, through the same liberation movement that fought a war next door in Guinea-Bissau — and a deliberate 1992 flag change marks Cape Verde's own break from that shared origin toward a distinct national identity.

Six flags in all: the Kingdom of Portugal's royal banner carried by the first settlers, that same kingdom's flag through the slave-trade centuries, a mid-19th-century constitutional update, the green-and-red Republic flag that flew through the independence struggle, the red-yellow-green PAIGC-style flag Cape Verde shared with Guinea-Bissau at independence, and finally the blue, white-red-white, ten-gold-star flag adopted in 1992.

Scope of this page: the archipelago of Cape Verde, c. 1460–present, six flags. Unlike most pages in this atlas, there is no fragmented pre-colonial chieftaincy or kingdom chapter — the islands were uninhabited before Portuguese discovery and settlement, so the timeline begins there. No internal governing split exists at any point in Cape Verde's history, so this page carries no internal-partition map. Part of a larger atlas — see the linked pages at the bottom.

1992 – present

Republic of Cape Verde (multi-party)

Republic, democratic

Who & why: Cape Verde holds its first free multi-party elections in January 1991, peacefully ending sixteen years of one-party PAICV rule, and a new flag is adopted on 22 September 1992. The design is a deliberate, well-documented break from the old red-yellow-green PAIGC symbolism the country had shared with Guinea-Bissau since independence — a fresh national identity for a newly democratized state, not a modification of the old banner.

What it represents: a blue field for the surrounding Atlantic, crossed by a horizontal white-red-white band, with a circle of ten gold five-pointed stars set toward the hoist — each star standing for one of the archipelago's ten main islands, unified in a ring.

1975 – 1992

Independent Cape Verde under PAICV

One-party state

Who & why: Cape Verde becomes independent on 5 July 1975, through the same PAIGC (African Party for the Independence of Guinea and Cape Verde) that fought an armed war for Guinea-Bissau's independence. Founded by Amílcar Cabral, himself of mixed Cape Verdean and Guinean descent, PAIGC's founders originally envisioned an eventual union of the two countries; Cape Verde's own path to independence is peaceful, following the 25 April 1974 Carnation Revolution in Lisbon, in sharp contrast to Guinea-Bissau's armed struggle. The planned union never materializes, and after a 1980 coup in Guinea-Bissau severs the two parties for good, Cape Verde's branch renames itself PAICV (African Party for the Independence of Cape Verde), ruling as the sole legal party until 1990–91.

What it represents: a flag nearly identical to Guinea-Bissau's own — red vertical band at the hoist bearing a black star, with the remaining field split into yellow above and green below — a direct visual echo of the shared PAIGC origin the two nations were founded from together.

1910 – 1975

Portuguese Republic: colony, overseas province, and independence struggle

Colony

Who & why: Portugal's 1910 revolution topples the monarchy and the new Republic adopts its green-and-red flag in 1911 — the same flag Portugal still flies today — carried over Cape Verde for the rest of colonial rule. Under the authoritarian Estado Novo, Lisbon reclassifies Cape Verde in 1951 from "colony" to "overseas province," a rebranding meant to blunt growing international pressure for decolonization rather than any real change in status. Amílcar Cabral founds PAIGC in 1956 and leads an armed independence war centered in Guinea-Bissau from 1963; Cape Verde itself sees no comparable fighting, and its independence instead follows the 25 April 1974 Carnation Revolution, which overthrows the Estado Novo in Lisbon and opens the way to decolonization across Portugal's African territories. Cape Verde becomes independent on 5 July 1975.

What it represents: green for hope and red for the blood of those who died defending the nation, with a yellow armillary sphere (a navigational instrument recalling the Age of Discovery) and the old royal shield at center — Portugal's national flag, flown without change over Cape Verde through both the colonial and overseas-province eras and the independence struggle that ended them.

1830 – 1910

Portuguese colonial rule under the constitutional monarchy

Colony

Who & why: Portugal's own 19th-century shift from absolute to constitutional monarchy brings a new national flag, flown in turn over Cape Verde as an ordinary Portuguese colony administered from Lisbon. The archipelago's Creole society, built up over three centuries of settlement and the slave trade, continues to develop through this period, with Cape Verdeans increasingly serving in the colonial administration elsewhere in Portugal's African territories owing to their language and education.

What it represents: blue at the hoist and white at the fly, with the Portuguese royal arms centered on the color boundary — the flag of the constitutional Kingdom of Portugal, carried across its colonies until the 1910 republican revolution.

1495 – 1830

Portuguese colonial rule and the Atlantic slave trade

Colony

Who & why: for three and a half centuries, Cape Verde is administered as a colony of the Kingdom of Portugal. Its position off the West African coast makes it a key — and notorious — waypoint and staging post in the Atlantic slave trade: enslaved Africans are brought through Ribeira Grande (later Cidade Velha) and other island ports for sale, provisioning, and onward transport across the Atlantic, a dark and well-documented role the islands play for generations. The mixing of Portuguese settlers, colonial clergy and officials, and enslaved and free Africans produces one of the first Atlantic Creole societies, with a distinct Kriolu language and culture taking shape from this era.

What it represents: the Kingdom of Portugal's white flag bearing the royal quinas shield (five small blue shields recalling the wounds of Christ, arranged in a cross), the same banner carried by the earliest settlers and flown across Portugal's expanding maritime empire through this whole span.

c. 1460 – 1495

Discovery and founding of Ribeira Grande

Colony

Who & why: the Cape Verde archipelago is uninhabited when Portuguese navigators, traditionally credited to Diogo Gomes and António da Noli (a Genoese captain in Portuguese service), reach it around 1460 — a genuinely different starting point from every other page in this hub, with no indigenous polity to displace or absorb. Settlement follows quickly: Ribeira Grande, on Santiago, is founded in 1462 and becomes the first European colonial town built in the tropics, soon serving as a supply and trading base for Portugal's expanding voyages down the West African coast.

What it represents: the Kingdom of Portugal's own flag — a white field bearing the royal quinas shield — carried by the settlers and flown over the new colony from its very founding, the same banner Portugal flies across its wider maritime empire at this date.
