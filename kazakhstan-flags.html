Flags of Kazakhstan

From nomadic Turkic tribal confederations on the open steppe, through the Kazakh Khanate's founding in 1465 and its division into three great zhuzes, a staged Russian imperial conquest, decades as a Soviet republic, to independence in 1991 and the sky-blue flag of the world's largest landlocked country.

Six flags across a vast span of steppe history, ending with the region's mineral-wealthiest, most multi-vector-diplomatic state — balancing relationships with Russia, China, and the West since 1991.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Kazakhstan, antiquity–present, six political entities. No interactive map or internal-region view is included here — that layer is deferred project-wide to a later phase. Part of a larger atlas — see the linked pages at the bottom.

1991 – present

Republic of Kazakhstan

Republic, independent

Who & why: Kazakhstan declared independence on 16 December 1991, the last Soviet republic to do so before the USSR's formal dissolution. Since independence it has become the region's mineral-wealthiest state — oil, gas, uranium, and metals — and has pursued a deliberately multi-vector foreign policy, balancing relationships with Russia, China, and the West rather than aligning firmly with any one power.

What it represents: a sky-blue field bearing a golden sun of 32 rays with a soaring steppe eagle (berkut) beneath it, and a vertical strip of traditional Kazakh ornament (the koshkar-muyiz, or "ram's-horn," pattern) at the hoist, adopted in June 1992.

1936 – 1991

Kazakh SSR

Soviet republic

Who & why: Kazakhstan was elevated to a full Union Republic of the USSR in 1936, separated out from the Russian SFSR where it had existed as an autonomous republic. It remained a Soviet republic until the USSR's collapse in 1991.

What it represents: the standard Soviet red flag with a gold hammer and sickle and star in the canton, distinguished from the plain Soviet flag and other republics' variants by a light-blue horizontal stripe near the bottom edge — a common pattern among the Central Asian SSR flags.

1920 – 1936

Kirghiz ASSR / Kazak ASSR

Autonomous republic

Who & why: an autonomous republic within the Russian SFSR was established in 1920. It was initially named the "Kirghiz ASSR" — Russian officials of the time confusingly applied the name "Kirghiz" to the Kazakhs, to distinguish them from Cossacks, a naming quirk corrected in 1925 when the republic was renamed the Kazak ASSR. It was elevated to full Union Republic status in 1936. The short-lived Alash Autonomy (Alash Orda), a Kazakh national government that arose amid the 1917 Russian Revolution and civil war and used its own light-blue flag, was suppressed by Bolshevik forces before this republic's formal establishment.

What it represents: the Russian SFSR's own red flag with gold hammer-and-sickle canton, flown as the governing flag of the autonomous republic before it gained its own distinct Kazakh SSR flag in 1936.

1731 – 1920

Russian imperial conquest and rule

Empire province

Who & why: the Junior Zhuz accepted Russian protection in 1731, seeking help against Zhungar (Oirat Mongol) incursions; Russian conquest and administrative absorption of the Middle and Senior Zhuzes proceeded in stages over the following century and a half, with the steppe fully incorporated into Russian imperial administration (the Steppe Krai and Turkestan Governorate-General) by the 1860s–80s. This long, staged conquest is treated here as one honest bridging entry rather than invented precise sub-dates. In its final years, the short-lived Alash Autonomy (1917–1920), a Kazakh national government formed amid the Russian Revolution, briefly asserted self-rule before Bolshevik forces suppressed it.

What it represents: the Russian imperial tricolor, flown across all territory under Russian imperial administration including the Kazakh steppe.

1465 – 1731

Kazakh Khanate

Khanate

Who & why: Kerei Khan and Janibek Khan broke away from the Uzbek Khanate around 1465, founding the Kazakh Khanate and consolidating the steppe's nomadic Turkic tribes into three great zhuzes — the Senior, Middle, and Junior hordes — each with its own territory and leadership structure. The khanate's authority began to fray under pressure from Zhungar incursions in the early 18th century, leading the Junior Zhuz to seek Russian protection in 1731.

What it represents: no fixed national flag is documented for a nomadic khanate organized around tribal zhuzes rather than a single centralized state; individual clans used their own tamga (brand-like clan emblems). The design shown is an informed reconstruction of that tamga tradition, not a documented banner.

antiquity – 1465

Nomadic Turkic tribal confederations

No flag documented

Who & why: long before the Kazakh Khanate's founding, the steppe now called Kazakhstan was home to successive nomadic Turkic powers — early Turkic khaganates, the Kimek and Kipchak tribal confederations, and, following the 13th-century Mongol conquest, the successor khanates of the fragmenting Golden Horde. No single unified state governed the whole territory through most of this span.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from this long span of shifting tribal and khanate power; national flags as a concept postdate it. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
