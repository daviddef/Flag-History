Flags of Saudi Arabia

From the birthplace of Islam in 7th-century Mecca and Medina, through centuries of fragmented emirates and Ottoman suzerainty over parts of the peninsula, the 18th-century religious-political alliance that produced the First Saudi State, its destruction and a Second Saudi State's rise and fall, to Abdulaziz ibn Saud's reconquest and the 1932 unification — one family's green banner, carrying the shahada and later a sword, running through all of it.

The shahada (the Islamic declaration of faith) has marked Saudi banners since the 18th-century Wahhabi movement; the sword beneath it, a later addition marking Ibn Saud's own reconquest, has stayed distinctive and unchanged in spirit even as the kingdom itself was built, lost, and rebuilt twice over.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Saudi Arabia, c. 630–present, seven political entities. No interactive map — this is a flag-only timeline; a region-wide map for the Arabian Peninsula is planned for a future phase. Part of a larger atlas — see the linked pages at the bottom.

1973 – present

Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (standardized)

Kingdom, independent

Who & why: a 1973 royal decree fixes the flag's precise proportions, calligraphy, and sword design in law, formalizing a banner the family had already flown for decades. The kingdom becomes a founding OPEC member, the world's largest oil exporter through the later 20th century, and host to Islam's two holiest cities, Mecca and Medina — a role its rulers style themselves "Custodian of the Two Holy Mosques" to reflect from 1986 onward.

What it represents: the same green field, shahada, and sword carried since the reconquest era, now specified precisely enough that the flag is never lowered to half-mast and never printed on disposable items, out of respect for the religious text it bears.

1932 – 1973

Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (unification)

Kingdom, independent

Who & why: Abdulaziz ibn Saud, having reconquered Riyadh in 1902 and steadily absorbed Nejd, Hejaz, and Asir, proclaims the unified Kingdom of Saudi Arabia on 23 September 1932 — still celebrated as the kingdom's national day. Oil is discovered at Dammam in 1938, transforming a poor desert kingdom into one of the world's most consequential energy producers within a generation.

What it represents: the green shahada-and-sword banner, already flown over Nejd and then Hejaz-Nejd, becomes the flag of the single unified state, though its exact calligraphy and proportions still vary somewhat from print to print until the 1973 decree.

1926 – 1932

Kingdom of Hejaz and Nejd

Kingdom, independent

Who & why: Ibn Saud captures Mecca and Medina from the Hashemite Kingdom of Hejaz in 1924–25, and is proclaimed King of Hejaz in January 1926 while keeping his older title as Sultan of Nejd — the two realms administered under one crown but not yet formally merged into a single kingdom, a transitional arrangement that lasts six years.

What it represents: Nejd's own green shahada banner carries over as the union's flag, a visible sign that this was Ibn Saud's state expanding outward rather than a genuinely new creation.

1902 – 1926

Emirate / Sultanate of Nejd (Al Saud reconquest)

Emirate / Sultanate

Who & why: the young Abdulaziz ibn Saud, exiled in Kuwait since the Rashidis drove his family from Riyadh, retakes the city in a night raid in January 1902, beginning the third and final Al Saud restoration. He formally upgrades his title from Emir to Sultan of Nejd in 1921 as his territory expands across central Arabia.

What it represents: a sword is added beneath the shahada around this reconquest, a visible mark of the campaign that rebuilt the family's rule after a Rashidi-Ottoman interlude — the same combination of text and blade that the modern kingdom's flag still carries.

1818 – 1902

Fragmented Najd: Ottoman-Egyptian occupation, Second Saudi State, Rashidi Ha'il

Fragmented / contested

Who & why: an Ottoman-Egyptian army destroys Diriyah in 1818, ending the First Saudi State. The Al Saud rebuild a smaller Second Saudi State around Riyadh from 1824, but it fractures under a succession dispute and finally falls to the rival Al Rashid emirate of Ha'il in 1891, which dominates Najd until Ibn Saud's 1902 reconquest. One honest bridging entry rather than three invented sub-flags, since no single banner can be attributed with confidence across this fractured, contested 84-year span.

What it represents: the Al Saud's own green shahada banner persists in local use during the Second Saudi State's decades, but no single flag governs this whole span — shown here as a plain placeholder rather than an invented design.

1744 – 1818

First Saudi State (Emirate of Diriyah)

Emirate

Who & why: in 1744 the ruler of the small oasis town of Diriyah, Muhammad ibn Saud, allies with the religious reformer Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab, combining political and religious authority in a pact that founds the Al Saud dynasty's first state. It expands across most of the Arabian interior by the early 1800s, including a 1803 capture of Mecca, before an Ottoman-Egyptian invasion crushes it in 1818.

What it represents: a plain green banner bearing the shahada, tied to the Wahhabi movement's own religious identity, is the earliest form of what becomes the enduring Saudi flag tradition — shown here as an informed reconstruction rather than a documented period specimen.

c. 630 – 1744

Caliphal and emirate-era Arabia (Hejaz and Najd)

Fragmented / caliphal suzerainty

Who & why: the Hejaz, home to Mecca and Medina, is the birthplace of Islam in the early 7th century and the seat of the earliest caliphates before authority shifts to Damascus and then Baghdad; the wider Arabian interior, including Najd, remains a patchwork of local emirates and tribal rule for most of this span, with the Ottomans asserting suzerainty over parts of the peninsula's edges from the 16th century onward without ever governing the deep interior directly.

What it represents: no flag in the modern sense exists across this millennium-long span; various caliphates and local dynasties used banners and seals rather than fixed territorial flags, and none can be attributed to this specific territory with confidence. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
