Flags of the Dominion of Newfoundland

A fully separate British Dominion — the same constitutional status Canada, Australia, and New Zealand held at the time, with its own parliament, currency, and Olympic team — from 1907 until the Great Depression bankrupted it into surrendering self-government altogether in 1934. Newfoundland spent the next fifteen years back under direct British colonial administration before voting, twice, in a bitterly contested pair of 1948 referendums, to join Canada as its 10th province in 1949.

Newfoundland is the only Dominion in this atlas that gave up its self-government voluntarily and never got it back as an independent state — its arc runs from colony, to full Dominion, back down to colony in all but name, and finally into another country entirely.

Scope of this page: the island of Newfoundland and mainland Labrador, 1610–present, four political stages. Newfoundland's 1907 Dominion status was a change in constitutional standing, not a change in flag — like several entries elsewhere in this atlas, the same banner can carry different political meaning across eras. 1949 – present

Province of Newfoundland (and Labrador), Canada

Canadian province

Who & why: two closely fought 1948 referendums — the first inconclusive among three options (independence, Confederation, or continued Commission government), the second a narrower 52–48 vote for joining Canada over restored independent Dominion status — lead to Newfoundland's entry into Confederation on 31 March 1949 as Canada's 10th province, under Premier Joseph Smallwood. The province formally adds "and Labrador" to its name in 2001, and adopts its own distinct provincial flag (a red, gold, blue, and white stylized banner by artist Christopher Pratt) in 1980.

What it represents: Canada's own Red Ensign, flown as the national and provincial flag from 1949 until the 1965 Maple Leaf flag replaced it nationwide; Newfoundland's own distinct provincial flag follows only in 1980.

1934 – 1949

Commission of Government

Suspended self-government

Who & why: crushed by war debt, a collapsed fish trade, and the Great Depression, Newfoundland's own parliament votes in 1933 to dissolve responsible government altogether, formally surrendering it to Britain on 16 February 1934 in exchange for financial rescue. A six-member Commission of Government, three Newfoundlanders and three Britons, appointed rather than elected, rules by decree for the next fifteen years — a Dominion voluntarily stepping back down to colonial status, something no other Dominion in the British Empire ever did.

What it represents: the plain Union Jack, flown as Newfoundland's official flag through the Commission years in place of the earlier Red Ensign, marking the reversion to direct colonial administration.

1907 – 1934

Dominion of Newfoundland

Dominion, self-governing

Who & why: the 1907 Colonial Conference in London upgrades Newfoundland, alongside Canada, Australia, New Zealand, and the Union of South Africa, to full Dominion status — self-governing in every internal matter, with foreign affairs still coordinated through Britain, but constitutionally equal in standing to Canada. As its own Dominion, Newfoundland raises the Newfoundland Regiment for the First World War, suffering catastrophic losses at Beaumont-Hamel on 1 July 1916, an event still marked as Memorial Day in Newfoundland every year.

What it represents: the same Red Ensign flown as a colony continues unaltered as the Dominion flag — 1907 changed Newfoundland's constitutional status, not its banner.

1610 – 1907

Colony of Newfoundland

British colony

Who & why: England establishes its first formal colony on the island at Cupers Cove in 1610, building on nearly a century of seasonal fishing settlement following John Cabot's 1497 landfall; Newfoundland becomes Britain's oldest colony. Responsible (self-)government arrives in 1855, giving the colony its own elected assembly and premier while still formally under the Crown — a stepping stone toward the 1907 Dominion upgrade.

What it represents: the standard British colonial Red Ensign pattern, Union Jack canton with a distinguishing colonial badge in the fly, flown from the mid-to-late 19th century onward as the colony's recognized flag.
