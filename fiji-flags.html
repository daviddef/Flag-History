Flags of Fiji

Fiji's indigenous Melanesian-Polynesian population organized for centuries into competing confederacies and chiefdoms — Bau, Rewa, Cakaudrove among them — with intricate systems of rank and inter-island alliance, but no single unified state. That changes only in 1871, when the paramount chief of Bau is proclaimed king under a Western-style constitution; three years later he cedes the islands to Britain outright. Ninety-six years of crown-colony rule follow, reshaped from 1879 onward by the arrival of Indian indentured laborers, before independence in 1970 restores full sovereignty to a nation whose modern demographics and politics were permanently redrawn by that colonial-era migration.

Four flags carry this lineage: no flag at all for the pre-contact chiefdoms, a real and short-lived royal ensign for the Cakobau kingdom, the British Blue Ensign for the colonial decades, and the light-blue shield flag that has flown, essentially unaltered, since 1970.

Scope of this page: the islands of present-day Fiji, pre-1871 to present, four political entries. No interactive map on this page — see the Pacific hub for the region-wide overview. Part of a larger atlas — see the linked pages at the bottom.

1970 – present

Independent Fiji

Independent state

Who & why: Fiji gains full independence from Britain on 10 October 1970. The new nation's politics and demographics are shaped in large part by a substantial Indo-Fijian population, descended from Indian indentured laborers brought in under British rule between 1879 and the early 1920s, alongside the indigenous iTaukei majority. Since independence, Fiji has experienced several military coups — in 1987, 2000, and 2006 — periods of upheaval that have repeatedly reshaped its constitutional arrangements and civilian governance.

What it represents: a light blue field recalling the surrounding Pacific, the Union Jack in the canton acknowledging the Commonwealth relationship retained after independence, and Fiji's coat of arms — a shield quartered by a red St George's cross, bearing a lion and motifs of sea, sugar cane, and coconut palm — placed on the fly.

1874 – 1970

British Crown Colony of Fiji

Colony

Who & why: paramount chief Ratu Seru Epenisa Cakobau cedes Fiji to Britain in 1874 — the Deed of Cession — ending the brief kingdom he had led. From 1879, Britain begins importing Indian indentured laborers, known as girmitiyas, to work the colony's sugar plantations; the indenture system runs until 1916 and is formally wound down by 1920, but it leaves behind a large, permanent Indo-Fijian community that reshapes the islands' demographics and, later, its politics for generations.

What it represents: the standard British Blue Ensign — the Union Jack in the canton on a dark blue field — carries Fiji's colonial badge, a roundel bearing a local landscape motif, in the fly, marking the islands as a possession administered directly from London.

1871 – 1874

Kingdom of Fiji (Cakobau)

Kingdom

Who & why: Ratu Seru Epenisa Cakobau, paramount chief of Bau, is proclaimed King of Fiji in 1871 under a Western-style constitution — a real, if brief, constitutional monarchy assembled amid competing chiefly rivalries and pressure from growing numbers of foreign settlers, in an attempt at national unification. The arrangement holds for only three years before Cakobau himself cedes the islands to Britain in 1874, judging colonial administration the more stable path forward.

What it represents: the Cakobau government's own flag: a blue field with the Union Jack in canton, acknowledging British influence even before formal cession, and a white dove carrying an olive branch on the fly, a conventional emblem of peace chosen for a fragile new state.

Indigenous Fijian chiefdoms

Indigenous chiefdoms, pre-contact

Who & why: for centuries before European contact, Fiji's indigenous Melanesian-Polynesian population organized into competing confederacies and chiefdoms — among them Bau, Rewa, and Cakaudrove — each built on intricate systems of rank, land custodianship, and shifting inter-island alliance. No single ruler or unified Fijian state existed across this whole span; that only changes when Cakobau is proclaimed king in 1871.

What it represents: no flag, in the European heraldic sense, was part of pre-contact Fijian political culture, and none is invented here to stand in for one. This entry is left honestly blank rather than retrofitted with a symbol that never existed for these chiefdoms.
