Flags of Tuvalu

Nine coral atolls scattered across a thin band of the central Pacific were home to distinct Polynesian seafaring communities for centuries before any European sail appeared — small populations bound by canoe voyaging, oral genealogy, and atoll-by-atoll custom, with no single flag or overarching polity to unite them. Britain's colonial record folds the islands first into a joint administration with the culturally and linguistically distinct Gilbert Islands, then, after decades of shared rule that Polynesian Ellice Islanders never fully welcomed, separates them into their own colony ahead of full independence.

Independence arrives on 1 October 1978 as Tuvalu, a name the islands had already chosen for themselves at separation three years earlier — and the light-blue ensign adopted then, its nine stars set out in the geographic pattern of the nine home islands, is the one still flown today.

Scope of this page: the islands of present-day Tuvalu, pre-1892 to present, four entries. No interactive map on this page — see the Pacific hub for the region-wide overview. Part of a larger atlas — see the linked pages at the bottom.

1978 – present

Independent Tuvalu

Independent state

Who & why: following the 1975 separation from the Gilbert Islands, the Ellice Islands complete the process on 1 October 1978, becoming the fully independent nation of Tuvalu within the Commonwealth. The new state keeps the queen as head of state under a parliamentary system, and joins the United Nations in 2000, one of the smallest sovereign nations on earth by both population and land area.

What it represents: a light blue field carries the Union Jack in the canton, marking continued Commonwealth membership, while nine yellow five-pointed stars scattered across the fly are set out in the actual geographic layout of Tuvalu's nine home islands — a literal map worked into the design itself.

1975 – 1978

Ellice Islands (separate British colony)

Colony

Who & why: a 1974 referendum sees the Polynesian Ellice Islanders vote decisively to separate from the Micronesian Gilbert Islands, their shared colony for decades despite real differences in language and culture. Britain grants the split in 1975, and the new colony takes the name Tuvalu ahead of full independence three years later, its own administration finally distinct from the Gilberts for the first time.

What it represents: the standard British colonial Blue Ensign, Union Jack canton on a navy field, carries the new colony's own badge disc on the fly — a formal marker, however brief, that the Ellice Islands now stood as their own distinct territory rather than an appendage of the Gilbert and Ellice Islands Colony.

1892 – 1975

Ellice Islands within the Gilbert and Ellice Islands colony

Colony (joint administration)

Who & why: Britain declares a protectorate over the Ellice Islands in 1892, alongside the Gilbert Islands to the north, and formally merges the two into the single Gilbert and Ellice Islands Colony in 1916. For over sixty years the Polynesian Ellice Islanders and the Micronesian Gilbertese are administered as one colony despite distinct populations, languages, and cultures — a joint arrangement that sows the resentment that eventually drives the 1974 separation referendum.

What it represents: this is the same joint colony's flag flown over the Gilbert Islands' own page for this period — one shared British Blue Ensign and badge disc covering both island groups, since no separate Ellice Islands administration or flag existed until the colony split in 1975.

pre-1892

Indigenous Polynesian Tuvaluan communities

Indigenous nations, pre-contact

Who & why: for centuries before British contact, the nine atolls of what is now Tuvalu were home to distinct Polynesian communities bound by seafaring tradition, oral genealogy, and atoll-by-atoll custom rather than any single overarching authority. Each atoll maintained its own chiefly structures and identity, connected by canoe voyaging across the surrounding ocean but never unified under one government or one banner.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from this era, and none is invented to stand in for one; flags as a formal heraldic device were never part of pre-contact Polynesian atoll culture in the Ellice Islands. Shown here honestly blank rather than retrofitted with a symbol.
