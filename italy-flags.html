Flags of Italy

From an Alpine county straddling the trade routes between France and Italy, through a red-cross duchy and a blue-cross kingdom that spent two quiet centuries building the army and administration that would one day unify the peninsula, a French Revolutionary interruption, sixty years as a liberal then fascist monarchy, a wartime split between a German puppet state in the north and the Allied-recognized crown in the south, to the plain tricolor republic of today — eleven flags tracing one dynasty's slow climb from a single Alpine county to the state that made Italy.

The tricolor itself is younger than the dynasty that eventually carried it: green, white, and red first flew together as the flag of the short-lived Cispadane Republic in 1797, a French Revolutionary client state in the Po valley — more than fifty years before the House of Savoy adopted the design as its own in 1848. The same three colors, in the same order, still fly over Italy today.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Italy, c. 1032–present, eleven political entities. A note on the map: Italy's official boundary data is a MultiPolygon — the mainland "boot" plus Sicily and Sardinia as separate landmasses. For the traced-border outline and the interactive split map below, this page uses the mainland polygon only; Sicily and Sardinia are real Italian territory throughout this history but are simplified out of the map graphics entirely. Both islands carried their own real, separate crowns for centuries worth remembering even though neither is drawn — the Kingdom of Sicily (from 1130) and the Kingdom of Sardinia (the same crown that, after 1720, eventually unified the mainland). A note on the pre-1861 timeline: rather than try to track every Italian state of the era at once, this page follows one specific lineage backward from unification — the House of Savoy's own Piedmont/Sardinia throughline, the state that actually did the unifying — and covers the other major Italian states of the era (the Bourbon Kingdom of the Two Sicilies, the Papal States, Austrian-ruled Lombardy-Venetia, the Grand Duchy of Tuscany, and others) in prose rather than as separate cards. Part of a larger atlas — see the map and linked pages at the bottom.

Italy Splits in Two, 1943–1945

Italy's real wartime partition: the German-backed puppet Italian Social Republic (RSI) in the north, nominally led by a German-rescued Mussolini governing from Salò on Lake Garda, against the Allied-recognized Kingdom of Italy in the south under Marshal Badoglio's government and King Victor Emmanuel III. Press play, or drag the slider.

Both sides flew a version of the same flag. There was no rival national banner — both governments flew the green-white-red tricolor. The Kingdom's version kept the royal Savoy shield; the RSI's dropped it, rejecting the monarchy it blamed for the 1943 "betrayal." The line below runs roughly along the front as it stood through the winter of 1943–44, well south of Rome — it solidifies only for the war years it was real, and dissolves for good with the German surrender in Italy on 29 April/2 May 1945.

1946 – present

Italian Republic

RepublicAlcide De GasperiSecular (special status for the Church)

Who & why: a 2 June 1946 institutional referendum abolishes the monarchy (54% to 46%) just weeks after Umberto II — the "May King" — took the throne from his abdicating father, Victor Emmanuel III. A new Constitution takes effect 1 January 1948. Italy becomes a founding member of NATO in 1949 and the European Coal and Steel Community in 1951, the seed of what becomes the EU.

What it represents: the plain tricolor, with no coat of arms of any kind — the republic breaks with the House of Savoy by removing its shield entirely rather than merely stripping the crown, a cleaner break than several neighboring monarchies-turned-republics managed.

1945 – 1946

Kingdom of Italy (reunified)

KingdomVictor Emmanuel IIIRoman Catholic

Who & why: German forces in Italy surrender on 29 April 1945, effective 2 May, reunifying the peninsula under a single government for the first time since September 1943. Victor Emmanuel III, tainted by two decades of collaboration with Mussolini, had already transferred his powers to his son as Lieutenant General of the Realm in June 1944, and formally abdicates on 9 May 1946 — weeks before the referendum ends the monarchy for good.

What it represents: the tricolor with the Savoy shield, but without its royal crown — stripped by a June 1944 decree as a concession to anti-monarchist opinion even before the war's end, the flag itself already signaling that the crown's days were numbered.

1943 – 1945

Italian Social Republic (RSI)

Fascist / puppet stateBenito MussoliniNominally Catholic (no genuine establishment)

Who & why: German commandos free Mussolini from his mountaintop prison on 12 September 1943; days later he proclaims the Italian Social Republic under German protection, nominally governing from Salò on Lake Garda while real authority rests with the occupying Wehrmacht and SS. The RSI conducts a brutal roundup of Jews and partisans, and its own paramilitary Black Brigades fight a vicious civil war against the Italian Resistance. It collapses with the German surrender in Italy on 29 April 1945; Mussolini is captured and executed by partisans the same day.

What it represents: the same tricolor, but stripped of the Savoy shield entirely — RSI propaganda framed the plain flag as a return to the "people's" tricolor of the Risorgimento, rejecting the monarchy it blamed for the September 1943 "betrayal," even while functioning as Berlin's client. Its war ensign, shown here, added a black eagle grasping a fasces.

1922 – 1943

Kingdom of Italy (Fascist era)

KingdomBenito MussoliniRoman Catholic

Who & why: Mussolini's Blackshirts stage the March on Rome in October 1922; King Victor Emmanuel III, declining to declare martial law, invites him to form a government, and the Fascists dismantle parliamentary democracy over the following years. Italy invades Ethiopia (1935–36) and joins Hitler's war in June 1940; military disasters and the July 1943 Allied invasion of Sicily bring the regime down when the Fascist Grand Council itself votes no confidence in Mussolini on 25 July 1943, and the King has him arrested the same day. Marshal Badoglio's caretaker government signs a secret armistice with the Allies on 3 September, announced 8 September — the trigger for the German seizure of Rome and the north that produces the RSI split two weeks later.

What it represents: the same royal tricolor and crowned Savoy shield, now ringed by a fascist fasces added by law in 1926 — a state emblem literally wrapping the monarchy's own arms in the party's symbol.

1861 – 1922

Kingdom of Italy (Liberal era)

KingdomVictor Emmanuel IIRoman Catholic

Who & why: the Kingdom of Italy is proclaimed on 17 March 1861 by the first unified parliament, with Victor Emmanuel II of Savoy — already King of Sardinia — simply keeping his own royal number as king of the new state. Venice is added in 1866; Rome, the last redoubt of the Papal States, is stormed and annexed in 1870, completing unification of the peninsula against the pope's own objections. The Bourbon Kingdom of the Two Sicilies, Austrian-ruled Lombardy-Venetia, and the Grand Duchy of Tuscany are among the states absorbed along the way. The next half-century adds Libya (1911) and, after the First World War fought as an Allied co-belligerent from 1915, Trentino, South Tyrol, Trieste, and Istria (1918–20).

What it represents: the tricolor with the crowned Savoy shield, carried directly over from the pre-unification Sardinian flag onto the whole peninsula — the state that did the unifying simply extended its own flag over the country it created, rather than adopting a wholly new symbol.

1848 – 1861

Kingdom of Sardinia (constitutional)

KingdomCharles Albert of SardiniaRoman Catholic

Who & why: King Charles Albert grants the Statuto Albertino, a constitution, in March 1848 amid the wave of revolutions sweeping Europe, and declares war on Austria days later in the First Italian War of Independence — a defeat that costs him his throne; he abdicates in 1849 in favor of Victor Emmanuel II. Under Victor Emmanuel II and his shrewd prime minister Cavour, the kingdom spends the 1850s building the alliances (the Crimean War, the 1858 Plombières pact with Napoleon III) and fighting the wars (the 1859 Second Italian War of Independence) that make it the nucleus around which the rest of Italy unifies by 1861.

What it represents: the new tricolor, adopted as the constitutional kingdom's own flag with the House of Savoy's crowned red-and-white shield at its center — a design fixed by the Statuto's flag decree just as the movement toward unification began, later simply expanding to cover the whole country unchanged.

1814 – 1848

Kingdom of Sardinia (restored)

KingdomVictor Emmanuel IRoman Catholic

Who & why: Victor Emmanuel I returns to Turin in May 1814 as Napoleon's empire collapses; the Congress of Vienna the following year confirms the restoration and enlarges the kingdom by adding the former Republic of Genoa, meant as a stronger buffer state against France. The restoration is conservative, rolling back Napoleonic-era legal reforms; Victor Emmanuel I abdicates in 1821 amid a liberal military uprising, passing the throne to his brother Charles Felix and then, in 1831, to a cousin, Charles Albert.

What it represents: the historic Savoy state flag — a blue field with a white cross — restored unchanged, the same design flown before the French interruption, asserting dynastic continuity as if the interruption had never happened.

1798 – 1814

Piedmont annexed to France

Occupied / annexedNapoleon BonaparteCatholic (majority faith, not exclusively established)

Who & why: French Revolutionary forces occupy Turin in 1798, forcing King Charles Emmanuel IV to cede his mainland territories and withdraw to the island of Sardinia, the one part of his kingdom the French never occupy; his brother and successor Victor Emmanuel I rules in exile from Cagliari for over a decade. Piedmont itself is formally annexed to France outright in 1802, organized as several French départements, and administered as such through Napoleon's rise and fall until his final defeat in 1814.

What it represents: the mainland shown on this page's map — Piedmont — flew the French Republican, then Imperial, tricolor throughout this period as annexed French territory. The House of Savoy's own blue-cross flag survived only on Sardinia itself, over a rump kingdom a fraction the size of the one lost.

1720 – 1798

Kingdom of Sardinia-Piedmont

KingdomVictor Amadeus IIRoman Catholic

Who & why: the House of Savoy trades the Kingdom of Sicily — awarded by the 1713 Treaty of Utrecht — for the poorer but more defensible island of Sardinia in 1720, taking the royal title "King of Sardinia" while governing the whole composite state from Turin in Piedmont, its real center of power. The 18th century is a period of steady, unglamorous state-building — army and administrative reform, a scientific and cultural flowering in Turin — that leaves Piedmont one of the best-organized states in Italy by the time revolutionary France arrives at its border.

What it represents: the same blue Savoy-cross flag flown by the preceding duchy, now representing a full kingdom — a promotion in rank that changed the ruler's title without changing the flag.

1416 – 1720

Duchy of Savoy

DuchyAmadeus VIII of SavoyRoman Catholic

Who & why: Holy Roman Emperor Sigismund elevates Count Amadeus VIII to Duke of Savoy in 1416, formal recognition of a dynasty that had already spent nearly four centuries expanding its base in the Alpine passes between France and Italy. The capital shifts decisively to Turin, on the Italian side of the Alps, under Emanuele Filiberto in the 1560s, beginning the state's long reorientation from a French-Alpine power into an Italian one — the same reorientation that, three centuries later, lets it lead Italian unification.

What it represents: the red field and white cross of the historic Savoy arms — "gules, a cross argent" — attested in the family's heraldry since at least the 14th century, among the oldest continuously-used dynastic symbols in this entire atlas.

c. 1032 – 1416

County of Savoy

CountyHumbert I “the Whitehanded”Roman Catholic

Who & why: Count Humbert I "Whitehands," traditionally regarded as the dynasty's founder, receives the core of the county around 1032 from the Holy Roman Emperor Conrad II, planting the House of Savoy astride the Alpine trade and pilgrim routes between France, Switzerland, and Italy. Nearly four centuries of steady territorial accumulation follow — through marriage, purchase, and the occasional war — before Amadeus VIII's elevation to duke in 1416 (see the entry above) formalizes what had become one of Europe's more substantial princely states.

What it represents: no securely documented county-wide banner survives from this long early period; the white-cross-on-red arms that define every later Savoy flag on this page begin appearing on individual seals and shields by around the 13th–14th century, but not as a fixed county state flag confirmed this far back. Shown here honestly blank rather than projecting the later cross backward past where it's actually attested.
