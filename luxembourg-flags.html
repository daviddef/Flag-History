Flags of Luxembourg

Europe's last Grand Duchy started as a single riverside fortress bought and traded between Frankish lords, spent three and a half centuries as one province among many inside the same Burgundian-then-Habsburg bloc that also ruled Belgium, lost more than half its territory and population to a treaty in 1839, and finally became a fully sovereign country in 1890 — not by war or revolution, but because a Dutch princess couldn't legally inherit a title tied to a five-century-old family succession pact.

The modern tricolor's red, white, and light blue trace back to the Grand Duchy's own coat of arms, worn by the House of Nassau — a deliberately different shade of blue from the Dutch flag it otherwise resembles, adopted informally after 1830 specifically so the two could be told apart.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Luxembourg, c. 963–present, ten political entities. Part of a larger atlas — see the map and linked pages at the bottom.

1944/45 – present

Grand Duchy of Luxembourg (restored)

Sovereign grand duchy

Who & why: Luxembourg City is liberated by US troops on 10 September 1944, though the Battle of the Bulge pushes back into the country's northern Ardennes corner that December, and full liberation isn't complete until January–February 1945. Grand Duchess Charlotte, who spent the occupation broadcasting to her country from exile in London and Montreal, returns in April 1945. Luxembourg abandons its 19th-century neutrality for good, becoming a founding member of NATO in 1949 and, from the 1952 European Coal and Steel Community onward, one of the six founding states of what becomes the European Union — the country's capital now also hosting major EU institutions.

What it represents: the same red-white-light-blue tricolor resumes without alteration — unlike Hungary or Poland nearby in this atlas, no emblem is added or removed after the war. The flag is given its first full legal description in 1848 and its precise 1:1:2 proportions and exact shade of blue fixed only in 1972, formalizing a design already a century old by then.

1940 – 1944/45

German occupation (Gau Moselland)

Occupied / annexed territory

Who & why: German forces invade on 10 May 1940, the same day as the Netherlands and Belgium, overrunning a country with almost no army to resist; Grand Duchess Charlotte and her government flee into exile. Gauleiter Gustav Simon administers Luxembourg first as a civil administration, then formally annexes it into the Nazi Gau Moselland in August 1942, banning the French language and Luxembourgish civic symbols and conscripting young Luxembourgish men directly into the German army. A general strike against that forced conscription breaks out at the end of August 1942 — brutally suppressed, but among the most significant civilian acts of resistance anywhere in occupied Western Europe.

What it represents: the national tricolor and coat of arms are suppressed outright; the swastika flag of the Reich flies in their place, over a country the Nazi administration treated not as occupied territory but as reclaimed German land. Shown here in a stylized, non-literal form rather than reproducing the historical symbol exactly.

1890 – 1940

Grand Duchy of Luxembourg (independent)

Sovereign grand duchy

Who & why: King William III of the Netherlands dies on 23 November 1890 without a surviving son. Dutch succession law lets his ten-year-old daughter Wilhelmina become queen, but the centuries-old Nassau Family Pact governing Luxembourg required a male heir in the direct line — a difference rooted in Salic-law-style succession rules that the Dutch crown itself had already abandoned. The two crowns split for good: Adolphe of Nassau-Weilburg, head of a cadet branch, becomes Grand Duke, followed by Guillaume IV (1905), then briefly Grand Duchess Marie-Adélaïde, who abdicates in January 1919 over perceived wartime collaboration with the occupying Germans, and finally her sister Charlotte, whose 45-year reign (1919–64) spans the Second World War.

What it represents: the same tricolor, but now flying over the first fully independent Luxembourgish state since 1443 — no foreign monarch shares the crown, no personal union links it to a larger kingdom, for the first time in nearly 450 years.

1839 – 1890

Grand Duchy of Luxembourg (personal union, reduced)

Personal union

Who & why: the 1839 Treaty of London finally settles the borders left unresolved by the 1830 Belgian Revolution: Luxembourg's western, French-speaking half — more than half its territory and population, including Arlon — is ceded outright to Belgium as the new Belgian province of Luxembourg. The smaller, German-speaking remainder stays a Grand Duchy in personal union with the Dutch crown, still legally separate from the Kingdom of the Netherlands itself. It also remains a member of the German Confederation until that body dissolves in 1866, and joins the German customs union (Zollverein) in 1842; the 1867 Treaty of London, resolving a war scare between France and Prussia over the country, declares Luxembourg permanently neutral and has the Prussian garrison withdraw from its fortress.

What it represents: the same red-white-light-blue tricolor, its colors drawn from the ducal coat of arms and increasingly used informally through the 1830s and 1840s to visually distinguish Luxembourg from the similar-looking Belgian and Dutch flags on either side of it.

1815 – 1839

Grand Duchy of Luxembourg (personal union, undivided)

Personal union

Who & why: the 1815 Congress of Vienna creates the Grand Duchy of Luxembourg as compensation for William I of the Netherlands, who had just lost his family's ancestral Nassau territories in Germany to Prussia. William rules Luxembourg in personal union — not as part of his Kingdom of the Netherlands, but as his own separate possession — while the Grand Duchy is simultaneously enrolled in the German Confederation, its fortress garrisoned by Prussian troops. When the 1830 Belgian Revolution breaks out, most of Luxembourg (outside the fortress city itself, which the Prussian garrison holds for William) sides with the rebels and is administered as part of Belgium in practice for nine years, until the 1839 treaty formally divides it.

What it represents: the earliest period in which Luxembourg's own tricolor is believed to emerge, drawn from the red lion and blue-and-white bars of the ducal coat of arms, though its use is not yet consistent or official this early — shown here as the same design that solidifies over the following decades, with that caveat.

1795 – 1815

French Republic / Empire annexation

Annexed territory

Who & why: Revolutionary France besieges Luxembourg's fortress — then among the strongest in Europe, nicknamed the "Gibraltar of the North" — for seven months before it surrenders in June 1795, part of the same wider French sweep across the Low Countries covered on the region's hub page. Luxembourg is annexed outright and reorganized as the Département des Forêts, its own institutions dissolved entirely rather than merely placed under a new overlord as in previous centuries. French conscription and anti-clerical policy provoke the Klëppelkrich ("Cudgel War") peasant uprising in 1798, crushed within weeks.

What it represents: the French tricolor, flown here for the first time not as one power replacing another as feudal overlord, but as direct incorporation into the French state itself — no separate Luxembourgish administration survives this period at all.

1714 – 1795

Austrian Netherlands (Duchy of Luxembourg)

Netherlands overlordship

Who & why: the direct Spanish Habsburg line dies out in 1700, touching off the War of the Spanish Succession; the 1713–14 Treaties of Utrecht and Rastatt split the inheritance, sending the Spanish Netherlands — Belgium and Luxembourg together — to the Austrian branch of the Habsburgs under Emperor Charles VI rather than to the new Bourbon king in Madrid. Luxembourg's fortress continues to be rebuilt and strengthened through this period, cementing its reputation as one of the most heavily fortified places in Europe. The same overlordship changes tracked on Belgium's own page apply here in lockstep — the two duchies share the identical sequence of rulers throughout this entire 350-year span.

What it represents: the Habsburg dynasty's own double-headed eagle on gold, distinct from the Burgundian cross flown by the same family's earlier Spanish-era administration — the ruling house is unchanged, but the crown worn is now Vienna's rather than Madrid's.

1581 – 1714

Spanish Netherlands (Duchy of Luxembourg)

Netherlands overlordship

Who & why: when the northern provinces renounce Philip II's rule in the 1581 Act of Abjuration, Luxembourg — always more remote from the revolt's center in Holland and Flanders — never leaves Habsburg hands at all. It remains part of the Spanish Netherlands, governed from Brussels on Madrid's behalf, through the Eighty Years' War and its confirmation at the 1648 Peace of Westphalia. The fortress changes hands briefly to France under Louis XIV (1684–97) before reverting to Spain by treaty, foreshadowing the far more permanent French annexation still a century away.

What it represents: the same red saltire-and-firesteel Cross of Burgundy flown across the Spanish Netherlands as a whole — the flag doesn't change at 1581 the way the Dutch Republic's does, because Luxembourg's own overlordship doesn't change either.

1443 – 1581

Burgundian / Habsburg Netherlands (Duchy of Luxembourg)

Netherlands overlordship

Who & why: Philip the Good, Duke of Burgundy, buys Luxembourg from the impoverished Duchess Elisabeth of Görlitz in 1443, fully securing it after her death in 1451 — the last major piece of the Burgundian Netherlands he spent three decades assembling. The Burgundian line dies out with Mary of Burgundy in 1482, passing the whole bloc by marriage to the Habsburgs; her grandson Charles V, born in Ghent in 1500, later declares the Seventeen Provinces, Luxembourg among them, one indivisible inheritance under the 1549 Pragmatic Sanction. Luxembourg's own institutions — its Estates, its laws, its own coinage — continue throughout, governed as one duchy among many rather than absorbed into a single state.

What it represents: the Cross of Burgundy — a red saltire on white, associated firesteel sparks recalling the Order of the Golden Fleece — the shared banner of the whole Burgundian and later Habsburg Netherlands, the same flag covered on Belgium's and the Low Countries hub's own pages.

963 – 1443

County / Duchy of Luxembourg (independent)

Independent county / duchy

Who & why: Siegfried I, Count of Ardennes, acquires the rocky outcrop known as the "Lucilinburhuc" ("little fortress") from the Abbey of St. Maximin in Trier in 963, traditionally reckoned the founding of Luxembourg; a settlement grows around the fortress he builds there. His descendants expand the County of Luxembourg over the following centuries, reaching a high point under Henry VII, who becomes Holy Roman Emperor in 1312, and his grandson Charles IV, who as emperor elevates the county to a Duchy in 1354 for his own half-brother, Wenceslaus I. The Duchy passes through several hands, including a stretch under Bohemian and then Habsburg-adjacent control, before its final owner, Duchess Elisabeth of Görlitz, sells it on to Burgundy in 1443.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from either the pre-heraldic County or the early Duchy period; medieval Luxembourgish counts and dukes used personal and dynastic seals rather than a fixed territorial banner. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
