Flags of the Belarusian Lands

From the semi-independent princes of Polotsk, through five centuries as the demographic and linguistic heartland of the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, a partition-era absorption into the Russian Empire, a chaotic five years of civil war and foreign occupation, seven decades as a Soviet republic, and a republic of its own since 1991 — a white-red-white tricolor that keeps getting displaced by a red-and-green design modeled on the Soviet era.

Unlike its neighbors Ukraine and Lithuania, Belarus never had a durable internal east–west or north–south split of its own: from Polotsk onward it was consistently governed as one piece, whoever held it. This page traces who held all of it, era by era.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Belarus, c. 862–present, eight political entities. Part of a larger atlas — see the map and linked pages at the bottom.

1995 – present

Republic of Belarus (current flag)

Sovereign state

Who & why: a contested May 1995 referendum, held under President Alexander Lukashenko — in office continuously since his election in July 1994, making him one of the longest-serving heads of state anywhere in the world — replaces the 1991 white-red-white tricolor with a red-and-green design closely modeled on the Soviet-era Byelorussian SSR flag, minus the hammer and sickle. A minor 2012 update simplified the hoist-side ornament pattern.

What it represents: red for the historic banner and for wartime sacrifice, green for the country's forests and fields; the white-on-red ornament pattern along the hoist is adapted from a traditional folk textile design, one of the few elements the government kept from pre-Soviet symbolism.

1991 – 1995

Republic of Belarus (first flag)

Sovereign state

Who & why: Belarus declares independence on 25 August 1991 as the USSR collapses, and adopts the white-red-white tricolor first used by the short-lived Belarusian People's Republic in 1918 — a deliberate act of continuity with that earlier, thwarted attempt at statehood. The 1995 referendum ends this flag's official use after less than four years.

What it represents: the same design flown in 1918 and again by wartime and post-war Belarusian emigre and resistance groups who never accepted Soviet rule — adopted in 1991 as a symbol explicitly disconnected from the communist era, which is precisely why it was replaced in 1995.

1922 – 1991

Byelorussian Soviet Socialist Republic

Communist state

Who & why: the BSSR is one of the four founding republics of the USSR on 30 December 1922, its flag simplifying over the decades from a plain red banner with gold lettering to the 1951 design shown here, which added the green stripe and hoist-side ornament that the modern flag still echoes. World War II devastates the republic more than almost any other Soviet territory: Nazi occupation from 1941–44 and the brutal partisan war fought against it cost Belarus an estimated quarter of its entire pre-war population, one of the highest per-capita losses of any country in the war.

What it represents: the standard Soviet republican formula — red field, gold hammer, sickle and star — personalized after 1951 with a green stripe (the republic's forests) and a white-on-red ornament pattern directly inherited, ironically, from the same folk-textile tradition the anti-Soviet 1918 tricolor also drew from.

1919 – 1922

Byelorussian SSR amid the Polish-Soviet War

Contested / civil war

Who & why: the Bolsheviks proclaim the Byelorussian Soviet Socialist Republic on 1 January 1919 in Minsk, but the republic's borders exist mostly on paper for the next three years: the wider Polish-Soviet War (1919–21) is fought substantially across Belarusian territory, with Minsk itself changing hands twice. The March 1921 Treaty of Riga ends the war by splitting Belarusian lands outright — the western half becomes part of the Second Polish Republic (administered as the "Kresy Wschodnie"), while the eastern half remains this reduced Soviet republic, its territory and population sharply diminished from what the Belarusian People's Republic had claimed only three years earlier.

What it represents: a plain red field with gold lettering or a simple hammer-and-sickle emblem — the generic early-Soviet republican banner, adopted before any distinctly Belarusian ornamentation was added; shown here with a broken border to mark a republic whose actual territory was still being fought over and cut in half even as the flag flew.

1918 – 1919

Belarusian People's Republic

Contested / civil war

Who & why: as the Russian Empire collapses, a Belarusian national council declares the Belarusian People's Republic (BNR) in Minsk on 25 March 1918, under German occupation following the Eastern Front's collapse. It is the first modern attempt at a Belarusian state — but with no army of its own, no international recognition, and German forces (soon replaced by advancing Bolsheviks) actually controlling the ground, it never exercises effective governance over the territory it claims. Minsk falls to the Red Army by the end of the year, and the BNR survives afterward only as a government-in-exile, a status it technically retains to this day.

What it represents: the white-red-white tricolor, based on the historic heraldic colors of the medieval Grand Duchy of Lithuania's Belarusian lands, adopted here as a national flag for the first time — the same design independent Belarus would revive in 1991, and that opposition and diaspora movements continue to fly today.

1795 – 1918

Russian Empire rule

Imperial territory

Who & why: the 1795 Third Partition of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth delivers the whole of Belarus, along with most of Ukraine, into the Russian Empire outright — see the Eastern Europe hub map for the wider partition story. Imperial policy actively discourages a distinct Belarusian identity, administering the territory simply as the "Northwest Region" (Severo-Zapadny Krai) and, after the failed 1863 uprising led locally by Kastuś Kalinoŭski, banning Belarusian-language printing outright for decades.

What it represents: the standard Russian imperial civil tricolor, flown across the empire with no distinct regional or Belarusian variant — itself a reflection of a governing policy that treated the territory as generic Russian land rather than a place with its own separate character.

1392 – 1795

Grand Duchy of Lithuania (Belarusian lands)

Grand duchy

Who & why: Lithuanian expansion into the former Rus' principalities is gradual through the 14th century — Gediminas's forces take Polotsk around 1307 — but by 1392, under Vytautas the Great, the Grand Duchy of Lithuania rules essentially all of modern Belarus as its demographic and cultural core, far more populous and economically developed than ethnic Lithuania itself. The 1569 Union of Lublin merges Lithuania with the Kingdom of Poland into a single Commonwealth, but keeps Belarus specifically under the Grand Duchy's own administration (unlike Ukraine's lands, transferred directly to the Polish Crown that same year) all the way through the Commonwealth's 1795 dissolution.

What it represents: the Pahonia (Belarusian) or Vytis (Lithuanian) — a white armored knight on a rearing horse on a red field — the Grand Duchy's own state emblem for four centuries. Notably, the Grand Duchy's official chancery language for most of this period was a form of Old Belarusian (Ruthenian), not Lithuanian, reflecting how central these lands were to the state's own administration.

c. 862 – 1392

Principality of Polotsk and the early East Slavic lands

Principality

Who & why: Polotsk, first mentioned in the chronicles in 862, is already one of the oldest and most self-directed power centers of the wider Kievan Rus' world by the 10th century, often ruled by its own line of princes even while nominally part of the same Rurikid realm as Kyiv — see the Eastern Europe hub map for that shared origin. The 1240 Mongol destruction of Kiev largely bypasses Polotsk and the other forested Belarusian-lands principalities, which instead drift gradually toward the newly consolidating Grand Duchy of Lithuania, founded around 1236, over the following century and a half.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from this pre-heraldic era; medieval Rus' principalities used princely seals and banners with religious imagery rather than a fixed territorial flag in the later heraldic sense. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
