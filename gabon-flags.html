Flags of Gabon

Gabon's capital, Libreville ("free town"), takes its name from its founding in 1849 by a group of Africans freed from an intercepted slave ship and resettled there under French naval protection — a genuinely distinctive origin story among the region's colonial capitals. From Myene coastal trading polities through French Equatorial Africa to a 1960 independence and more than half a century of Bongo-family rule underwritten by oil wealth, this page traces three flags across roughly a century and a half.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Gabon, from its pre-colonial coastal polities through French colonization to the modern republic. No interactive map on this page — deferred to a future phase of the atlas. Part of a larger atlas — see the linked pages at the bottom.

1960 – present

Gabonese Republic (independent)

Republic, independentLéon M'baSecular (no state religion)

Who & why: Gabon becomes independent on 17 August 1960 under Léon M'ba, its first president. Omar Bongo succeeds him in 1967 and governs for 42 years until his death in 2009; his son Ali Bongo then rules until a military coup ends the family's 56-year hold on power in August 2023. Gabon's substantial offshore oil reserves have made it one of the wealthier states per capita in the region even as that wealth has concentrated heavily around the ruling circle.

What it represents: the same green-yellow-blue horizontal tricolor adopted at independence, with the central French-tricolor canton of the pre-independence autonomous-republic flag dropped: green for the country's dense equatorial forest, yellow for the equator (which runs directly through Gabon), and blue for the Atlantic coastline.

1959 – 1960

Gabon (autonomous republic within the French Community)

Autonomous republicLéon M'baRoman Catholic (favored, not established)

Who & why: Gabon votes to join the French Community as a self-governing republic in the September 1958 referendum, gaining internal autonomy while France retains control of defense and foreign affairs; this brief transitional status runs until full independence less than two years later, in August 1960.

What it represents: the new green-yellow-blue tricolor design carries a French Tricolor canton in the upper hoist, marking Gabon's continued constitutional tie to France during this transitional period, dropped once independence is achieved.

1839 – 1959

French Gabon

ColonyLouis Édouard Bouët-WillaumezRoman Catholic (favored, not established)

Who & why: France signs its first treaties with Mpongwe (Myene) King Denis on the Gabon estuary in 1839, and in 1849 a French naval vessel resettles a group of Africans freed from an intercepted slave ship at a new outpost named Libreville. Gabon becomes part of French Congo, then a separate colony within French Equatorial Africa from 1910, governed from the federal capital at Brazzaville throughout.

What it represents: the French Tricolor is flown as the colonial ensign throughout; no distinct territorial flag existed for Gabon under direct French colonial rule.

pre-1839

Myene coastal polities and interior peoples

Pre-colonial politiesNo single ruler documentedTraditional/ancestral religion

Who & why: before French contact, the Gabon estuary and coast are home to Myene-speaking peoples (including the Mpongwe, whose King Denis signs the first treaties with France), with Fang and other peoples populating the dense interior forest. No single unifying kingdom governs the territory that becomes Gabon.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from this pre-colonial era for any of the region's polities; shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
