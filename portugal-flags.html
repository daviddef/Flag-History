Flags of Portugal

One of Europe's oldest unchanged borders, held almost continuously by one line of rulers — interrupted exactly once, for sixty years, when a Spanish king inherited the throne and Portugal simply stopped being its own kingdom in law, if never quite in spirit. Seven flags span a Burgundian vassal county, the Age of Discovery, the Iberian Union, and two very different twentieth-century republics bookending forty-eight years of dictatorship.

Portugal's own green-and-red flag with its armillary sphere is younger than it looks — adopted only in 1911, after the monarchy fell — but its design has not changed since: the same flag flew over the First Republic, the Estado Novo dictatorship, and today's democracy alike.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day mainland Portugal, c. 1096–present, seven political entities. Offshore Azores and Madeira are omitted from the traced map, following this atlas's practice for island territories. Part of a larger atlas — see the map and linked pages at the bottom.

Portugal through seven eras, on its own real border

Portugal doesn't have a clean internal governance split the way Spain's Reconquista frontier or Castile/Granada does — there is no honest internal line to draw within Portugal itself for most of its history. So this map does the honest thing instead: the whole country, on its own real traced coastline, changing color as a single unit each time the government running it changes — including the one truly dramatic moment, 1580, when Portugal's color has to match a foreign crown's, because for sixty years it effectively did.

No internal border is invented here. Unlike the Hungary or Spain pages in this atlas, Portugal's own map never splits into two simultaneously-governed pieces — even the Iberian Union wasn't a partition of Portugal, it was the whole country changing hands at once while Madrid ruled it through its own separate Portuguese institutions. 1974 – present

Portuguese Republic (Third Republic)

Republic

Who & why: the Carnation Revolution of 25 April 1974 — a nearly bloodless military coup that becomes a genuine popular uprising, named for the flowers protesters placed in soldiers' rifle barrels — ends the Estado Novo in a single day. A turbulent transitional period follows, settled by the 1976 constitution, which establishes the democratic, parliamentary republic that governs Portugal today. Portugal joins the European Community in 1986.

What it represents: the exact same flag adopted in 1911 — green for hope, red for the blood of those who died for the nation, the armillary sphere recalling the Age of Discovery, the shield carrying the old quinas and castles. No design change was needed to mark the return to democracy; the republic simply reclaimed a flag the dictatorship had never altered either.

1926 – 1974

Military Dictatorship & Estado Novo

Authoritarian dictatorship

Who & why: a military coup on 28 May 1926 topples the chaotic, coup-prone First Republic, opening a "National Dictatorship" (Ditadura Nacional) that governs without a clear ideology for its first years. António de Oliveira Salazar, brought in as finance minister in 1928 for his ability to balance the budget, steadily accumulates power and becomes prime minister in 1932; his 1933 constitution formally establishes the corporatist, Catholic, censorship-heavy Estado Novo ("New State"). Salazar rules until incapacitated by a stroke in 1968, succeeded by Marcelo Caetano, whose own more cautious version of the regime lasts until 1974.

What it represents: unlike Spain's Franco or many other 20th-century regimes, the Estado Novo never replaced the national flag — it kept the 1911 republican design exactly as adopted, coopting rather than discarding the symbol of the republic it had just overthrown.

1910 – 1926

First Portuguese Republic

Republic

Who & why: a republican revolution on 5 October 1910 overthrows King Manuel II after just two years on the throne, ending 770 years of monarchy. The republic that follows is chronically unstable — forty-five different governments in sixteen years — caught between radical anticlericalism, economic crisis, and Portugal's costly participation in the First World War, ending in the 1926 coup.

What it represents: the green-and-red flag, adopted 30 June 1911, deliberately broke from the monarchy's blue-and-white in both color and symbolism — green for hope and red for the blood of those who fell in the republican cause, with the armillary sphere (a navigational instrument tied to Portugal's Age of Discovery) replacing the royal crown.

1640 – 1910

Kingdom of Portugal (Braganza, restored)

Kingdom

Who & why: after the 1 December 1640 palace coup in Lisbon ousts the Spanish administration, John, Duke of Braganza, is proclaimed King John IV, founding the House of Braganza; Spain doesn't formally recognize independence until the 1668 Treaty of Lisbon. The dynasty presides over Brazil's gold boom, the 1755 Lisbon earthquake, Napoleon's invasions and the royal court's flight to Rio de Janeiro (1807–1821), and, after Brazilian independence in 1822, a long and often turbulent transition toward constitutional monarchy — the Liberal Wars of 1828–1834 finally settle it in favor of a constitutional, rather than absolute, crown. The dynasty ends abruptly with King Carlos I's assassination in 1908 and his son Manuel II's overthrow in 1910.

What it represents: blue and white, adopted in this final form in 1830 under the constitutional monarchy, with the royal shield — the same quinas-and-castles arms carried since the Middle Ages — now topped with a royal crown to mark the restored, sovereign dynasty.

1580 – 1640

Iberian Union (Portugal under the Spanish Habsburgs)

Personal union

Who & why: King Sebastian dies heirless at the 1578 Battle of Alcácer Quibir in Morocco; the elderly Cardinal-King Henry, his successor, dies childless two years later, triggering a succession crisis. Philip II of Spain, Sebastian's uncle, presses his claim by force, defeats a rival claimant at the Battle of Alcântara, and is acclaimed King Philip I of Portugal by the Cortes of Tomar in 1581. For the only sixty-year stretch in its history, Portugal answers to a monarch based in Madrid — though, crucially, as a personal rather than legal union: Portugal keeps its own laws, currency, Cortes, and colonial administration throughout.

What it represents: no distinct "Union-era Portugal" flag is separately documented; Portuguese ships and forces of this period flew the Cross of Burgundy — the red saltire of gnarled branches used across the whole composite Habsburg monarchy, from the Netherlands to the Spanish Americas — alongside, not replacing, continued local use of the older Portuguese royal arms.

1139 – 1580

Kingdom of Portugal (medieval, independent)

Kingdom

Who & why: Afonso Henriques, Count of Portugal, defeats a Moorish coalition at the Battle of Ourique in July 1139 and is acclaimed king by his troops on the field; León recognizes full Portuguese independence by the Treaty of Zamora in 1143, and the papacy confirms it in 1179. The medieval kingdom completes its own reconquest by 1249 — among the oldest stable borders in Europe — then, from the early 1400s, launches the maritime Age of Discovery: Henry the Navigator's voyages, Bartolomeu Dias rounding the Cape of Good Hope in 1488, Vasco da Gama reaching India in 1498, and Pedro Álvares Cabral reaching Brazil in 1500, building a trading empire that stretches from Brazil to Africa to Goa to Macau.

What it represents: the quinas — five small shields recalling, in national legend, five Moorish kings defeated by Afonso Henriques — on a blue field, itself bordered in red with gold castles (added after the 1249 conquest of the Algarve, commemorating the castles taken); the same arms, in updated forms, remain on Portugal's flag to this day.

c. 1096 – 1139

County of Portugal

Vassal county

Who & why: King Alfonso VI of León grants the County of Portugal, between the Minho and Tagus rivers, to his son-in-law Henry of Burgundy around 1096 as a frontier fief against Al-Andalus — part of a broader pattern of French/Burgundian knights drawn south by the Reconquista. Henry rules as count until his death in 1112; his widow, Teresa of León, governs as regent until their son, Afonso Henriques, comes of age, rebels against his own mother's faction at the 1128 Battle of São Mamede, and takes personal control of the county he will lead to independence eleven years later.

What it represents: no securely documented banner survives specifically for the county in this pre-heraldic period — the systematic use of arms and fixed territorial flags across western Europe is still a generation or two away. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented, in line with this atlas's practice elsewhere.
