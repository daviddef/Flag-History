Flags of the Mughal Empire

A Central Asian prince's conquest of the Indian subcontinent, sustained across two centuries by rulers who ranged from religiously tolerant and administratively brilliant to militarily overextended and fiscally exhausted — ending not in one dramatic collapse but in a long, uneven fade from continental power to a British-controlled rump, formally abolished only after the failed 1857 rebellion.

Babur, a Timurid prince descended from both Timur and Genghis Khan, brings the dynasty's own martial and administrative traditions south into India; his descendants build one of the wealthiest and most architecturally ambitious states of the early modern world before it is gradually hollowed out from within and without.

Scope of this page: the Mughal Empire on the Indian subcontinent, 1526–1857, six political phases. The empire predates and directly precedes British colonial India; see South Asia hub for how the subcontinent's own pages pick up the story where this one ends. 1803 – 1857

Token Delhi rump under British East India Company influence

Rump state, subordinate

Who & why: the East India Company's 1803 capture of Delhi during the Second Anglo-Maratha War leaves the Mughal emperor a Company pensioner, ruling in name only over the city and a small surrounding territory while the Company administers the real business of empire across most of India. The 1857 Indian Rebellion (the Sepoy Mutiny) briefly rallies around the aged emperor Bahadur Shah II as a symbolic figurehead; its failure gives the British the pretext to end the fiction entirely.

What it represents: no distinct Mughal flag from this rump period is separately documented; the Company's own colors are shown here schematically to represent the real locus of power during this era.

1707 – 1803

Long decline (post-Aurangzeb)

Empire, declining

Who & why: Aurangzeb's death in 1707 ends the last reign of real centralized Mughal power; his decades of near-constant warfare in the Deccan and his reversal of earlier religious-tolerance policies leave the treasury drained and regional resentment high. A rapid succession of weak emperors follows as the Maratha Confederacy expands aggressively across central and western India, Persian ruler Nadir Shah sacks Delhi itself in 1739 (carrying off the Peacock Throne), and Afghan invasions under Ahmad Shah Durrani further weaken the court through the mid-18th century. Real power increasingly passes to regional nawabs and, from the 1757 Battle of Plassey onward, to the British East India Company.

What it represents: the green alam (battle standard) tradition continues through this era as the court's military and religious emblem, though the empire it represents controls ever less territory; shown as a reconstruction, since no single fixed state flag in the modern sense is precisely documented.

1658 – 1707

Aurangzeb (territorial peak, fiscal and religious strain)

Empire, at territorial peak

Who & why: Aurangzeb seizes the throne in 1658 after a war of succession against his brothers and imprisons his own father, Shah Jahan, for the remainder of his life. Under Aurangzeb the empire reaches its greatest-ever territorial extent, pushing deep into the Deccan and South India by the 1680s–90s. But relentless military campaigning against the Marathas, reintroduction of the jizya tax on non-Muslims, and a stricter religious policy than his predecessors' strain both the treasury and the empire's famously broad base of support, sowing the discontent that erupts after his death.

What it represents: the green alam battle-standard tradition, carried by Mughal armies and associated with the dynasty's Timurid and Islamic heritage, is shown here at its most territorially expansive use; reconstructed, as no single fixed national flag design is precisely documented for this period.

green alam, imperial peak — the Taj Mahal era
1628 – 1658

Shah Jahan (imperial peak, architectural golden age)

Empire, at territorial peak

Who & why: Shah Jahan, Akbar's grandson, presides over the empire's cultural and architectural high point, commanding vast wealth from a stable, expanded realm. Grief over the 1631 death of his wife Mumtaz Mahal leads to the construction of the Taj Mahal in Agra (1632–53), alongside the Red Fort and the Peacock Throne in his new capital of Shahjahanabad (Old Delhi). His reign is later remembered as the empire's zenith of stability and prosperity, even as court intrigue over succession builds beneath the surface.

What it represents: the green alam battle-standard tradition continues as the dynasty's military and religious emblem through this era of peak wealth and building; reconstructed, since no single fixed national flag design survives with precision from the period.

1556 – 1628

Akbar the Great to Jahangir (consolidation, tolerance, administration)

Empire, independent

Who & why: Akbar, crowned at age 13 in 1556 after his father Humayun's brief restoration and sudden death, rules for nearly half a century and is widely regarded as the empire's greatest administrator. He abolishes the jizya tax on non-Muslims, marries into Rajput nobility, patronizes a syncretic court culture (including his own short-lived Din-i Ilahi religious synthesis), and builds a centralized revenue and provincial (subah) system that outlasts him by centuries. His son Jahangir (r. 1605–27) largely maintains this settlement while expanding court patronage of Mughal painting and gardens.

What it represents: the green alam — a battle standard rooted in Timurid and broader Islamic military tradition — is the best-attested Mughal emblem of this era, shown here as an informed reconstruction rather than a precisely documented fixed design.

1526 – 1556

Babur and Humayun (founding and early instability)

Empire, independent

Who & why: Babur, a Timurid prince from Ferghana (in modern Uzbekistan) descended from both Timur and, through his mother, Genghis Khan, is driven from his own Central Asian homeland before turning south. His decisive victory at the First Battle of Panipat in April 1526 — using field artillery and Ottoman-style tactics against the far larger army of the Delhi Sultanate's Ibrahim Lodi — founds the empire. Babur dies just four years later in 1530; his son Humayun loses most of the empire to the Afghan noble Sher Shah Suri in the 1540s and spends over a decade in Persian exile before reconquering Delhi in 1555, dying the following year in an accidental fall.

What it represents: the green alam (battle standard) tradition, carried from the dynasty's Central Asian and Islamic military heritage, is the earliest attested Mughal emblem; shown here as an honest reconstruction of the type rather than a precisely documented fixed design.
