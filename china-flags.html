Flags of China

China's imperial history is among the longest continuously documented state traditions on earth, running from the Qin unification of 221 BCE through two thousand years of dynastic rule to the fall of the last emperor in 1912. But none of it used a national flag in the modern sense — that idea only arrives with the late Qing's contact with Western and Japanese naval powers in the 1860s–80s. This page treats the deep dynastic span honestly: dynasty-associated colors and devices where the historical record supports them, clearly marked as not being flags, followed by the real flags of the 1889 Qing dragon banner, the 1912 Republic, and the People's Republic of China proclaimed in 1949.

This is the mainland Chinese lineage: the People's Republic of China (1949–present) and everything that came before it on the mainland. Taiwan's own page in this atlas covers the Republic of China's continued flag lineage after its 1949 relocation there, and the Yuan Dynasty (1271–1368) is only briefly noted here — it was the Mongol Empire's rule over China, and its deeper history belongs to this atlas's Mongol Empire page.

Scope of this page: the Chinese mainland, from the Qin unification in 221 BCE to the present, ten political periods. Taiwan's political status is disputed: the People's Republic of China (founded 1949, this page's current entry) claims Taiwan as its own territory, while Taiwan has been self-governing under the Republic of China's government since 1949, with its own military, elections, and currency. This page states that dispute as a live, unresolved fact without taking a position on which claim is correct — see Taiwan's own page for the Republic of China's side of the story since 1949. the Five-Starred Red Flag — unchanged since 1949
1949 – present

People's Republic of China

Communist one-party state

Who & why: the Chinese Communist Party, led by Mao Zedong, wins the Chinese Civil War against the Nationalist Kuomintang and proclaims the People's Republic in Beijing on 1 October 1949, while the defeated Nationalist government relocates to Taiwan (see Taiwan's own page). The PRC governs the mainland continuously since, through the Great Leap Forward, the Cultural Revolution, Deng Xiaoping's post-1978 market reforms, and its emergence as the world's second-largest economy. The PRC claims Taiwan as part of its own territory, a claim Taiwan's government disputes; this remains an unresolved, live political question.

What it represents: designed by Zeng Liansong and adopted just before the founding declaration, the large gold star stands for the Communist Party's leadership, the four smaller stars for the social classes (workers, peasants, urban petty bourgeoisie, and national capitalists) the party claimed to unite. Red is the color of revolution and of Han Chinese tradition alike.

1928 – 1949

Republic of China (Nationalist government, mainland)

Republic, one-party dominant

Who & why: Chiang Kai-shek's Nationalist Kuomintang completes the Northern Expedition against regional warlords in 1928 and adopts this flag — already the Kuomintang's own party banner since 1917–25 — as the national flag, ending the Beiyang era. The government fights a brutal, if intermittently paused, civil war against the Communist Party from 1927 almost continuously to 1949, interrupted by the full-scale Japanese invasion of 1937–45 (part of World War II in the Pacific), before losing the mainland to the Communists in 1949 and relocating to Taiwan, where this same flag continues today — see Taiwan's own page.

What it represents: the white 12-rayed sun on blue (Blue Sky with a White Sun) was Kuomintang founder Sun Yat-sen's own revolutionary emblem; the rays stand for the twelve traditional double-hours of the day, urging constant progress. Red for the field completes "Blue Sky, White Sun, and a Wholly Red Earth."

1912 – 1928

Republic of China (Beiyang government)

Republic, fragmented

Who & why: the Xinhai Revolution, beginning with the Wuchang Uprising in October 1911, topples the last Qing emperor, six-year-old Puyi, who formally abdicates in February 1912, ending over two thousand years of imperial rule. The republic that follows is nominally unified but in practice runs through competing Beiyang Army warlord factions and a series of weak central governments in Beijing, until the Kuomintang's Northern Expedition brings the country under one nationalist government by 1928.

What it represents: five horizontal stripes for the "five races" the new republic claimed to unite — Han (red), Manchu (yellow), Mongol (blue), Hui/Muslim (white), and Tibetan (black) — a deliberate break from the dynastic dragon banner toward an inclusive multi-ethnic republic.

1644 – 1912 (national flag from 1889)

Qing Dynasty

Imperial dynasty

Who & why: the Manchu-led Qing conquer Beijing in 1644 and rule China for nearly 270 years, China's last imperial dynasty. Facing Western and Japanese naval powers that all sailed under national flags, the Qing court adopts a triangular yellow dragon banner for its navy in the 1860s–70s and formalizes a rectangular version as the empire's first official national flag in 1889. The dynasty's final decades bring the Opium Wars, the Taiping Rebellion, and mounting foreign pressure, ending with the 1911 Xinhai Revolution and Puyi's 1912 abdication.

What it represents: the azure five-clawed dragon, an imperial emblem used for centuries before it ever appeared on a flag, chasing a flaming red pearl across a field of imperial yellow — the dynasty's own color. This is the flag's late, most standardized 1889 form; earlier Qing use was informal and mostly naval.

1368 – 1644

Ming Dynasty

Imperial dynasty

Who & why: Zhu Yuanzhang, a former peasant and monk, leads the rebellion that drives out the Mongol Yuan Dynasty and founds the Ming in 1368, restoring native Han Chinese rule. The Ming build the Forbidden City, send Zheng He's treasure fleets across the Indian Ocean, and complete much of the Great Wall's surviving stone construction, before falling to peasant rebellion and the invading Manchu in 1644.

What it represents: no flag in the vexillological sense exists yet anywhere in Chinese governance; imperial authority is marked instead through dragon-embroidered banners, yellow as the emperor's reserved color, and elaborate court regalia. Shown here honestly as undocumented rather than invented, with the dynasty's own red-and-gold palette as a reference only.

1271 – 1368

Yuan Dynasty (Mongol rule)

Foreign imperial dynasty

Who & why: Kublai Khan, grandson of Genghis Khan, completes the conquest of Song China and proclaims the Yuan Dynasty in 1271, ruling China as one part of the wider Mongol Empire's fractured successor khanates. Yuan rule ends in 1368 when Ming rebellion drives the Mongol court back to the steppe. This page keeps the Yuan entry brief on purpose: the Mongol Empire's own formation, conquests, and banners are covered in far greater depth on this atlas's dedicated Mongol Empire page, and Mongolia's own later national history is covered on its own page too.

What it represents: no fixed Yuan state flag is documented; Mongol military and court banners of this era are treated fully on the Mongol Empire page rather than duplicated here.

960 – 1279

Song Dynasty

Imperial dynasty

Who & why: Zhao Kuangyin reunifies most of China after the chaotic Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms period, founding the Song in 960. The dynasty presides over a commercial and technological golden age — movable type, gunpowder weapons, paper money — but never fully controls the north, losing it to the Jurchen Jin in 1127 (the Northern/Southern Song divide) before the remaining Southern Song is finally conquered by Kublai Khan's Mongols in 1279, absorbed into the Yuan.

What it represents: as with the Ming, no state flag exists yet; red is the dynasty's associated ceremonial color under traditional five-elements cosmology, shown here as a color reference rather than a documented banner.

618 – 907

Tang Dynasty

Imperial dynasty

Who & why: the Li family founds the Tang in 618 after the short, harsh Sui Dynasty's collapse, presiding over what is widely regarded as a high point of premodern Chinese civilization — cosmopolitan Chang'an (then likely the world's largest city), the flourishing of Tang poetry, and Silk Road trade at its peak. The dynasty weakens after the An Lushan Rebellion (755–63) and finally collapses in 907, opening the fractured Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms period that the Song later reunifies.

What it represents: again, no flag survives from this period; the placeholder gold reflects the dynasty's imperial and Buddhist-influenced court palette rather than any attested banner.

206 BCE – 220 CE

Han Dynasty

Imperial dynasty

Who & why: Liu Bang founds the Han after the short-lived Qin's collapse, ruling (with the brief Wang Mang interregnum splitting it into Western and Eastern Han) for roughly four centuries — a span so long and so formative that China's majority ethnic group still calls itself Han today. The dynasty expands the empire's borders, opens the Silk Road under Emperor Wu, and standardizes Confucian state ideology, before regional warlordism ends it in 220 CE, opening the Three Kingdoms period.

What it represents: no national flag exists this early; red, associated with the fire element that Han cosmology assigned the dynasty, is shown here as an informed color reference only.

221 – 206 BCE

Qin Dynasty

Imperial dynasty

Who & why: King Zheng of the state of Qin conquers the last of the rival Warring States and proclaims himself Qin Shi Huang, the "First Emperor," in 221 BCE — the moment this page treats as China's first unified imperial state, and the origin of the very name "China" in most Western languages. His famously short but transformative fifteen-year dynasty standardizes writing, currency, and cart-axle widths, links early wall fortifications into what becomes the Great Wall's first version, and builds the terracotta army; it collapses into civil war within four years of his death, giving way to the Han.

What it represents: no flag exists in this era; Qin state ideology, following the Five Elements theory, formally favored black as the color of the water element the dynasty claimed to rule under — a documented historical detail shown here as a color reference, not an invented flag.
