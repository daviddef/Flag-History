Flags of Bangladesh

The land of historic Bengal — the delta of the Ganges and Brahmaputra — passes through ancient Buddhist and Hindu dynasties, an Islamic sultanate, two centuries as a province of larger empires, a century and a half of British colonial rule, a hurried 1947 partition, twenty-four years as the geographically separated eastern wing of Pakistan, and a nine-month war of independence, before emerging in December 1971 as the People's Republic of Bangladesh — the youngest sovereign state, and the youngest flag, in this hub.

Bangladesh's own flag is comparatively brief in its history but direct in its symbolism: a red disc, for the sun and for the sacrifice of the liberation struggle, set against a green field for the land itself. It was first raised as an improvised wartime banner in March 1971 and settled into its present, simpler form the following year.

Scope of this page: the territory of present-day Bangladesh (historic East Bengal), c. 3rd century BCE – present, eight political entities. The Mughal era (1576–1757) is covered here only briefly, as one province among many under a single imperial flag; the empire's own fuller lineage is covered on its own page — see the link in that entry. The 1971 Liberation War is stated factually and briefly, limited to well-documented dates and outcomes rather than a full military or human account. 1972 – present

People’s Republic of Bangladesh

Republic, independent

Who & why: Sheikh Mujibur Rahman, released from Pakistani custody in January 1972, returns to Dhaka to lead the new state as its first head of government, and a constitution is adopted later that year. In January 1972 the wartime flag is formally simplified: the gold silhouette map inside the red disc is dropped, since it printed and sewed inconsistently and read differently on each side of the cloth, and the disc's position is fixed slightly toward the hoist so that it appears visually centered when the flag flies.

What it represents: green for Bangladesh's fields and forests, and the red disc for the rising sun and for the blood of those lost in the 1971 war for independence — the same design, unaltered since 1972, still flown today.

Paint guide
Proportions10:6 field; red disc radius = 1/5 of the flag's length, centered vertically, its own center set at 2/5 (not 1/2) of the length from the hoist.
LayoutPlain green field, plain red disc, no other emblem.

March – December 1971

Liberation War & provisional Bangladesh

Provisional government, wartime

Who & why: after the Awami League wins a decisive majority in Pakistan's December 1970 general election but is denied the transfer of power, the Pakistani military launches a crackdown in East Pakistan in late March 1971. Sheikh Mujibur Rahman is arrested and independence is declared on 26 March 1971; a provisional government is organized in exile at Mujibnagar in April. A nine-month war follows, fought in large part by the Mukti Bahini resistance, until India enters the conflict in December 1971 and Pakistani forces surrender at Dhaka on 16 December 1971, the day Bangladesh's independence is secured.

What it represents: a green flag with a red sun disc, its center shifted toward the hoist, bearing a gold silhouette map of Bangladesh inside the disc; students at Dhaka University first raised a version of this design on 2 March 1971, and the provisional government adopted it soon after as the new nation's banner.

1947 – 1971

East Bengal / East Pakistan

Province, union state

Who & why: the August 1947 partition of British India splits Bengal along the Radcliffe Line: West Bengal joins India, and East Bengal joins the new Dominion of Pakistan as its eastern wing, geographically separated from West Pakistan by roughly 1,600 km of Indian territory (renamed East Pakistan in 1955). Tensions build over the decades that follow — the 1952 Bengali Language Movement, sparked by a push to make Urdu Pakistan's sole national language, becomes an early and formative grievance, later commemorated internationally as Mother Language Day. Political and economic marginalization of the Bengali-majority east feeds a growing nationalist movement that culminates in the Awami League's 1970 election mandate.

What it represents: Pakistan's national flag, adopted in 1947 — a green field for the Muslim majority with a white hoist stripe for religious minorities, and a white crescent and star — flown across both wings of the state, including East Pakistan.

1858 – 1947

Bengal under the British Raj

Colony, crown rule

Who & why: following the 1857 rebellion, the British Crown formally assumes direct control of Company territories under the Government of India Act 1858, ending Company rule and beginning the Raj. Bengal is governed as its own presidency and, later, province within British India; Lord Curzon's controversial 1905 partition of Bengal along religious lines is reversed in 1911 after mass protest, foreshadowing the deeper partition to come in 1947. British rule over Bengal ends with Indian independence and the 1947 partition.

What it represents: the Union flag of the United Kingdom, flown with the Star of India badge (used on Raj-era ensigns and viceregal flags) marking British India as a crown possession; shown here as a schematic composite rather than any single specific ensign.

1757 – 1858

Bengal under East India Company rule

Colony, company rule

Who & why: the British East India Company defeats Nawab Siraj-ud-Daulah at the Battle of Plassey on 23 June 1757, gaining effective control of Bengal, and formalizes its authority in 1765 when the Mughal emperor Shah Alam II grants it the diwani (the right to collect revenue) over Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa. The Company rules Bengal as a commercial and administrative enterprise, nominally under Mughal suzerainty at first, for a century, until the 1857 rebellion ends Company governance.

What it represents: the Company's own red-and-white striped ensign, carrying the British Union flag in the canton, used on its ships and establishments across its Indian territories, including Bengal, through this period.

1576 – 1757

Bengal under the Mughal Empire

Empire, province

Who & why: the Mughal Emperor Akbar completes the conquest of the Bengal Sultanate in 1576, absorbing Bengal as one of the empire's wealthiest provinces (a subah), administered from Dhaka for much of this period. Bengal remains under Mughal governance — increasingly autonomous under its own nawabs in the 1700s as central Mughal authority weakens — until the Company's victory at Plassey in 1757. For the empire's own fuller lineage and flags, see the dedicated Mughal Empire page in this atlas.

What it represents: a green field bearing a gold sun motif, representative of the Mughal imperial alam (banner) tradition used across the empire's provinces, shown here schematically for Bengal's own span under that empire rather than as a province-specific record.

1204 – 1576

Bengal Sultanate

Sultanate

Who & why: Muhammad bin Bakhtiyar Khalji's 1204 conquest brings Bengal under Delhi Sultanate governors; the region breaks away to found its own independent Bengal Sultanate in 1352, which endures — through several ruling dynasties, including the Ilyas Shahi and Hussain Shahi lines — as one of the era's major regional Islamic powers, known for trade, textiles, and architecture, until Mughal conquest in 1576.

What it represents: no single specific Bengal Sultanate flag survives in the documentary record; the plain dark field with a crescent shown here follows the general conventions of regional Islamic sultanate banners of the period and should be read as an informed reconstruction, not a documented Bengal-specific design.

c. 3rd century BCE – 1204 CE

Historic Bengal (ancient & medieval)

Kingdom / empire, regional

Who & why: the Bengal delta passes through the Maurya and Gupta empires, local dynasties such as Shashanka's early 7th-century kingdom, and, from the 8th century, the Buddhist Pala Empire, one of the region's most powerful and long-lived states. The Hindu Sena dynasty supplants the Palas in the 12th century, ruling until the 1204 conquest that opens the Islamic era in Bengal.

What it represents: no securely documented flag or standardized banner survives from any of these dynasties; ancient and early medieval South Asian states are attested through inscriptions, coinage, and royal seals rather than vexillological records. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

Reading the tags

Kingdom / empire, regional — sovereign under a local or regional crown
Sultanate — independent Islamic regional state
Empire, province — a province of a larger multi-region empire
Colony, company rule — governed by a chartered trading company
Colony, crown rule — governed directly by a foreign crown
Province, union state — a constituent wing/province of a larger sovereign state
Provisional government, wartime — a government formed during an active war of independence
Republic, independent — sovereign under its own elected government
Leader — the named ruler most associated with the era's start
State religion — the official/established faith, not the population's
