Flags of Bhutan

A Himalayan kingdom of fragmented valley lords, unified in the 1620s–30s by a Tibetan lama-turned-statesman into a unique dual theocracy, and reorganized in 1907 into the hereditary monarchy that still rules today — Bhutan is the rare case in this atlas of a country that was never colonized, never partitioned, and never absorbed into a neighbor's union. Its dragon banner, the Druk, has marked the state in one form or another since unification itself.

Bhutan calls itself Druk Yul, "Land of the Thunder Dragon," after the Drukpa Buddhist lineage that shaped its unification, and the white dragon on the modern flag is that same Druk — not a 20th-century invention, but the visual endpoint of a symbol that predates the flag it now sits on by roughly three centuries.

Scope of this page: the territory of present-day Bhutan, c. 900–present, five entries. Bhutan's 1865 Treaty of Sinchula (ceding the Duars strip to British India after a short conflict) and its 1910 Treaty of Punakha (British-guided foreign relations, never colonial administration) mark its relationship with the British Raj but do not interrupt Bhutanese sovereignty or self-governance at any point, so neither produces its own entry here. Kingdom of Bhutan (modern, standardized flag)

Kingdom, hereditary monarchyJigme Dorji WangchuckVajrayana Buddhism (state religion)

Who & why: under the third and fourth Druk Gyalpos, Jigme Dorji Wangchuck and Jigme Singye Wangchuck, Bhutan opens cautiously to the outside world — joining the UN in 1971, ending its self-imposed isolation in stages, and modernizing its institutions while carefully guarding its own culture and sovereignty. Somewhere in this stretch (commonly dated to the late 1960s, with the exact year debated among sources) the national flag's design, proportions, and colors are fixed into the form still flown today.

What it represents: the flag is split diagonally, yellow upper triangle (the secular authority of the Druk Gyalpo) over orange lower triangle (the Buddhist spiritual tradition and the Drukpa monastic bodies), with the white Druk, the Thunder Dragon, centered along the divide, clutching jewels that represent the nation's wealth and security. The dragon's snarling posture is read as guardianship, not aggression.

Kingdom of Bhutan (early national flag)

Kingdom, hereditary monarchyJigme WangchuckVajrayana Buddhism (state religion)

Who & why: the earliest version of a Bhutanese national flag is commonly associated with the runup to the 1949 Treaty of Friendship with newly independent India, which confirms Bhutan's full sovereignty over its own internal affairs while India guides its external relations — a deliberately narrow arrangement, never colonial rule. Under the second Druk Gyalpo, Jigme Wangchuck, and into the reign of the third, Jigme Dorji Wangchuck, the young kingdom needs a flag to represent itself at this kind of state occasion for the first time.

What it represents: the same diagonal yellow-over-orange field and white Druk as the modern flag, but sources disagree on the exact hues, the triangle proportions, and even whether the flag was square rather than rectangular in this early period. Shown here as an informed approximation, muted and slightly desaturated to flag that uncertainty, rather than as a documented specification.

Kingdom of Bhutan (early hereditary monarchy)

Kingdom, hereditary monarchyUgyen WangchuckVajrayana Buddhism (state religion)

Who & why: on 17 December 1907, an assembly of monks, officials, and regional leaders elects Ugyen Wangchuck, the powerful penlop (governor) of Trongsa, as Bhutan's first hereditary Druk Gyalpo, ending the dual Druk Desi system and founding the Wangchuck dynasty. The 1910 Treaty of Punakha follows, formalizing British-guided (never colonial) management of Bhutan's foreign relations while leaving its internal governance fully in Bhutanese hands.

What it represents: no single, fixed national flag is confidently documented for this transitional early-monarchy period; dragon banners and local Druk emblems continue in monastic and state use, but a codified national flag is not attested until the treaty-era design that follows. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

Unified Bhutan — the Druk Desi theocracy

TheocracyZhabdrung Ngawang NamgyalVajrayana Buddhism (Drukpa Kagyu, state religion)

Who & why: Ngawang Namgyal, a Tibetan lama of the Drukpa Kagyu lineage who fled succession disputes in Tibet, arrives in Bhutan in 1616 and over the following decades unifies its fractious valleys into a single state, formalizing a dual system of governance: a spiritual head, the Je Khenpo, and a temporal ruler, the Druk Desi, ruling in tandem. The system holds, in modified form, for close to three centuries. The 19th century brings real strain — rival regional penlops (district governors) contest power and central authority weakens at times — but no rival state or rival flag emerges; the Druk Desi framework itself persists on paper even as, from the 1850s onward, Ugyen Wangchuck, the penlop of Trongsa, steadily consolidates real power, decisively so after his 1885 victory at the Battle of Changzamdo, setting up the transition to hereditary monarchy in 1907.

What it represents: no single fixed national banner survives from this era in the way later flags are documented, but the Druk, the Thunder Dragon, is the defining symbol of Namgyal's unified state from the outset — giving the country its own name, Druk Yul. Shown here as a stylized reconstruction of that emblem on a plain field, representing the symbol's presence through the period rather than a specific attested flag.

c. 900 – 1616

Fragmented valley lords and monastic principalities

Fragmented chieftainciesNo single ruler documentedBuddhism (Vajrayana, varied by school)

Who & why: before unification, the valleys of what becomes Bhutan are held by local lords and clan chiefs, with Buddhist monastic schools — arriving in waves from Tibet from roughly the 8th century onward and intensifying with the Drukpa Kagyu presence from the 12th and 13th centuries — exercising growing religious and, in places, political influence. No single ruler or state unites the region for the whole of this span; that only changes with Ngawang Namgyal's arrival in 1616 and the unification he carries out over the following decades.

What it represents: no securely documented flag or banner survives from this fragmented, pre-unification era. Individual valleys and monastic estates likely used their own local emblems, but none can be attributed to the region as a whole with any confidence. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
