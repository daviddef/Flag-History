Flags of Central & Southern Africa

Fourteen countries, from the Congo basin to the Cape. Long before any European flag arrived here, this region held real states of its own: the Kingdom of Kongo on the Atlantic coast, the stone-walled capital of Great Zimbabwe and the Kingdom of Zimbabwe it anchored, the Zulu Kingdom that Shaka forged through the Mfecane, the Basotho kingdom Moshoeshoe I built in the mountains of Lesotho, and the Ndebele (Matabele) kingdom that split from the Zulu and resettled in what is now Zimbabwe.

European colonization arrived in overlapping waves and was not uniform in character. Portugal held Angola and Mozambique from the 1500s into the 1970s. Britain and the Netherlands both colonized the Cape, and their rivalry shaped South Africa, Namibia (German, not Anglo-Dutch, until 1915), Zimbabwe, Zambia, Malawi, Botswana, Lesotho, and Eswatini. Belgium's rule of the Congo Free State under King Leopold II, from 1885 to 1908, was a private royal fiefdom run through forced rubber labor, mutilation, and mass killing on a scale that shocked contemporary observers and remains one of the most severe documented atrocities of the colonial era; Belgium annexed it as a state colony in 1908, and it did not become independent, as the Democratic Republic of the Congo, until 1960. And South Africa's own history includes apartheid: a system of state-mandated racial segregation and white-minority rule formally instituted in 1948, which classified and controlled the population by race, forcibly displaced Black South Africans into homelands and townships, and denied Black, Coloured, and Indian South Africans basic political rights, until decades of resistance and international pressure brought multiracial elections in 1994 and Nelson Mandela's presidency.

About this hub

Central and Southern Africa's flag history is inseparable from its colonial history, and that history was not gentle here. The Congo Free State under Leopold II is among the most severe documented atrocities of the colonial era; German South West Africa's suppression of the Herero and Nama uprisings (1904-08) is widely recognized as the 20th century's first genocide; and South Africa's apartheid system, formalized in 1948 and dismantled only in 1994, structured an entire society around racial hierarchy by law. This atlas states these facts plainly rather than softening them, consistent with how it treats difficult history elsewhere.
