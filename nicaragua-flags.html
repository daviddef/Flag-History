Flags of Nicaragua

From indigenous chiefdoms around Lakes Managua and Nicaragua, through the Spanish colonial cities of León and Granada — rivals whose feud shaped Nicaraguan politics for centuries — three centuries as the largest province of the Captaincy General of Guatemala, a joint 1821 independence, annexation to the First Mexican Empire, membership in the Federal Republic of Central America, and finally an eventful century and a half of sovereign statehood under one continuously reused blue-white-blue banner.

Nicaragua's flag has flown, largely unchanged in design, over Conservative and Liberal governments, the Somoza family dictatorship, the 1979 Sandinista Revolution, and the Contra War that followed — the same banner claimed by opposing sides of nearly every one of the country's internal conflicts.

Scope of this page: the territory of present-day Nicaragua, c. 1200–present, six political entities. This page carries no interactive map — flag timeline only.
blue-white-blue, federation-descended arms with five volcanoes and rising sun rays
1908 – present

Republic of Nicaragua (modern)

Republic, independentJosé Santos ZelayaSecular (no state religion)

Who & why: the current flag and arms design is fixed by law in 1908 (proportions later refined in 1971) and survives every subsequent upheaval: the Somoza family dictatorship (1936–1979, propped up through most of that span by U.S. support), the 1979 Sandinista Revolution that overthrows it, and the 1980s Contra War between the Sandinista government and U.S.-backed rebels. Both the Sandinista government and its opponents fly the same national banner throughout — the flag itself is never a partisan symbol in these conflicts.

What it represents: the blue-white-blue bands descend directly from the Federal Republic of Central America's flag; the arms — five volcanoes (for the five federation states) beneath a rainbow and a liberty cap, with added rays of the rising sun — assert the same claim of federation heritage that Nicaragua's neighbors also carry.

1838 – 1908

Republic of Nicaragua (early)

Republic, earlyNo single ruler documentedRoman Catholic (traditional, gradually curtailed)

Who & why: Nicaragua secedes from the crumbling Federal Republic on 30 April 1838, becoming fully sovereign. The 19th century is turbulent: the rival cities of León (Liberal) and Granada (Conservative) contest the capital for decades, and in 1855–57 the American filibuster William Walker seizes power outright and is expelled only after a combined Central American military campaign. Successive governments adopt their own variations of the coat of arms through this period, all on the same blue-white-blue field.

What it represents: continuity with the federation's own tricolor, carrying an early, less standardized version of the volcano-and-rainbow arms later fixed in 1908.

1823 – 1838

Federal Republic of Central America (member state)

Federation memberManuel José ArceRoman Catholic (constitutionally established)

Who & why: after breaking from the First Mexican Empire, the five former Guatemalan provinces — including Nicaragua, the union's largest territory — form the United Provinces of Central America on 1 July 1823, reorganized as the Federal Republic in 1824. Rivalry between León and Granada undermines Nicaraguan participation from the start, and Nicaragua formally secedes as the federation disintegrates in April 1838, ahead of the union's final end elsewhere around 1841.

What it represents: the blue-white-blue triband adopted by the federation in 1823, with a central coat of arms — five volcanoes for the five member states beneath a rainbow and a Phrygian liberty cap on a staff. Every successor state, Nicaragua included, keeps a version of these colors.

1821 – 1823

Annexation to the First Mexican Empire

Empire (annexed)Agustín de IturbideRoman Catholic (exclusive, per the Plan de Iguala)

Who & why: the Captaincy General of Guatemala, including Nicaragua, declares independence from Spain jointly with its neighbors on 15 September 1821. León's authorities initially favor annexation to Agustín de Iturbide's new Mexican Empire while Granada is more hesitant, but the whole province is absorbed regardless within months, only to be released again when the empire collapses in 1823.

What it represents: the green-white-red tricolor of the First Mexican Empire, flown briefly over Nicaragua before the empire's fall opens the way to a Central American federation instead.

1524 – 1821

Spanish colonial rule (Captaincy General of Guatemala)

ColonyFrancisco Hernández de CórdobaRoman Catholic (Spanish Crown, exclusive)

Who & why: Francisco Hernández de Córdoba founds Granada, on Lake Nicaragua, and León, near Lake Managua, both in 1524, establishing Spanish control over the territory's most populous zones. Nicaragua becomes the largest province of the Captaincy General of Guatemala, administered from Guatemala City under the Viceroyalty of New Spain, though its distance from the capital and the León–Granada rivalry keep it politically distinct throughout the colonial period.

What it represents: the red raggedy saltire (Cross of Burgundy) on a plain field, the standard military and civil ensign flown across Spain's American colonies, including throughout Central America.

c. 1200 – 1524

Nicarao, Chorotega, and neighboring chiefdoms

Indigenous kingdomNicarao (namesake cacique)Nicarao/Chorotega polytheism

Who & why: by the time of first Spanish contact, the territory around Lakes Managua and Nicaragua is home to Nahua-speaking Nicarao chiefdoms (whose ruler's name the Spanish later apply to the whole country) alongside Chorotega and other indigenous groups, organized in cacicazgos rather than one unified state. Gil González Dávila's 1522–23 expedition makes first sustained contact; sustained conquest and settlement follow in 1524.

What it represents: no securely documented flag or banner survives from this pre-conquest era; Mesoamerican polities used feathered standards and painted devices rather than fixed heraldic flags, and none can be attributed to any single Nicaraguan cacicazgo with confidence. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
