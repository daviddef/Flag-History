Flags of Mauritius

Uninhabited until Dutch sailors named it for their stadtholder in 1598, Mauritius passed through Dutch abandonment, French plantation settlement, and British conquest before becoming, after 1834, the destination for one of the largest movements of Indian indentured labor anywhere in the world — a migration that reshaped the island's demographics so thoroughly that its modern population is majority Indo-Mauritian. Independence in 1968 was followed by a rare regional achievement: sustained, uninterrupted multiparty democracy ever since.

Mauritius's current flag, adopted at independence in 1968, has never changed — not even when the country became a republic in 1992, replacing the British monarch with a president as head of state while keeping everything else, including the flag, the same.

Scope of this page: the main island of Mauritius and its outer dependencies, from uninhabited Dutch landfall in 1598 to present, four political-flag eras. No indigenous population predates 1598, so this page carries no precolonial "no flag documented" entry. The 1710–1715 gap, when the Dutch had abandoned the island and no power yet governed it, is noted in the narrative rather than given its own card. 1968 – present

Mauritius (independent Commonwealth realm, then republic)

Republic, independent

Who & why: Mauritius becomes independent from Britain on 12 March 1968 as a Commonwealth realm under the British monarch, led by Prime Minister Seewoosagur Ramgoolam, whose Indo-Mauritian political base reflects the demographic legacy of 19th-century indentured immigration. Sugar remains the economic backbone at independence, though the country diversifies successfully into textiles, tourism, and financial services in the decades that follow. On 12 March 1992, the 24th anniversary of independence, Mauritius becomes a republic within the Commonwealth, replacing the monarch with a president as head of state while changing nothing else about the flag or the political system otherwise. Since independence Mauritius has held regular, competitive multiparty elections without interruption or coup — a genuinely rare record among postcolonial African and Indian Ocean states.

What it represents: four equal horizontal bands — red for the struggle for independence, blue for the Indian Ocean surrounding the island, yellow for the light of independence, and green for the island's year-round agriculture.

1810 – 1968

British colonial rule

Colonial territory

Who & why: Britain captures the island from France in December 1810 during the Napoleonic Wars, and the 1814 Treaty of Paris confirms British possession, renaming it Mauritius; unusually, Britain allows French civil law, language, and Franco-Mauritian landowning elites to remain largely intact. The sugar-plantation economy continues, but the 1833–34 abolition of slavery in the British Empire creates an acute labor shortage that colonial authorities solve by importing indentured laborers from India on a massive scale — roughly half a million arrive over the following decades, permanently remaking the island's demographics into an Indo-Mauritian majority alongside Franco-Mauritian, Creole, and Sino-Mauritian communities. Political reform proceeds gradually through the 20th century, with an elected legislature established from 1885 and full internal self-government by the mid-1960s, ahead of independence in 1968.

What it represents: the standard British colonial pattern — a defaced Blue Ensign bearing the territory's own badge — flown as the colonial flag throughout British rule.

1715 – 1810

French colonial rule (Isle de France)

Colonial territory

Who & why: France claims the island in 1715, five years after the Dutch abandon it, renaming it Isle de France, though real development waits until the French East India Company begins active settlement in the 1720s–30s. Under governors such as Bertrand-François Mahé de La Bourdonnais, the island grows into a major naval and privateering base and sugar-plantation colony worked by enslaved labor brought from Madagascar and East Africa. Britain finally captures the island in December 1810, ending French rule, though the island's French-descended population, language, and law endure under the British administration that follows.

What it represents: France's own royal ensign, plain white bearing the gold Bourbon fleur-de-lis, flown for most of this monarchy-era colonial period; the Revolutionary Tricolor supersedes it from the 1790s as France's own flag changes, in the final two decades before the British conquest.

1598 – 1710

Dutch landfall and settlement attempt

Colonial territory

Who & why: a Dutch fleet under Admiral Wybrand van Warwijck lands on the previously uninhabited island in September 1598, naming it Mauritius after stadtholder Maurice of Nassau. The Dutch East India Company establishes a small settlement from 1638, using the island chiefly as a supply and timber station and briefly introducing sugarcane and, notoriously, hunting the flightless dodo to extinction within decades of contact. Struggling with poor soil, cyclones, and runaway-slave unrest, the Company abandons the settlement for good in 1710, leaving the island ungoverned for five years before France's 1715 claim.

What it represents: the Prince's Flag (orange-white-blue), the Dutch Republic's characteristic ensign of the era, shown here in its later, more standardized red-white-blue form as the orange dye was known to fade to red in period use; no flag specific to this small outpost is separately attested.
