Flags of Vietnam

From over a thousand years under Chinese imperial administration, through independent Vietnamese dynasties, French colonization as part of Indochina, a war-torn decade of contested independence, a formal 1954 partition into two rival states, and a devastating war that ends in 1975–76 reunification — seven entries across one of Southeast Asia's longest and most contested national histories.

Vietnam's modern flag — a gold star on red — was first raised by Ho Chi Minh's independence movement in 1945 and became the flag of the entire reunified country in 1976, after three decades in which it represented only the northern half.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Vietnam, before 111 BCE–present, seven political entries. The 1954–1975 partition into North and South Vietnam, and the 1945–1954 period of contested sovereignty against reasserted French colonial rule, are each shown as single dual-flag entries rather than invented sub-periods, since no clean date split exists for either genuinely contested span. This page carries no internal-partition map; that phase is deferred project-wide. Part of a larger atlas — see the linked pages at the bottom.

1976 – present

Socialist Republic of Vietnam

Republic, socialist

Who & why: North Vietnamese forces take Saigon on 30 April 1975, ending the Vietnam War; a National Assembly formally reunifies the country as the Socialist Republic of Vietnam on 2 July 1976, under Communist Party rule. Vietnam later normalizes relations with the United States (1995) and undertakes sweeping market reforms (đổi mới) while remaining a one-party state.

What it represents: the same gold-star-on-red flag flown by the Democratic Republic of Vietnam since 1945, now extended over the entire reunified country.

1954 – 1975/76

Democratic Republic of Vietnam (North) / Republic of Vietnam (South)

North: Republic, socialistSouth: Republic

Who & why: the 1954 Geneva Accords, ending the First Indochina War, partition the country at the 17th parallel pending elections that are never held. Ho Chi Minh's communist-led Democratic Republic governs the North from Hanoi; the anti-communist, US-backed Republic of Vietnam governs the South from Saigon under Ngô Ñình Diệm and later a succession of military governments. Escalating insurgency and direct US military involvement from 1965 turn the standoff into the Vietnam War, which kills an estimated two million or more Vietnamese before the North's final 1975 offensive takes Saigon.

What it represents: the North's gold-star-on-red flag, flown since 1945 and later extended nationwide; the South's yellow field with three red stripes ("cờ vàng ba sọc đỏ"), inherited from the earlier French-aligned State of Vietnam and still used today by overseas Vietnamese communities opposed to the current government.

1945 – 1954

Democratic Republic of Vietnam (declared) / French Indochina War

Republic, contestedFrench-aligned state

Who & why: Ho Chi Minh proclaims Vietnamese independence in Hanoi on 2 September 1945, days after Japan's surrender. France refuses to accept the loss of its colony and, after a brief standoff, the First Indochina War breaks out in December 1946. France sets up a rival, nominally independent State of Vietnam under former emperor Bảo Đại in 1949 to compete for legitimacy. France's decisive defeat at Điện Biên Phủ in May 1954 forces it to withdraw, and the July 1954 Geneva Accords partition the country at the 17th parallel.

What it represents: Ho Chi Minh's Việt Minh raise the gold-star-on-red flag at independence; the French-backed State of Vietnam answers with a yellow field and three red stripes, a design that outlives it as the later South's flag.

1887 – 1945

French Indochina

Colonial (France)

Who & why: France formally unites its Vietnamese possessions (Tonkin, Annam, Cochinchina) with Cambodia into the Indochinese Union in October 1887, adding Laos in 1893; colonial rule spans a formal protectorate over most of Vietnam and direct control of the Cochinchina colony in the south. After France's 1940 defeat by Germany, Japan occupies Indochina while leaving Vichy French administration nominally in place, before seizing direct control in a March 1945 coup that briefly restores Vietnamese emperor Bảo Đại as a Japanese client just months before Japan's own surrender.

What it represents: the plain French tricolor, the colonial power's own national flag, with no distinct Indochinese symbol.

1802 – 1887

Nguyễn dynasty (independent empire)

Empire, independent

Who & why: Nguyễn Ánh unifies the country after decades of civil war, founding the Nguyễn dynasty and taking the name Gia Long in 1802 — the last Vietnamese imperial dynasty and the first to rule the full length of the country from Huế. French military pressure builds through the mid-1800s, and a series of forced treaties from the 1860s onward progressively strip the empire of sovereignty, culminating in the 1884 Treaty of Huế that establishes the French protectorate completed by 1887.

What it represents: imperial yellow, the color reserved for the emperor in Vietnamese and wider East Asian tradition, forms the field for the dynasty's later banners.

938 – 1802

Independent Vietnamese dynasties

Kingdom, independent

Who & why: Ngô Quyền's 938 victory over a Southern Han fleet at the Bạch Đằng River ends over a thousand years of intermittent Chinese imperial administration and opens nearly nine centuries of independent Vietnamese dynastic rule — the Lý, Trần, later Lê, and others — interrupted only briefly by renewed Chinese occupation (1407–1427) and periods of civil war between rival lords, before the Nguyễn finally reunify the country in 1802.

What it represents: no single flag is securely documented across this long dynastic span; imperial banners and dragon standards changed with each ruling house, so this entry is shown honestly blank rather than inventing one continuous symbol.

Chinese imperial dragon standard, over the administered commanderies
before 938

Chinese imperial rule (Bắc thuộc)

Chinese imperial rule

Who & why: successive Chinese dynasties — beginning with Han conquest in 111 BCE and continuing, with a notable interruption during the Trưng sisters' rebellion (40–43 CE), through the Tang — administer the Red River Delta as a directly ruled set of commanderies for over a thousand years, a period Vietnamese history remembers as Bắc thuộc ("Northern domination"). Repeated rebellions gradually weaken central control, and the Tang dynasty's own collapse creates the opening Ngô Quyền exploits in 938.

What it represents: the imperial Chinese dragon standard, representing the ruling dynasty of the day rather than any distinct Vietnamese symbol, since none existed under direct imperial administration.
