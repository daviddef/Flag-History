Flags of the Bohemian Crown

From a pre-heraldic Přemyslid duchy carved out as Great Moravia collapsed, through a silver two-tailed lion that survived almost unbroken for 750 years across duchy, kingdom, and Habsburg crownland alike, to two catastrophic 20th-century ruptures and a peaceful one — eleven flags, ending with a tricolor-and-wedge design that the Czech Republic simply kept flying after Slovakia left, unmodified since 1920.

White and red are Bohemia's own oldest colors, older than the kingdom itself — when the interwar republic needed a flag distinct from newly-independent Poland's identical white-over-red bicolor, it added a blue wedge from the hoist rather than invent new colors, and that wedge is the only substantive change this lineage's flag has seen in over a century.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Czechia (Bohemia and Moravia), c. 895–present, eleven political entities. Part of a larger atlas — see the map and linked pages at the bottom.

Bohemia and Moravia, two crownlands

Czechia's own real border, split into the two historic crownlands of the Bohemian Crown: Bohemia in the west, the larger core around Prague, and Moravia in the east (with Czech Silesia folded in), governed at various points by its own margrave and its own provincial diet, entirely separately from Bohemia. Moravia outlasted Austria-Hungary as a distinct government — twice. John of Luxembourg carved it off as a separate margraviate for his son Jan Jindřich in 1349; interwar Czechoslovakia revived the same distinction in 1928 as "Land Moravia-Silesia," with its own assembly, right alongside Land Bohemia. 1993 – present

Czech Republic

Sovereign state

Who & why: the "Velvet Divorce" splits Czechoslovakia into two sovereign states by mutual parliamentary agreement, effective 1 January 1993, without a referendum and without the violence that accompanied Yugoslavia's breakup the same decade. The Czech Republic joins NATO in 1999 and the EU in 2004.

What it represents: rather than design a new flag, the Czech Republic simply kept flying the 1920 Czechoslovak flag — a move Slovakia's negotiators objected to at the time, since the 1992 constitutional law dividing the country's assets never formally settled who could keep it. No new design has ever been adopted.

1989 – 1992

Czech and Slovak Federative Republic

Republic (transitional)

Who & why: the Communist Party cedes power within weeks of the November 1989 Velvet Revolution; Václav Havel becomes president that December. The state is renamed "Czechoslovak Federative Republic" in March 1990, then hyphenated to "Czech and Slovak Federative Republic" a month later to satisfy Slovak objections to the first version — the so-called "Hyphen War." It dissolves on 31 December 1992.

What it represents: the flag itself never changes through any of this — only the state's name and constitution do, twice in six months. Free elections in June 1990 confirm the transition; Czech and Slovak nationalist parties spend the next two years negotiating, then agreeing to, separation.

1948 – 1989

Czechoslovak Socialist Republic

Communist state

Who & why: the Communist Party seizes full control in the February 1948 coup, ending the fragile postwar coalition. The country is formally renamed "Czechoslovak Socialist Republic" under the 1960 constitution; the 1968 "Prague Spring" reforms under Alexander Dubček are crushed by a Warsaw Pact invasion that August, opening two decades of harsh "normalization" that only ends with 1989's Velvet Revolution.

What it represents: unlike Hungary or Poland, Communist Czechoslovakia never added a party emblem to its flag — the 1920 white-red-blue design continues unaltered, with socialist symbolism confined to the separate state coat of arms and a distinct presidential/military flag rather than the national flag itself.

1945 – 1948

Third Czechoslovak Republic

Republic (restored)

Who & why: the Prague Uprising and Soviet liberation in early May 1945 end Nazi occupation; the reconstituted state expels most of its ethnic German population (the Beneš decrees) amid widespread postwar violence, while a "National Front" coalition including the Communist Party governs under President Edvard Beneš. The Communists' rigged consolidation of power in February 1948 ends this brief democratic interlude.

What it represents: the full 1920 Czechoslovak flag returns exactly as before Munich — a deliberate assertion of legal continuity with the pre-1938 republic, treating the Nazi occupation years as an illegal interruption rather than a genuine change of state.

1939 – 1945

Protectorate of Bohemia and Moravia

Fascist / puppet state

Who & why: German troops occupy the Czech rump state on 15–16 March 1939, the day after Slovakia declares its own separate, German-aligned state; Hitler proclaims the Protectorate the following day. Nominal Czech administration under State President Emil Hácha continues, but Reich Protector Konstantin von Neurath (later the far harsher Reinhard Heydrich) holds real power; deportations to camps and the 1942 destruction of Lidice in reprisal for Heydrich's assassination mark the era's worst atrocities.

What it represents: the blue wedge is dropped, since Berlin considered it a symbol of the now-separated Slovak lands, leaving the plain white-over-red bicolor — itself the ancient heraldic colors of Bohemia, flown here as an officially permitted regional emblem subordinate to the swastika flag that flew above it on every public building.

1938 – 1939

Second Czecho-Slovak Republic

Republic (rump)

Who & why: the 30 September 1938 Munich Agreement, signed by Britain, France, Germany, and Italy without a Czechoslovak representative present, forces the cession of the German-majority Sudetenland; Poland and Hungary seize further border territory within weeks. The diminished, newly-hyphenated "Czecho-Slovakia" grants Slovakia and Ruthenia broad autonomy and drifts toward authoritarian, German-dictated policy until the state collapses entirely in mid-March 1939.

What it represents: the flag itself is unchanged from the First Republic — a state stripped of roughly a third of its population and most of its border fortifications within weeks, still flying the same banner as a fading claim to the republic it used to be.

1918 – 1938

First Czechoslovak Republic

Republic

Who & why: Tomáš Garrigue Masaryk proclaims independence on 28 October 1918 as Austria-Hungary disintegrates, joining Czech and Slovak lands (plus Carpathian Ruthenia) into a single new state for the first time. Interwar Czechoslovakia becomes Central Europe's most durable democracy, with a diversified industrial economy inherited largely intact from the old Austrian half of the empire.

What it represents: the historic Bohemian white-over-red bicolor is adopted in 1918, but a blue wedge is added from the hoist in March 1920 specifically to distinguish it from the newly independent Poland's identical plain white-over-red flag — blue chosen partly to represent Slovakia and Ruthenia, completing a tricolor read as red-white-blue, the pan-Slavic colors.

1526 – 1918

Kingdom of Bohemia (Habsburg-ruled)

Kingdom (Habsburg crown)

Who & why: after King Louis Jagiellon drowns at Mohács in 1526, the Bohemian estates elect Ferdinand of Habsburg — the same year Ferdinand also becomes King of Hungary. Bohemia keeps its own crown, diet, and chancellery in personal union with Vienna until the 1620 Battle of White Mountain, after which the Habsburgs impose direct rule, mass re-Catholicization, and German as the dominant administrative language; Bohemia becomes a crownland of the Austrian Empire from 1804 and of Austria-Hungary's Cisleithanian half from 1867.

What it represents: the lion banner continues essentially unchanged across four centuries of foreign dynastic rule — the same silver two-tailed lion, now flown as one crownland's heraldic banner among several in a much larger multinational empire rather than as an independent kingdom's own flag.

1198 – 1526

Kingdom of Bohemia (independent)

Kingdom

Who & why: Duke Přemysl Otakar I secures a hereditary royal title from Philip of Swabia in 1198, permanently confirmed by Emperor Frederick II's 1212 Golden Bull of Sicily, which also guarantees the Bohemian nobility's right to elect the Holy Roman Emperor. The kingdom flourishes under Charles IV (r. 1346–78, also Holy Roman Emperor, who founds Prague's university and much of its Gothic core) before ending at the 1526 Battle of Mohács, where the Jagiellonian king Louis dies without an heir.

What it represents: the same silver lion, now a second tail added under later Přemyslid kings — tradition credits the change to Přemysl Otakar II's 13th-century ambitions, a small heraldic flourish marking the shift from ducal banner to a fully royal one.

1158 – 1198

Duchy of Bohemia (heraldic era)

Duchy

Who & why: Duke Vladislaus II receives a personal, non-hereditary royal title from Emperor Frederick Barbarossa in 1158 for military support during an Italian campaign; the title lapses back to duke on his death, but not before it establishes the silver lion as the dynasty's own emblem. His son Přemysl Otakar I later converts the honor into the permanent hereditary kingship of 1198.

What it represents: the single-tailed silver lion on red, among the oldest heraldic devices still in continuous use anywhere in Europe — attested on ducal seals from this period onward, the same beast that (with a second tail added later) remains part of the Czech state coat of arms today.

c. 895 – 1158

Duchy of Bohemia (early Přemyslid)

Duchy

Who & why: the Přemyslid dynasty, already ruling in Prague from around 870, gains full independence as the Moravian state that had overshadowed it collapses under Magyar pressure c. 895–907. Duke Wenceslaus I (later Saint Wenceslaus, martyred c. 935, patron saint of the Czech lands ever since) accepts Christianity and East Frankish overlordship; his grandson Boleslaus II completes the conversion of the surrounding countryside through the later 10th century.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from this pre-heraldic era, more than two centuries before European heraldry itself develops — the Přemyslids used dynastic and religious symbols (an eagle, later the figure of St. Wenceslaus himself) on coinage and seals, but no fixed territorial banner. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
