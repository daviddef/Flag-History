Flags of the Carpathians and the Danube

From two separate medieval principalities paying tribute to the Ottoman Sultan, through a fifteen-year personal union that quietly became one country, a kingdom that tripled in size overnight in 1918, a five-year wartime whiplash between fascist dictatorship and Soviet occupation, and forty-two years of one-party rule — eleven political entities, and a blue-yellow-red tricolor whose three colors are literally the two principalities' own old colors laid side by side.

Wallachia's traditional color was yellow, under a black eagle; Moldavia's was a blood-red field under a black aurochs' head. When the two united in 1859, their flags fused into a single vertical tricolor — the same blue, yellow, and red that (with the state emblem removed) is still Romania's flag today.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Romania, c. 1330–present, eleven political entities across two parallel medieval tracks that converge in 1859. Part of a larger atlas — see the map and linked pages at the bottom.

Two principalities and a borrowed province: 1541–1918

Modern Romania's own real border, split into the three lands that took nearly four centuries to become one country: Wallachia in the south, Moldavia in the east, and Transylvania — inside the Carpathian arc, ruled from Budapest and Vienna for the whole of this window — joining only in 1918. Wallachia and Moldavia united in 1859 without a single shot fired; Transylvania waited another fifty-nine years and a world war. Alexandru Ioan Cuza was elected ruler of both principalities within nineteen days of each other in January 1859 — a legal loophole in the same double election nobody had explicitly forbidden. Transylvania, ruled from outside the whole time, only joined by a vote of its own assembly at Alba Iulia in December 1918. The lines below solidify only where the pieces were genuinely ruled apart, and they dissolve for good, one at a time, exactly when each union became real.

1989 – present

Romania (post-Communist republic)

Sovereign stateIon IliescuSecular (Orthodox pre-eminence)

Who & why: a popular uprising beginning in Timișoara on 16 December 1989 topples Nicolae Ceaușescu within days; he and his wife Elena are tried and executed on Christmas Day. A new constitution is adopted by referendum in December 1991, and Romania joins NATO in 2004 and the EU in 2007.

What it represents: the Communist state's coat of arms is cut out of the tricolor's center within hours of the revolution starting — protesters carried flags with a literal hole in them — leaving the plain blue-yellow-red field, legally readopted on 27 December 1989 and unchanged since.

1947 – 1989

People's / Socialist Republic of Romania

Communist stateGheorghe Gheorghiu-DejState atheism

Who & why: with the Red Army in occupation and King Michael I forced to abdicate on 30 December 1947, the Communist Party proclaims the People's Republic the same day, completing a takeover begun with the rigged November 1946 election. The state is renamed the Socialist Republic of Romania under a new constitution in 1965, the year Nicolae Ceaușescu becomes party leader; his increasingly erratic, nationalist one-man rule dominates the following quarter-century until the 1989 revolution.

What it represents: the tricolor is kept, but a state emblem (oil derricks, wheat sheaves, forested mountains, a red star) is added at center in 1948 and redesigned again in 1965 — the same year the coat of arms formally replaces the royal one still lingering in official use, and the country's name changes twice without the flag's three colors ever being questioned.

1944 – 1947

Kingdom of Romania (Soviet occupation)

Occupied / transitionalKing Michael IRomanian Orthodox (state-favored)

Who & why: on 23 August 1944, 23-year-old King Michael I personally leads a coup that arrests Ion Antonescu and switches Romania to the Allied side overnight — a move that shortens the war but does not spare the country a Soviet occupation. A Soviet-installed government under Petru Groza takes power in March 1945; the rigged election of November 1946 eliminates any real opposition, and Michael is forced to abdicate at gunpoint on 30 December 1947.

What it represents: the same royal tricolor continues to fly — the monarchy's formal continuity is the last fig leaf over a country already functionally run from Moscow, the flag itself the one thing that does not yet register how completely power has already changed hands.

1940 – 1944

Kingdom of Romania (National Legionary State / Antonescu dictatorship)

Fascist / Axis-alignedIon AntonescuRomanian Orthodox (state-favored)

Who & why: the August 1940 Second Vienna Award strips Northern Transylvania away to Hungary; King Carol II abdicates in September under the pressure, and General Ion Antonescu proclaims the National Legionary State in partnership with the fascist Iron Guard. A Guard-led rebellion and pogrom in January 1941 is crushed by Antonescu's own army, and he rules alone as "Conducător" from then on, joining the Axis invasion of the Soviet Union in June 1941 before being arrested in the King's own coup in August 1944.

What it represents: the royal tricolor flies throughout, unaltered by either the Iron Guard's brief partnership in government or Antonescu's subsequent one-man rule — the Guard's own cross-and-eagle emblem stayed a party symbol, never replacing the state flag itself.

1918 – 1940

Kingdom of Romania (Greater Romania)

KingdomKing Ferdinand IRomanian Orthodox (state-favored)

Who & why: as Austria-Hungary collapses at the end of World War I, an assembly of Transylvanian Romanians votes union with the Kingdom at Alba Iulia on 1 December 1918, following Bessarabia's own union in March and Bukovina's in November — Romania's territory and population roughly double. 1881 – 1918

Kingdom of Romania

KingdomKing Carol IRomanian Orthodox (state-favored)

Who & why: full independence from the Ottoman Empire, won on the battlefield in the 1877–78 Russo-Turkish War, is internationally recognized at the Congress of Berlin in 1878; Prince Carol of Hohenzollern-Sigmaringen, ruling as domnitor since 1866, is crowned King Carol I on 10 May 1881. Romania stays neutral for the first two years of World War I before entering on the Allied side in 1916, suffering a near-total occupation before the war's final turn.

What it represents: the same blue-yellow-red tricolor inherited from the 1859 union now carries a royal crown and the Hohenzollern dynasty's own arms on the state version — the flag's colors stay the united principalities' own, even as the crown above them is newly, deliberately foreign.

1859 – 1881

United Principalities of Moldavia and Wallachia

Personal unionAlexandru Ioan CuzaRomanian Orthodox Christianity

Who & why: Colonel Alexandru Ioan Cuza is elected ruler (domnitor) of Moldavia on 5 January 1859 and, through a legal loophole nobody had closed, of Wallachia too on 24 January — the same man ruling both thrones creates a de facto union the Great Powers had not explicitly authorized but chose not to unwind. Cuza's sweeping land reforms provoke the boyars, who force his abdication in 1866; a German prince, Carol of Hohenzollern-Sigmaringen, is invited to take his place the same year.

What it represents: a new vertical tricolor, formally adopted in 1861–62, fuses Wallachia's blue and yellow with Moldavia's red — the same three colors, in the same order, that Romania still flies today — with the two principalities' own arms (eagle, aurochs head) combined on a single shield.

1600 – 1859

Principality of Wallachia (Ottoman vassal)

Ottoman vassal principalityMichael the Brave (Mihai Viteazul)Romanian Orthodox Christianity

Who & why: Wallachia pays tribute to the Ottoman Sultan while keeping its own princes, courts, and church, autonomy that narrows sharply once the Sultan begins appointing Greek Phanariote princes directly from Constantinople from 1715 on, replacing the native boyar dynasties. The Treaty of Adrianople (1829) restores native princes and opens the principality to Russian-backed reform, setting up the 1859 union.

What it represents: the black eagle on a gold field, holding a cross in its beak, is attested on Wallachian princely flags and seals from the 17th and 18th centuries onward — one of the two heraldic devices, alongside Moldavia's aurochs head, that fuse onto the united principalities' shield in 1859.

1600 – 1859

Principality of Moldavia (Ottoman vassal)

Ottoman vassal principalityIeremia MovilăRomanian Orthodox Christianity

Who & why: Moldavia keeps its own princes and institutions under Ottoman suzerainty on much the same terms as Wallachia, likewise losing effective self-rule to appointed Phanariote princes after 1711. Russian influence grows through the 18th and early 19th centuries, and Moldavia loses its eastern half, Bessarabia, to Russia outright in 1812 — territory Romania will spend the next two centuries intermittently regaining and losing again.

What it represents: the black aurochs' head with a star between its horns, a rose to one side and a crescent moon to the other, on a red field — Moldavia's own princely arms, attested from at least the 15th century and still, today, the base of several Romanian county and city emblems in the historic Moldavian lands.

c. 1330 – 1600

Principality of Wallachia (founding era)

Founding principalityBasarab IRomanian Orthodox Christianity

Who & why: Basarab I defeats an invading Hungarian royal army at the Battle of Posada in 1330, securing effective independence for the young principality he founded south of the Carpathians; his name gives the ruling Basarab dynasty, and eventually the modern region of Basarabia, its own. Wallachia's most famous ruler of this era, Vlad III "the Impaler" (Dracula), reigns intermittently in the 1450s–70s, waging brutal resistance campaigns against Ottoman expansion. The principality's most striking later moment comes in 1600, when Prince Michael the Brave briefly rules Wallachia, Moldavia, and Transylvania together — the first time in history one man held all three, though the union collapses within a year of his 1601 assassination.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from Wallachia's first two and a half centuries; princely seals of the era show a version of the black eagle later fixed on the gold field shown two cards down, but not reliably as a flag. Shown here honestly blank rather than projecting the later, better-attested design backward.

Principality of Moldavia (founding era)

Founding principalityBogdan IRomanian Orthodox Christianity

Who & why: Dragoș crosses the eastern Carpathians as a Hungarian vassal around 1350–59 to found a march against Tatar raids; his successor Bogdan I breaks from Hungary around 1363 to found Moldavia as a fully independent principality in its own right. Stephen the Great (r. 1457–1504) becomes the era's dominant figure, winning dozens of battles against Ottoman, Hungarian, and Polish armies alike and building dozens of churches and monasteries, several now UNESCO World Heritage sites, to commemorate his victories.

What it represents: as with Wallachia, no flag is securely documented for Moldavia's own founding centuries, though the aurochs-head device that later becomes its fixed emblem already appears on princely seals under Stephen the Great. Shown here honestly blank rather than borrowing the later, better-attested flag shown two cards up.

Reading the tags

Principality / kingdom — medieval or dynastic polity
Occupied / transitional — sovereignty contested or foreign-controlled
Republic — democratic, sovereign or contested
Communist state — one-party, Soviet-aligned
Fascist / Axis-aligned — wartime military dictatorship
Leader — the named ruler most associated with the era's start
