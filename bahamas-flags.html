Flags of the Bahamas

A traditional first landfall of Columbus in 1492, followed by a Spanish slave-raiding campaign so thorough that the islands sat nearly empty for over a century before anyone resettled them. English planters and adventurers arrive from the 1640s, Nassau curdles into a pirate haven by the early 1700s, and a royal governor sent to stamp that out in 1718 opens two and a half centuries of unbroken British Crown rule — ending only with independence in 1973.

Three flags for three very different kinds of sovereignty: an indigenous society that left no banner behind, a colony that flew Britain's ensign for longer than most of its Caribbean neighbors combined, and a modern nation whose aquamarine and gold are meant to be read, quite literally, as sea, sand, and sun.

Independent Commonwealth of the Bahamas

Independent state

Who & why: internal self-government arrives with a new 1964 constitution, and in 1969 the territory formally renames itself from the "Colony of the Bahama Islands" to simply "The Bahamas." Full independence within the Commonwealth follows on 10 July 1973, marked with the lowering of the Union Flag and the raising of a newly designed national flag over Clifford Park in Nassau.

What it represents: the two aquamarine bands stand for the sea surrounding the islands, the gold band for the sun and the sand, and the black hoist triangle for the unity and strength of the Bahamian people drawing energy from land and sea alike.

British Bahamas: from the Pirate Republic to Crown Colony

British colony

Who & why: English settlement begins with the Eleutheran Adventurers in 1648 and a second wave to New Providence in 1666, but by the early 1700s a governance vacuum turns Nassau into the "Republic of Pirates," sheltering Blackbeard and dozens of other captains. The Crown responds by commissioning Woodes Rogers as the first royal governor; he arrives in 1718 under the motto Expulsis Piratis — Restituta Commercia ("Pirates expelled, commerce restored") and restores royal administration. The Bahamas remain a British Crown Colony continuously from that point until 1973.

What it represents: the Blue Ensign defaced with a colonial badge, formalized in the 19th and early 20th centuries, depicting a sailing ship approaching an island under a rising sun — a common pattern across the British Caribbean, with the badge alone distinguishing one colony's ensign from another's.

Lucayan Taíno Bahamas and early depopulation

Indigenous / unsettled

Who & why: San Salvador, in the southern Bahamas, is traditionally identified as Christopher Columbus's first landfall in the Americas on 12 October 1492, where he encountered the islands' Lucayan (Taíno) inhabitants. Spain never effectively settled the Bahamas, but its raiders did: within a few decades the Lucayan population was almost entirely wiped out by slave raiding — forced removal to work the gold mines and pearl fisheries of Hispaniola and Cuba — compounded by imported disease. The islands were left nearly depopulated for over a century, resettled only from 1648 by the English, whose loosely governed Nassau curdled by the early 1700s into the notorious "Republic of Pirates" that the Crown finally brought to heel in 1718.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from Lucayan society, nor from the loosely governed pirate period that followed English settlement; both eras predate or fall outside any fixed heraldic tradition for these islands. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
