Flags of the Netherlands

From a patchwork of feudal counties with no shared banner, through a Burgundian-Habsburg union, an eighty-year war of secession, a genuine Golden Age republic, three distinct Napoleonic-era governments in under twenty years, a treaty-built kingdom that lost its southern half within fifteen years, and a five-year wartime occupation — ten political entities, and a red-white-blue tricolor that keeps reappearing, sometimes orange, on the other side of every rupture.

The Dutch tricolor is older than it looks: it started life orange-white-blue (the "Prince's Flag," after William of Orange) during the Revolt, and had faded — partly by dye chemistry, partly by politics — to today's red by the mid-17th century, well before the French Revolution gave most of Europe the idea of a national tricolor at all.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Netherlands, c. 1000–present, ten political entities. Belgium and Luxembourg — part of the same Burgundian-Habsburg bloc until 1581, and reunited with the Netherlands twice more after that — have their own pages; see the shared map and the linked pages at the bottom.

One country, nine changes of flag

Modern Netherlands' own territory, unlike Belgium next door, was never durably split along an internal line after 1581 — the Union of Utrecht's seven founding provinces and the later "Generality Lands" are a real distinction, but a fiddly and largely administrative one that never produced a solidified internal border worth drawing on a map. 1945 – present

Kingdom of the Netherlands

Sovereign kingdom

Who & why: liberation reaches the last occupied Dutch territory in May 1945, restoring the constitutional monarchy under Queen Wilhelmina. The postwar decades bring the loss of Indonesia after a bitter independence war (1945–49), founding membership of NATO (1949) and the European project from the 1950s onward, and Suriname's independence in 1975 — the kingdom's overseas map shrinking even as its European core prospers.

What it represents: the same red-white-blue tricolor flown since the Republic, its exact shade finally standardized by royal decree in 1937 after centuries of the red band drifting between orange-red and true red depending on dye and fashion.

1940 – 1945

Reichskommissariat Niederlande (German occupation)

Occupied territory

Who & why: Germany invades on 10 May 1940 without a declaration of war; the bombing of Rotterdam on 14 May forces capitulation the next day. Queen Wilhelmina and the government flee to London, while Arthur Seyss-Inquart governs as Reichskommissar. Persecution of the Dutch Jewish population escalates through deportations from 1942; a national railway strike in support of the Allied advance in 1944–45 triggers a German transport blockade and the "Hunger Winter," which kills roughly 20,000 civilians.

What it represents: the Dutch tricolor was never formally abolished but its public display was steadily restricted and finally banned outright in 1943; German state buildings and the occupation authority instead flew the flag of Nazi Germany, shown here schematically as the ruling power's own banner rather than any Dutch flag.

1830 – 1940

Kingdom of the Netherlands (post-secession)

Sovereign kingdom

Who & why: Belgium's August 1830 revolution and the failed Ten Days' Campaign of 1831 cost the kingdom its southern provinces; the 1839 Treaty of London formalizes the new borders, including Luxembourg's partition (see the Low Countries hub). William I abdicates in 1840; his successors preside over a slow shift from royal authority toward parliamentary government — the constitution of 1848 curbs the crown's power after a wave of European revolutions — and the country stays neutral through the First World War.

What it represents: no change to the flag at all; the same tricolor that flew over the larger union keeps flying over the smaller kingdom that remains, the clearest visual sign that the Dutch state itself considered 1830 a loss of territory rather than a rupture in its own continuity.

1813 – 1830

United Kingdom of the Netherlands

Sovereign kingdom

Who & why: as French power collapses, William VI of Orange returns from exile in England and is proclaimed "Sovereign Prince" on 1 December 1813. The Congress of Vienna then builds a stronger buffer state against France: on its terms, William is crowned King William I in March 1815 and formally merges his realm with the former Austrian Netherlands (Belgium) and Luxembourg that June, creating a single kingdom across all three — cross-reference the Low Countries hub for the full three-country picture.

What it represents: the historic Dutch tricolor, unchanged through the transition from French rule to independent principality to enlarged kingdom — the flag's continuity masking a genuinely new and much larger state underneath it.

1810 – 1813

French Empire (direct annexation)

Annexed territory

Who & why: frustrated that his brother Louis was protecting Dutch trade from the Continental System's blockade of Britain, Napoleon forces Louis's abdication and annexes the former Kingdom of Holland directly into the French Empire on 9 July 1810, dividing it into French départements (Zuiderzee, Bouches-de-l'Yssel, Frise, and others). Direct rule ends when Cossack cavalry, part of the wider collapse following Napoleon's Russian campaign, reach the country in November 1813 and the population rises against the French garrison.

What it represents: no separate Dutch flag at all for these three years — the French tricolor itself, flown as it was over every other department of the Empire, the only period in this whole timeline when the Netherlands has no flag of its own whatsoever.

1806 – 1810

Kingdom of Holland

Client kingdom

Who & why: Napoleon installs his younger brother Louis Bonaparte as king on 5 June 1806, hoping for a more pliable and useful satellite than the fractious Batavian Commonwealth had proven. Louis surprises Napoleon by governing in genuinely Dutch interests — learning the language, moving the capital to Amsterdam, and resisting enforcement of the Continental System against Dutch merchants — earning his own brother's growing fury and eventual removal.

What it represents: Louis kept the existing red-white-blue tricolor as the kingdom's flag rather than imposing a new French-style design, a deliberate choice to present his rule as continuous with Dutch tradition rather than as a foreign imposition.

1795 – 1806

Batavian Republic

Republic

Who & why: French Revolutionary troops cross the frozen rivers in January 1795, and Stadtholder William V flees to England as the Patriot movement, radicalized allies of the French, proclaims the Batavian Republic on 19 January 1795. A 1798 constitution creates a unitary state for the first time, ending the old provincial confederation; internal factional strife eventually leads Napoleon to reorganize the government into the more centralized Batavian Commonwealth under Grand Pensionary Rutger Jan Schimmelpenninck from 1805.

What it represents: the republic kept the historic red-white-blue tricolor to claim continuity with the Dutch nation, briefly adding a small canton with a liberty cap and pole in 1795–96 to signal its revolutionary, French-aligned politics before dropping the addition and reverting to the plain tricolor.

1581 – 1795

Dutch Republic (United Provinces)

Republic

Who & why: the northern provinces sign the Union of Utrecht on 23 January 1579 as a mutual-defense pact against Spain, then go further with the Act of Abjuration on 26 July 1581, formally renouncing Philip II. Spain doesn't formally recognize the loss until the Treaty of Münster, part of the Peace of Westphalia, on 30 January 1648. In between comes the Dutch Golden Age — the VOC founded in 1602, colonial and trading footholds from Batavia to New Amsterdam to Nagasaki, and the art of Rembrandt and Vermeer — alongside real internal strain, including a First Stadtholderless Period (1650–72) and the catastrophic French invasion of the 1672 "Rampjaar."

What it represents: the Revolt's own banner started as the orange-white-blue "Prince's Flag," after William of Orange; by the mid-17th century it had shifted to red-white-blue, partly because orange dye faded unreliably and partly because red suited a republic wary of the House of Orange's own princely ambitions during the stadtholderless years. The red version, the Statenvlag, became the settled national flag for the rest of the Republic's existence.

1433 – 1581

Burgundian / Habsburg Netherlands

Personal union / duchy

Who & why: Philip the Good, Duke of Burgundy, secures Holland, Zeeland, and Hainaut in 1433 after a long conflict with his cousin Jacqueline of Bavaria, folding them into the wider Burgundian bloc that already included Flanders and Brabant (see the Low Countries hub). The line passes to the Habsburgs on Mary of Burgundy's marriage in 1477; her grandson Charles V, born in Ghent in 1500, adds Guelders, Utrecht, Overijssel, Groningen, and Frisia by the 1520s–40s, then formalizes the whole "Seventeen Provinces" as one indivisible inheritance in the 1549 Pragmatic Sanction. His son Philip II's heavy taxation and religious persecution provoke the revolt that ends this era in 1581.

What it represents: the Burgundy Cross — a red diagonal saltire of knotted, raguly branches on white, the personal emblem of the Burgundian dukes — used across the whole Burgundian-Habsburg realm rather than as a flag specific to the northern provinces alone; the same design that continues over the southern Netherlands (future Belgium) after 1581.

c. 1000 – 1433

Fragmented medieval counties

Feudal patchwork

Who & why: what is now the Netherlands was a patchwork of separate feudal territories with no shared ruler: the County of Holland (formally named from 1101), the County of Zeeland, the prince-bishopric of Utrecht, the Duchy of Guelders, and the fiercely independent Frisian lands, which recognized no feudal overlord at all under the tradition of "Frisian freedom" until a Saxon conquest in 1498. Each had its own count, bishop, or assembly, its own coinage, and its own local banners.

What it represents: no single flag existed for this territory because no single polity did; each county or lordship used its own heraldic devices rather than a shared national symbol. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented, ending when Philip the Good began assembling Holland, Zeeland, and Hainaut under one Burgundian hand in 1433.
