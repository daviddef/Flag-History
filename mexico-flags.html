Flags of Mexico

Mexico's flag lineage begins with a founding legend that still centers the modern flag: the Mexica (Aztec) people, told by their god to build a city wherever they saw an eagle perched on a cactus devouring a serpent, found that sign on a lake island and built Tenochtitlan there in 1325 — the same eagle-and-cactus emblem anchors Mexico's tricolor today. From that empire's 1521 fall through three centuries as New Spain, two short-lived empires, a war that cost half the national territory, and the 1910–20 Revolution, this page traces seven eras.

The 1846–48 Mexican-American War, triggered by a border dispute following Texas's earlier secession, ends with the 1848 Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, in which Mexico cedes roughly half its national territory — including present-day California, Nevada, Utah, and most of Arizona and New Mexico — to the United States.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Mexico, pre-1519–present, seven political entities. No interactive map on this page — that layer is deferred project-wide to a future phase. Part of a larger atlas — see the linked pages at the bottom.

1917 – present

United Mexican States (modern)

Republic, independent

Who & why: the Mexican Revolution (1910–1920), a sweeping and costly conflict against Porfirio Díaz's long dictatorship and its successors, produces the progressive 1917 Constitution that still governs Mexico today. The eagle emblem is refined again in 1968, ahead of the Mexico City Olympics, into its current, more naturalistic form.

What it represents: green (independence), white (the Catholic religion, historically; today unity), and red (the union of European and Indigenous peoples, historically; today the martyrs' blood) frame the founding eagle-and-cactus-and-serpent emblem from the Mexica migration legend.

1867 – 1917

Restored Republic, the Porfiriato & the Revolution

Republic, independent

Who & why: Benito Juárez restores the republic after Maximilian's execution in 1867, followed by Porfirio Díaz's long, modernizing but increasingly authoritarian rule (the "Porfiriato," 1876–1911). Díaz's fall triggers the Mexican Revolution (1910–1920), a prolonged and often violent struggle involving figures like Francisco Madero, Pancho Villa, and Emiliano Zapata that reshapes Mexican society and eventually produces the 1917 Constitution.

What it represents: the tricolor continues through this turbulent half-century with only minor emblem revisions, shown here as one bridging entry across a genuinely fast-changing and consequential period.

1864 – 1867

Second Mexican Empire

Foreign-imposed empire

Who & why: France, seeking to collect Mexican debts and exploit its own internal Liberal-Conservative conflict, invades in 1862 (defeated at first at the Battle of Puebla, 5 May, now Cinco de Mayo) and installs Austrian Archduke Maximilian as emperor in 1864. President Benito Juárez's republican government never surrenders, waging continued resistance until French troops withdraw and Maximilian is captured and executed by firing squad in 1867.

What it represents: the same green-white-red tricolor, with four golden eagles added to the corners marking Maximilian's imperial (rather than republican) government.

1823 – 1864

First Federal & Centralist Republics

Republic, independent

Who & why: a republic replaces Iturbide's short-lived empire in 1823, but the decades that follow are genuinely turbulent: repeated swings between federalist and centralist constitutions, Texas's 1836 secession, and the disastrous 1846–48 Mexican-American War, which ends with Mexico ceding roughly half its national territory to the United States under the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo.

What it represents: the same tricolor structure as the empire, with the crown removed from atop the eagle to mark republican rather than monarchical government, shown here as one bridging entry across a genuinely fast-changing 41 years.

1821 – 1823

First Mexican Empire

Empire, independent

Who & why: Mexico wins independence from Spain in September 1821 after an 11-year war beginning with Miguel Hidalgo's 1810 revolt; Agustín de Iturbide, architect of the final independence pact, has himself proclaimed emperor in 1822, but growing republican opposition forces his abdication within less than two years.

What it represents: the first Mexican tricolor, designed under the 1821 Plan of Iguala — green, white, and red for independence, religion, and unity — centered on a crowned eagle perched on a cactus, referencing the Mexica founding legend of Tenochtitlan.

1521 – 1821

Viceroyalty of New Spain

Colonial rule

Who & why: Hernán Cortés, aided by Indigenous allies resentful of Aztec rule and by a devastating smallpox epidemic, completes the conquest of Tenochtitlan in August 1521, ending the Aztec Empire and founding Mexico City on its ruins. Nearly three centuries of Spanish colonial rule follow as the Viceroyalty of New Spain, marked by the fusion (and frequently forced imposition) of Spanish, Catholic, and Indigenous cultures, and by a war of independence that begins with Miguel Hidalgo's 1810 Grito de Dolores.

What it represents: the Cross of Burgundy (a red saltire on white), the common flag of the Spanish Empire's overseas viceroyalties, flown across New Spain's territory for most of this colonial period.

1325 – 1521

Aztec (Mexica) Empire

Empire, independent

Who & why: the Mexica people found Tenochtitlan in 1325 on a lake island, guided (per their founding legend) by the sight of an eagle devouring a serpent atop a cactus — the same emblem still on Mexico's flag today. By the early 1500s Tenochtitlan is one of the largest cities on Earth, anchoring an empire covering much of central Mexico, until Hernán Cortés's Spanish-led conquest (aided by Indigenous rivals of the Aztecs and by smallpox) topples it in 1519–1521.

What it represents: no flag in the European heraldic sense existed in Mesoamerica; Aztec military and religious standards (often feathered devices carried on the back) served a related signaling purpose. Shown here as an informed reconstruction of the eagle-cactus-serpent founding emblem on a plain field, not a documented banner, since the true device would not have taken this rectangular flag form.
