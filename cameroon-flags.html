Flags of Cameroon

Cameroon's colonial history is unusually divided even by the standards of this atlas: a single German protectorate, seized by Anglo-French forces in the First World War, was carved into two separate mandates — one French, one British — that were administered under entirely different colonial systems for four decades before reuniting into one country in 1961. That split is not a historical footnote; it is the direct reason Cameroon still has two official languages today.

From the Bamum and Duala kingdoms of the pre-colonial era, through German Kamerun, the Franco-British split, reunification, and a shift from federal to unitary statehood, this page traces five flags across roughly a century and a half.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Cameroon, from its pre-colonial kingdoms through the German protectorate, the French/British mandate split, and reunification to the modern republic. 1975 – present

Republic of Cameroon (unitary state)

Republic, unitaryAhmadou AhidjoSecular (no state religion)

Who & why: a 1972 referendum abolishes the federal structure, renaming the country the United Republic of Cameroon under President Ahmadou Ahidjo; it is renamed again, simply the Republic of Cameroon, in 1984 under Paul Biya, who has governed continuously since 1982. The move from federal to unitary government is itself contested by many Anglophone Cameroonians, and tension over the country's English-speaking regions' autonomy continues into the present day.

What it represents: the green-red-yellow vertical tricolor keeps its pan-African colors, but the two stars of the federal era are replaced in 1975 by a single central gold star symbolizing national unity rather than the federation's two constituent states.

1961 – 1975

Federal Republic of Cameroon

Federal republicAhmadou AhidjoSecular (no state religion)

Who & why: a February 1961 United Nations plebiscite splits British Cameroons: the southern zone votes to join independent French Cameroun, while British Northern Cameroons votes to join Nigeria instead. On 1 October 1961, Southern Cameroons formally joins to form the Federal Republic of Cameroon, with East Cameroon (the former French territory) and West Cameroon (the former Southern British Cameroons) as its two federated states — the direct origin of the country's Anglophone-Francophone divide that persists today.

What it represents: the same green-red-yellow tricolor gains a second gold star, one for each of the two federated states joined in reunification.

1957 – 1961

Republic of Cameroon (French trusteeship & independence)

Republic, independentAndré-Marie MbidaRoman Catholic (favored, not established)

Who & why: French Cameroun adopts self-government within the French Union in 1957 and this flag with it; full independence follows on 1 January 1960 under President Ahmadou Ahidjo, making it one of the earliest sub-Saharan African states to become independent. The republic's leadership immediately begins working toward reunification with the neighboring British-administered territory.

What it represents: the green-red-yellow vertical tricolor uses the pan-African colors associated with Ethiopian independence, chosen as a plain, starless banner for the new republic ahead of full sovereignty.

1916 – 1957

French Cameroun & British Cameroons (split mandate/trusteeship)

Colony (mandate/trusteeship)No single ruler documentedMixed Christian missions (no formal establishment)

Who & why: Anglo-French forces conquer German Kamerun during the First World War, and by 1916 the territory is split between France (roughly the larger eastern and central portion, French Cameroun) and Britain (two narrow western strips, administered as British Cameroons alongside neighboring Nigeria). Both become League of Nations mandates in 1922 and United Nations trust territories after 1946, but under entirely separate administrations, laws, and languages — the direct origin of Cameroon's modern Anglophone-Francophone divide.

What it represents: France flies its own Tricolor over French Cameroun throughout this period; Britain administers its two strips as part of Nigeria and flies British colonial ensigns there rather than any Cameroon-specific flag. No unified territorial flag exists for this split era.

1884 – 1916

German Kamerun

ColonyGustav NachtigalNo official state religion

Who & why: German trader Gustav Nachtigal signs treaties with Duala chiefs in July 1884, establishing the protectorate of Kamerun. German rule is marked by harsh forced-labor concession policies in the interior before the colony is lost during the First World War, occupied by invading Anglo-French forces by 1916 and formally stripped from Germany at Versailles in 1919.

What it represents: the black-white-red horizontal tricolor of the German Empire's civil ensign, flown as the colonial flag across all German protectorates including Kamerun; a colonial variant bore the imperial eagle and Kamerun's own badge in official use, simplified here to the plain tricolor field.

c. 1394 – 1884

Bamum, Duala, and other pre-colonial kingdoms

Pre-colonial kingdomsNchare Yen (Bamum founder)Traditional/ancestral religion

Who & why: the territory that becomes Cameroon holds many independent polities before European contact, most notably the Bamum Kingdom in the western grassfields (founded around 1394, and still existing today as a traditional monarchy with its own written script, invented by Sultan Njoya around 1896) and the Duala chiefdoms of the coast, who become key trading intermediaries with Portuguese, and later German, merchants from the 15th century onward.

What it represents: no single flag unified this diverse pre-colonial landscape; the Bamum Kingdom itself used royal regalia and, from Njoya's reign, its own script and symbols, but no securely documented national-level flag survives for the region as a whole. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
