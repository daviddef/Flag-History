Flags of Saint Lucia

No island in the Caribbean was fought over more than Saint Lucia. Control passed between Britain and France roughly fourteen separate times across the 17th and 18th centuries — more changes of hands than any other Caribbean territory — as the two empires' wars in Europe kept spilling over onto this one island and its excellent natural harbor at Castries.

That back-and-forth finally ended in 1814, when the island settled for good as a British colony, and it stayed that way until independence in 1979. Four flags cover the whole span: a long stretch of sustained Kalinago control that outlasted most other islands' indigenous resistance, the messy Anglo-French contest itself, final British rule, and the modern flag's stylized Pitons.

Scope of this page: the island of Saint Lucia, c. 1500–present, four entries, no interactive map. This atlas normally maps internal partitions or multi-power divisions with a dedicated visualization; Saint Lucia's history doesn't split that way — it's a single island that repeatedly changed hands as a whole, not a territory divided between powers at any one time. Rather than inventing false precision with a card (and an exact date) for each of the roughly fourteen flips between Britain and France, the third entry below names that pattern honestly as one bridging span. 1979 – present

Independent Saint Lucia

Independent stateJohn ComptonNo established church

Who & why: Saint Lucia gains full independence from Britain on 22 February 1979, having already carried this flag as an internally self-governing colony since 1967. It remains a Commonwealth realm, with the British monarch as head of state represented by a Governor-General, and joins the Organisation of Eastern Caribbean States alongside its Lesser Antilles neighbors.

What it represents: a cerulean-blue field for the surrounding Caribbean Sea and sky, with a large gold triangle in front of a larger black triangle edged in white — together forming a stylized image of the Pitons, the island's twin volcanic peaks near Soufrière. The black and white are read as symbolizing the harmony of the island's African and European heritage.

1814 – 1979

British Saint Lucia (final rule)

British colonyNo single ruler documentedRoman Catholic tradition / Anglican establishment

Who & why: the 1814 Treaty of Paris, closing the Napoleonic Wars, confirms Saint Lucia as British for good — ending the long cycle of changing hands once and for all. A sugar-plantation slave economy follows, with emancipation coming in 1834 (full freedom in 1838), then a shift toward banana cultivation as sugar declines. Saint Lucia is part of the short-lived West Indies Federation from 1958 to 1962 before moving toward self-government and, eventually, independence in 1979.

What it represents: the British colonial Blue Ensign, defaced with a Saint Lucia badge depicting the Pitons in the fly — the standard pattern flown across Britain's Caribbean colonies, each distinguished only by its own badge.

1660 – 1814

Britain and France's fourteen changes of hands

Anglo-French contestedNo single ruler documentedRoman Catholic tradition

Who & why: after decades of fierce Kalinago resistance kept European settlement off the island through most of the earlier 1600s, both France and England began serious settlement attempts from the 1650s–60s. What followed was not one calm colonial period but roughly fourteen separate captures and recaptures by British and French forces across a century and a half, as the great powers' wars in Europe — King William's War, the Seven Years' War, the American Revolutionary War, the French Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars — repeatedly spilled over into fights for this one island and its harbor at Castries. Control flipped back and forth again and again until the 1814 Treaty of Paris finally and permanently settled Saint Lucia as British.

What it represents: France's white ensign with its gold fleur-de-lis is shown here as representative of this span, not as a claim of one uninterrupted French period — the prose above spells out the real back-and-forth. France held the island for a slightly larger cumulative share of these years and left the lasting cultural imprint: Saint Lucia's patois and place names remain heavily French to this day, unusual for an eventually-British colony.

c. 1500 – 1660

Kalinago Saint Lucia

Kalinago / pre-colonialNo single ruler documentedKalinago spiritual tradition

Who & why: Saint Lucia's indigenous Kalinago population resisted European settlement especially fiercely and successfully. Spanish, English, and Dutch attempts to settle the island in the late 1500s and early 1600s were repeatedly destroyed or abandoned, leaving Saint Lucia under effective Kalinago control for over a century and a half after first European contact — longer than almost anywhere else in the Lesser Antilles, and long enough that the island is sometimes called a "sole survivor" for how long that resistance held out.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from this period. That absence reflects genuinely sustained indigenous control of the island, not simply a gap in the historical record, and is shown here honestly as a placeholder rather than an invented banner.
