Flags of Jordan

From the Nabataean traders who carved Petra out of the desert rock, through Roman Provincia Arabia, Crusader Oultrejordain, Mamluk and four Ottoman centuries, a British Mandate, and finally a Hashemite kingdom descended from the Sharifate of Mecca — eight flags across two millennia, the last of them unchanged since 1928.

This region's most recent history includes genuinely contested, still-unfolding events elsewhere in the Levant. Where the record below touches on them — the 1948 war, the West Bank — it sticks to dates, names, and documents, describing what happened without characterizing anything still actively disputed today.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Jordan, antiquity–present, eight political entities. No interactive map or internal-region view is included here — that layer is deferred project-wide to a later phase. Part of a larger atlas — see the linked pages at the bottom.

1946 – present

Hashemite Kingdom of Jordan

Kingdom, independent

Who & why: the March 1946 Treaty of London ended the British Mandate, and Emir Abdullah I was proclaimed king of the Hashemite Kingdom of Transjordan two months later. The Hashemite dynasty descends from the Sharifate of Mecca, through Sharif Hussein bin Ali, who led the 1916 Arab Revolt. In the 1948 war Transjordan took control of the West Bank, annexing it in 1950 (the kingdom was renamed Jordan the following year); Jordan formally relinquished its legal and administrative claims to the West Bank in 1988. The kingdom has been a comparatively stable constitutional monarchy since independence.

What it represents: black, white, and green horizontal bands (representing the Abbasid, Umayyad, and Fatimid caliphates respectively) with a red chevron bearing a white seven-pointed star at the hoist, a design descended from the 1916 Arab Revolt flag and in use, unchanged, since 1928.

1921 – 1946

British Mandate (Emirate of Transjordan)

Mandate

Who & why: Transjordan was split administratively from Mandatory Palestine in 1921, with Emir Abdullah — brother of the short-lived Arab Kingdom of Syria's Faisal — installed as its ruler under British oversight. The emirate had its own local flag (a variant of the Arab Revolt flag) alongside the British Union Flag as the mandatory power's own banner.

What it represents: the British Union Flag flew as the mandatory power's flag; the Emirate of Transjordan's own black-white-green banner with red chevron, adopted in 1928, is the direct ancestor of the kingdom's modern flag shown above.

1517 – 1921

Ottoman Empire

Empire province

Who & why: Ottoman conquest in 1516–17 absorbed the region east of the Jordan River into the wider Ottoman provincial system, administered from Damascus. Four centuries of Ottoman rule followed, ending with the empire's WWI-era collapse and the subsequent British Mandate arrangement.

What it represents: the Ottoman state flag, a red field with a white crescent and star, flown across all Ottoman provincial territory.

1260 – 1517

Mamluk Sultanate

Sultanate

Who & why: the Mamluks, ruling from Cairo, absorbed the region in 1260 after crushing a Mongol invasion at Ain Jalut, administering it through provincial governors until the Ottoman conquest of 1516–17.

What it represents: Mamluk heraldry used gold and other emblematic blazons tied to individual sultans and officers rather than one fixed state flag; the design here is an informed reconstruction, not a documented single national banner.

1189 – 1260

Ayyubid rule

Sultanate

Who & why: Saladin's Ayyubid dynasty retook the region from the Crusaders, including the fortress of Kerak in 1188–89, ending the Lordship of Oultrejordain. Ayyubid rule continued until the Mongol invasion of 1260, which the Mamluks then repelled and replaced with their own rule.

What it represents: no single fixed state flag is documented for this era; shown here as an honest placeholder.

1115 – 1189

Crusader Lordship of Oultrejordain

Crusader state

Who & why: King Baldwin I of Jerusalem pushed east of the Jordan and Dead Sea to found the lordship of Oultrejordain in 1115, building the fortress of Montreal (Shawbak); the fortress of Kerak was added later. Saladin's forces retook the territory by 1189.

What it represents: the Jerusalem cross, associated with the wider Kingdom of Jerusalem of which Oultrejordain was a lordship, shown here as a regionally representative design rather than a documented flag specific to the lordship itself.

636 – 1115

Early Islamic rule (Jund al-Urdunn)

Caliphate

Who & why: following the Rashidun conquest of the 630s, the region east of the Jordan River was organized as Jund al-Urdunn ("the Jordan district"), one of the Umayyad Caliphate's military-administrative districts, and remained under successive Umayyad and Abbasid caliphal rule and their governors for nearly five centuries, until the arrival of Crusader forces in 1115. This long span is treated here as one honest bridging entry.

What it represents: no single fixed flag is documented across the Umayyad and Abbasid periods; plain white (the color most consistently associated with Umayyad banners) is shown here as representative of the earlier part of this span.

antiquity – 636

Ancient Jordan

No flag documented

Who & why: the Nabataean Kingdom, an Arab trading people whose capital Petra grew wealthy controlling desert caravan routes, flourished from roughly the 4th century BCE until Rome annexed it in 106 CE as the province of Arabia Petraea. Roman and then Byzantine rule continued over the region's cities (part of the Decapolis league) until the Rashidun Arab conquest of the 630s.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from this span; national and civic flags as a concept postdate it entirely. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
