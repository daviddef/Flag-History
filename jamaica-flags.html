Flags of Jamaica

A thinly held Spanish backwater for a century and a half, seized by Cromwell's fleet in 1655 and remade into Britain's richest sugar colony on the backs of enslaved Africans, and finally an independent nation on 6 August 1962 — Jamaica's own flag is still the only national banner in the world that carries no red, white, or blue at all.

Four flags across five centuries: the Cross of Burgundy over Santiago de la Vega, two centuries of the plain Union Jack, a defaced Blue Ensign carrying the colony's own badge, and the gold-black-green saltire flown since the eve of independence.

1962 – present

Independent Jamaica

Independent stateAlexander BustamanteNo established church
Migration & conquest:Emigration: to the UK, US & Canada (1960s–)

Who & why: Jamaica withdraws from the short-lived West Indies Federation (1958–62) and becomes independent on 6 August 1962, within weeks of Trinidad and Tobago. The new flag is chosen by a bipartisan parliamentary committee and adopted just before independence day; Alexander Bustamante becomes the country's first prime minister.

What it represents: a gold diagonal saltire divides the field into black triangles at the hoist and fly and green triangles top and bottom. A popular explanation reads it as "hardships there are, but the land is green and the sun shineth" — and, famously, it is the only national flag in the world with no red, white, or blue in it at all.

1875 – 1962

British Crown Colony of Jamaica (colonial ensign era)

British colonySir John Peter GrantAnglicanism (disestablished 1870)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Indian & Chinese indentured laborers (1845–1917)Emigration: labor migration & the Windrush generation (1900s–)

Who & why: from the later nineteenth century, British colonies increasingly flew a defaced Blue Ensign carrying their own badge in the fly. Jamaica's badge, a heraldic shield centered on a golden pineapple, came into informal use around the 1870s and was formalized by Colonial Office regulation in 1906. This span also carries the 1865 Morant Bay rebellion and its brutal suppression under Governor Eyre, the direct imposition of Crown Colony rule that followed, and the slow, uneven growth of local self-government through the early twentieth century, ending with the West Indies Federation's collapse in 1962.

What it represents: the standard colonial pattern — a dark blue field with the Union Jack in the canton and the individual colony's own badge on the fly — shown here with a simplified version of Jamaica's shield-and-pineapple badge.

1655 – 1875

British Jamaica (plain Union Jack)

British colonyWilliam Penn & Robert VenablesAnglican (established)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: EnglandImmigration: enslaved Africans via the slave trade

Who & why: an English expeditionary force sent by Oliver Cromwell as part of the "Western Design" seizes Jamaica from Spain in May 1655, after failing to take Santo Domingo. For roughly two centuries the island is administered simply under the Union flag, with no distinguishing colonial badge yet in use. Jamaica becomes Britain's wealthiest sugar colony, built on massive enslaved labor; slavery is abolished in 1834 and full emancipation follows in 1838. The 1831–32 Baptist War, led in part by the enslaved preacher Samuel Sharpe, is among the largest slave uprisings in the British Caribbean and helps push Parliament toward abolition.

What it represents: simply the Union flag of Great Britain (and, after 1801, of the United Kingdom), flown without alteration — the ordinary practice before colonies were given their own distinguishing badges.

1509 – 1655

Spanish Jamaica

Spanish colonyJuan de EsquivelRoman Catholic (established)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: SpainImmigration: enslaved Africans (from 1510s)

Who & why: Juan de Esquivel establishes the first Spanish settlement, Sevilla la Nueva, around 1509–10; the colony's capital shifts inland to Santiago de la Vega (later Spanish Town) around 1534. Spanish Jamaica is always a minor, thinly populated backwater next to Cuba or Hispaniola, held mainly for provisioning passing fleets. The island's indigenous Taíno population, already reduced by disease and forced labor, is devastated within a few decades of contact. Spanish rule ends when Cromwell's expeditionary force captures the island in 1655.

What it represents: the Cross of Burgundy, a red diagonal saltire of knotted branches on white, is the standard Spanish colonial and maritime banner of this era, flown across Spain's American possessions rather than a flag unique to Jamaica.
