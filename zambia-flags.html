Flags of Zambia

Zambia takes its name from the Zambezi River, the great waterway (and its Victoria Falls) that borders much of the country. Its path runs from the Lozi Kingdom of Barotseland, which secured a special protected status distinct from the rest of the territory under British rule, through Cecil Rhodes's chartered British South Africa Company, direct Crown administration as Northern Rhodesia, an unpopular decade inside a settler-dominated federation, and finally independence in 1964 under Kenneth Kaunda.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Zambia, from the Lozi Kingdom of Barotseland and other pre-colonial polities through British colonial rule to the modern republic. 1964 – present

Republic of Zambia (independent)

Republic, independentKenneth KaundaChristian nation (constitutional declaration)

Who & why: Northern Rhodesia becomes independent as Zambia on 24 October 1964, taking its name from the Zambezi River, under founding president Kenneth Kaunda, who governs until 1991. Barotseland's special protected status under the Litunga is formally absorbed into the unitary Zambian state around independence, a point of ongoing debate within the country since.

What it represents: green for the land's natural resources and vegetation, the orange African fish eagle for freedom, and the red-black-orange vertical block for the freedom struggle, the country's people, and its mineral wealth (especially copper) respectively — a deliberate break from the British Blue Ensign tradition of the colonial era.

1953 – 1964

Northern Rhodesia in the Federation of Rhodesia and Nyasaland

Colony (federated)Godfrey HugginsAnglican (favored, not established)

Who & why: Britain joins Northern Rhodesia, Southern Rhodesia, and Nyasaland into the Central African Federation in 1953, a structure pushed largely by the region's white settler minority and deeply unpopular among the African populations of all three territories, who saw it as a vehicle for entrenching settler political and economic privilege. Sustained African nationalist opposition (including Kenneth Kaunda's own political rise) contributes to the Federation's dissolution at the end of 1963, after which Northern Rhodesia moves quickly toward its own independence the following year.

What it represents: the British Blue Ensign defaced with the Federation's own badge, used as Northern Rhodesia's flag for the Federation's decade of existence, distinct from the territory's separate pre- and post-Federation colonial badge.

1924 – 1953

Northern Rhodesia (Crown protectorate)

ColonyHerbert StanleyAnglican (favored, not established)

Who & why: the British Crown takes over direct administration of Northern Rhodesia from the British South Africa Company in 1924, ending three decades of company rule. The Litunga (king) of the Lozi Kingdom of Barotseland retains a distinct treaty relationship and internal autonomy under indirect rule throughout this period, a special status not shared by the rest of the territory.

What it represents: the standard British colonial Blue Ensign pattern, defaced with Northern Rhodesia's own badge on a white disc, distinguishing it from neighboring Southern Rhodesia's separate colonial flag.

1891 – 1924

British South Africa Company rule (North-Western & North-Eastern Rhodesia)

Chartered company ruleCecil RhodesAnglican (favored, not established)

Who & why: Cecil Rhodes's British South Africa Company (BSAC) extends mineral concessions and administrative control north of the Zambezi from 1890–91, initially as two separate territories (North-Western and North-Eastern Rhodesia, unified administratively in 1911 as Northern Rhodesia) under royal charter rather than direct Crown rule — a private commercial entity exercising governmental authority, a genuinely unusual arrangement even by the standards of colonial Africa. The Company negotiates its 1890 concession with the Lozi king Lewanika, whose Barotseland kingdom retains distinct protected status.

What it represents: a British colonial-pattern Blue Ensign defaced with the Company's own badge, used as the administrative flag of the BSAC's territories north of the Zambezi until direct Crown administration begins in 1924.

c. 1830 – 1891

Lozi Kingdom of Barotseland (and neighboring Bemba, Chewa, and other polities)

Kingdom, independentMulambwaTraditional/ancestral religion

Who & why: the Lozi (Barotse) Kingdom, centered on the Zambezi floodplain in the territory's west, is the region's most organized and durable pre-colonial state, with a distinct royal court under the Litunga. Elsewhere across the territory, the Bemba, Chewa, Ngoni, and other peoples maintain their own chieftaincies. King Lewanika of Barotseland negotiates directly with the British South Africa Company in 1890, securing a treaty relationship that preserves a measure of internal autonomy not extended to the rest of the territory under later colonial rule.

What it represents: no securely documented national flag survives from this pre-colonial era for Barotseland or its neighboring kingdoms; royal authority was expressed through regalia, drums, and symbolic objects. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
