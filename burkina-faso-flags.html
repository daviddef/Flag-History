Flags of Burkina Faso

From the Mossi kingdoms of Ouagadougou and Yatenga — among the most durable pre-colonial states in West Africa, ruled by a paramount Moogho Naaba and never fully absorbed by their larger neighbors — through French conquest and half a century as the colony of Upper Volta, to independence in 1960 and a 1984 revolution that renamed the country and remade its flag: four flags (plus one honest blank) across five centuries.

The 1984 renaming from Upper Volta to Burkina Faso, and the switch from a black-white-red tricolor to today's red-green flag with its gold star, was a deliberate break by Thomas Sankara's revolutionary government — a rejection of a colonial-era name in favor of one built from the country's own languages. That flag has now flown, unaltered, through nearly every change of government since, including Sankara's own 1987 assassination.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Burkina Faso, c. 1500–present, five political entities. The Mossi kingdoms are treated as a single entry because they used no textile flags at all, not because their history was simple — Ouagadougou and Yatenga were in practice separate, sometimes rival, courts under the same broad Mossi cultural and political tradition. 1984 – present

Burkina Faso

Republic, independent

Who & why: on 4 August 1984, the revolutionary government of Captain Thomas Sankara renames the country from Upper Volta — a colonial-era label with no meaning in any Burkinabè language — to Burkina Faso, joining the Mooré word for "upright, incorruptible people" with the Dioula word for "fatherland." Sankara's four years in power bring sweeping literacy, vaccination, and land-reform campaigns before he is killed in a coup on 15 October 1987 led by his close associate Blaise Compaoré, who then governs for 27 years. The flag adopted under Sankara outlasts him: it flies unchanged through Compaoré's long rule, his 2014 ouster, and the coups of 2022.

What it represents: a two-band flag of red over green with a centered gold five-pointed star, replacing the old black-white-red tricolor entirely. Red and green are widely shared pan-African colors signaling solidarity with other post-colonial African states; the gold star stands for the guiding light of the revolution.

1960 – 1984

Republic of Upper Volta (independent)

Republic, independent

Who & why: full independence from France is declared on 5 August 1960, with Maurice Yaméogo as the first president. The following 24 years bring chronic instability — military coups in 1966, 1980, 1982, and 1983, and repeated changes of government and constitution — but the country's name, borders, and flag all carry over unchanged from the autonomous-republic period, until an August 1983 coup brings a young army captain, Thomas Sankara, to power.

What it represents: the same black-white-red horizontal tricolor first adopted in 1959, flown continuously through independence and every change of government until the 1984 renaming.

1958 – 1960

Autonomous Republic of Upper Volta (French Community)

Autonomous republic

Who & why: in the 28 September 1958 constitutional referendum, Upper Volta — like most of French West Africa — votes to become an autonomous republic within the newly formed French Community, gaining internal self-government while France retains control of defense, foreign affairs, and currency. The territory adopts its own national flag and anthem in this period, symbols carried forward unchanged into full independence less than two years later.

What it represents: a horizontal tricolor of black, white, and red, adopted in 1959. Contemporary explanations tie the three colors to the country's three principal rivers — the Black Volta, White Volta, and Red Volta — whose combined name the country itself carried until 1984.

1904 – 1958

French Upper Volta (colonial)

Colony

Who & why: with the Mossi kingdoms subdued by French military columns in 1896–97, the territory is absorbed into the colony of Upper Senegal and Niger in 1904, part of French West Africa. A separate colony of Upper Volta is carved out on 1 March 1919, roughly along today's borders. It proves short-lived as a distinct unit: administrators dissolve it on 5 September 1932, splitting its territory among Ivory Coast, French Sudan, and Niger for administrative economy during the Depression, before reconstituting it as its own territory again on 4 September 1947, after sustained pressure from Voltaic elites and colonial officials alike. It remains a separate territory for the rest of French rule.

What it represents: as in every French colonial territory, the French tricolor is the flag flown, with no distinct local ensign; Upper Volta's borders and administrative status shift twice within this period, but it never has, or needs, a flag of its own.

c. 1500 – 1904

Mossi Kingdoms (Ouagadougou & Yatenga)

Kingdoms, pre-colonial

Who & why: oral tradition and early chronicles place the founding of the Mossi kingdom of Ouagadougou (Wogodogo) around the late 15th century under Naaba Oubri, with the northern kingdom of Yatenga splitting off and consolidating separately by the mid-16th century. Alongside smaller Mossi states such as Tenkodogo and Fada N'Gourma, these became some of the most durable pre-colonial polities in this part of West Africa, organized around a paramount ruler at Ouagadougou, the Moogho Naaba ("Ruler of the World"), who presided over a structured court of ministers and provincial chiefs and whose states resisted absorption into the region's larger Islamic empires and jihadist states for centuries. French military columns reached and subdued Ouagadougou in 1896–97, with French administrative control over the Mossi states consolidated through the early 1900s.

What it represents: like most pre-colonial West African kingdoms, the Mossi states used no flags in the European heraldic sense. Royal and political authority was expressed instead through regalia — the Moogho Naaba's ceremonial horse and procession, courtly drumming, and hierarchies of chiefly titles — not through any textile standard. No flag is shown here because none is documented; inventing one would misrepresent the record.
