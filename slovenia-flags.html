Flags of the Julian Alps

From the half-legendary Slavic principality of Carantania, through four centuries as a Habsburg duchy, a WWII partition into three occupation zones, and Yugoslav federation, to the small Alpine republic of today — twelve flags, one long argument about who governs the Slovene-speaking lands.

Slovenia's throughline is unusual: its 19th-century tricolor — white, blue, red — survives almost untouched from the Habsburg-era Duchy of Carniola all the way to the flag flying today. Only the crest in the corner ever really changes.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Slovenia, c. 700–present, twelve political entities. Part of a larger atlas covering the former Yugoslav lands — see the map and linked pages at the bottom.

Three crownlands, one nation — eventually

Slovenia's own real border, split into the three historic lands that were, for most of history, governed separately: the Littoral (Primorska) on the Adriatic, Carniola (Kranjska) at the center, and Styria (Štajerska) in the northeast. Press play, or drag the slider.

Slovenia's three regions rarely shared a ruler either. The Littoral spent centuries under Aquileia, then Venice, then Italy twice in the 20th century; Styria answered to the Habsburgs fifty years before Carniola did; only after 1947 were all three governed as a single administrative unit. The border lines below only solidify where two pieces were actually ruled apart — they dissolve the moment one government held all three.

1991 – present

Republic of Slovenia

Sovereign state

Who & why: Slovenia declared independence from Yugoslavia on 25 June 1991, triggering the brief Ten-Day War with the Yugoslav army before Belgrade withdrew — the shortest and least bloody of the Yugoslav wars of secession. Slovenia joined the EU and NATO in 2004.

What it represents: the same white-blue-red tricolor carried since Carniola, the red star finally replaced by a new coat of arms designed by artist Marko Pogačnik: Mount Triglav (Slovenia's highest peak and a national emblem in its own right), two wavy blue lines for the sea and rivers, and three gold six-pointed stars from the arms of the medieval Counts of Celje — the one indigenous Slovene noble dynasty of real power, extinct since 1456, deliberately invoked to root the new state in something older than either Habsburg or Yugoslav rule.

1945 – 1991

Socialist Republic of Slovenia

Socialist republic

Who & why: one of six constituent republics of Tito's SFR Yugoslavia, roughly reunifying ethnic-Slovene territory in one administrative unit for the first time — including the Slovene Littoral (Koper) after the Trieste dispute was resolved in the 1950s–70s.

What it represents: the traditional white-blue-red tricolor, kept for continuity, with a gold-bordered red star added at center — the standard formula applied across every Yugoslav republic's flag, communism laid directly over each nation's own historic colors.

1941 – 1945

Axis Partition of Slovenia

Occupation / puppet state

Who & why: after the April 1941 invasion of Yugoslavia, Slovene territory was carved into three pieces and directly annexed by three different powers: Nazi Germany took Styria and Upper Carniola (folded into the Reichsgau Steiermark/Kärnten, with mass expulsions and a ban on the Slovene language); Fascist Italy annexed the Province of Ljubljana as a special province; Hungary annexed the small Prekmurje district. No Slovene state existed anywhere on the map.

What it represents: each occupier flew its own national flag over its slice — the Nazi flag over the north, the Italian tricolor over Ljubljana and the south, the Hungarian tricolor over Prekmurje in the far northeast. The Slovene Partisan resistance, organized as the Liberation Front from April 1941, fought under a version of the old white-blue-red tricolor with a red star, reclaiming the national colors even as the state itself had vanished.

1918 – 1941

Kingdom of SHS / Kingdom of Yugoslavia

Kingdom

Who & why: Slovene lands joined the new South Slav kingdom under the Serbian Karađorđević dynasty; the 1920 Carinthian plebiscite kept most Slovene-speaking Carinthia in Austria, while the Julian March (Trieste, Gorizia, most of Istria) went to Italy — both losses still felt keenly in Slovene national memory. What remained became the Drava Banovina after 1929.

What it represents: the pan-Slavic tricolor, blue-white-red, the kingdom's fixed ordering from the 1921 Vidovdan Constitution onward — the same flag used across the whole kingdom, Slovenia included.

29 Oct – 1 Dec 1918

State of Slovenes, Croats and Serbs

Sovereign state (unrecognized)

Who & why: as Austria-Hungary disintegrated at the end of WWI, the National Council of Slovenes, Croats and Serbs in Zagreb declared a new, internationally unrecognized state covering all South Slav lands of the collapsing empire — Slovene lands, Croatia-Slavonia, Dalmatia, Bosnia, and Vojvodina.

What it represents: the pan-Slavic tricolor in red-white-blue order, distinct from Carniola's white-blue-red. It lasted five weeks before merging into the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes on 1 December 1918.

1849 – 1918

Austrian Crownlands (Carniola, Styria, Carinthia, Littoral)

Colonial / dynastic rule

Who & why: the 1848 revolutions forced the abolition of the short-lived Kingdom of Illyria in 1849; rather than restore a single Illyrian unit, Vienna reverted to administering the Slovene-speaking lands as separate Austrian crownlands — Carniola, Styria, Carinthia, and the Littoral (Küstenland) — each with its own diet and governor, all answering directly to Vienna. This arrangement carried through the 1867 Compromise into Cisleithania and lasted until Austria-Hungary's collapse at the end of WWI in 1918.

What it represents: no single flag covered all four crownlands together — each flew the common Austrian imperial black-and-gold alongside its own crownland arms (Carniola's white-blue-red land colors among them). Shown here is the shared Habsburg dynastic banner flown over all of them, the closest thing to a common symbol for the period.

1813 – 1816

Austrian Military Restoration (Illyria Reoccupied)

Military administration

Who & why: Austrian troops retake the former Illyrian Provinces from the retreating French in 1813, but the 1815 Congress of Vienna's territorial settlement takes another two years to translate into a new civil administration — the region sits under provisional Habsburg military governance in the meantime, its final status (a restored crownland structure versus a single new kingdom) not yet decided.

What it represents: no distinct flag is documented for this short provisional-governance interval; imperial Austrian authority is asserted on the ground before Emperor Francis I formally proclaims the Kingdom of Illyria in 1816.

1816 – 1849

Kingdom of Illyria (Habsburg)

Kingdom (crownland)

Who & why: after the Congress of Vienna (1815), Emperor Francis I reorganized the recovered Napoleonic territory into a new Habsburg kingdom-status crownland, seated again at Ljubljana — keeping the French-era administrative unification while reasserting dynastic rule.

What it represents: Austrian imperial colors and the Habsburg double-headed eagle stamped over the same borders the French had drawn. Abolished after the 1848 revolutions, its territory split back into separate crownlands (Carniola, Carinthia, the Littoral).

1809 – 1813

Illyrian Provinces (Napoleonic France)

Colonial rule

Who & why: after defeating Austria in 1809, Napoleon united Carniola, western Carinthia, Gorizia, Trieste, Istria, and parts of Croatia and Dalmatia into a single French province, seated at Ljubljana — cutting Austria off from the Adriatic.

What it represents: the plain French tricolor, but the administration briefly introduced schooling and legal notices in Slovene alongside French — a small, much-remembered gesture of linguistic recognition in an era when Slovene had almost no official standing. Austria retook the region by 1813.

973/1364 – 1918

Duchy of Carniola

Colonial / dynastic rule

Who & why: Carniola (Kranjska) became a hereditary Habsburg crownland in the 14th century and stayed one until 1918, governed from Vienna with its own diet seated in Ljubljana (Laibach). It was, and remained, the most solidly Slovene-speaking of all the Habsburg crownlands.

What it represents: the duchy's arms — a crowned eagle with a crescent and three stars on its breast — produced the "land colors" white-blue-red, fixed by Emperor Ferdinand I in 1836. In 1848, as revolution swept the empire, Slovene national activists adopted these Carniolan colors as a pan-Slovene tricolor — the same order and colors Slovenia's flag still carries today.

828 – 973

Frankish Marches (March of Carniola)

Colonial / dynastic rule

Who & why: after a series of revolts against Frankish overlordship in the early 9th century, the Carolingian Franks abolished Carantania's native princely line around 828 and absorbed the territory directly into Carolingian administration, organizing it as marches — chiefly the March of Carniola, alongside neighboring marches covering Carinthia and the Styrian highlands — governed by Frankish-appointed margraves rather than native dukes. After the Carolingian Empire fragmented, the marches passed to the East Frankish (later German) kingdom.

What it represents: no native Slovene polity existed in this period, and no flag or heraldic device is attested — the Franks and their East Frankish successors administered the marches directly from outside. The span traditionally ends around 973, the year cited for the March of Carniola's first appearance as a more distinct entity under its own local counts, setting up the Duchy of Carniola that follows.

c. 658/745 – 828

Principality of Carantania

Principality

Who & why: one of the earliest Slavic polities in the eastern Alps, Carantania is remembered for the investiture ritual at the Prince's Stone near Krnski Grad, in which a free commoner formally seated the new duke — cited in Slovene national memory (and, per some historians, by Thomas Jefferson) as an early example of consent-based rule. It became a Bavarian, then Frankish (Carolingian) vassal duchy through the 8th century.

What it represents: the black panther on gold shown here is a much later heraldic invention, first documented in Styrian sources centuries after Carantania fell, then adopted by 19th- and 20th-century Slovene nationalists as a "first state" symbol. No genuine 8th-century flag exists to depict.
