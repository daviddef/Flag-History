Flags of Liberia

Liberia's story does not begin like most others in this atlas. For centuries the Kru, Vai, Gola, and other peoples of this stretch of West African coast and forest governed themselves in fragmented, independent communities, with no say whatsoever in what came next: in 1822, the American Colonization Society began landing free-born and formerly enslaved Black Americans at Cape Mesurado, a controversial and deeply contested project even among abolitionists. What followed is nonetheless a genuine milestone — on 26 July 1847 the settlers' Commonwealth declared itself the Republic of Liberia, Africa's first republic and the only African state never formally colonized by a European power.

The flag chosen at that 1847 independence — eleven red-and-white stripes for the eleven signers of the Declaration of Independence, a blue canton with a single white star, deliberately modeled on the flag of the United States — has not changed since, through a century of Americo-Liberian settler dominance over the indigenous majority, a 1980 coup, two devastating civil wars, and the peace that has held since 2003.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Liberia, c. 1000–present, six political entities. The pre-1822 entry covers indigenous polities that had no involvement in or say over the colonization that followed — a genuinely different story from most of this atlas, and one this page states plainly rather than glossing over. From 1822 onward the lineage is single and continuous: American Colonization Society settlement, then Commonwealth, then Republic, with no internal partition and so no internal-partition map. 2003 – present

Republic of Liberia (postwar)

Republic, independent

Who & why: the August 2003 Accra Comprehensive Peace Agreement ends the second civil war and forces Charles Taylor into exile; a transitional government oversees disarmament until Ellen Johnson Sirleaf wins election in 2005, taking office in January 2006 as Africa's first elected female head of state. Reconciliation is slow and incomplete — a Truth and Reconciliation Commission reports in 2009, and instability, an Ebola epidemic (2014–16), and continued poverty all test the peace — but the republic itself holds, with successive peaceful transfers of power through the 2010s and 2020s.

What it represents: the same 1847 flag, carried through the postwar period without alteration, its eleven stripes and single star now standing for a republic rebuilding rather than one at war.

1980 – 2003

Republic of Liberia (coup and civil war)

Republic, civil conflict

Who & why: on 12 April 1980, Master Sergeant Samuel Doe leads a violent coup that kills President William R. Tolbert and ends 133 years of Americo-Liberian political dominance, but Doe's own increasingly repressive rule collapses into open war when Charles Taylor's National Patriotic Front invades in December 1989. The first civil war (1989–97) and the second (1999–2003) together kill an estimated quarter-million people and displace far more, drawing in regional peacekeepers and, eventually, UN forces.

What it represents: the flag adopted in 1847 does not change through any of this — no faction in either war replaces the national banner, and the eleven stripes and single star fly, at least nominally, over a state fighting for its survival.

1847 – 1980

Republic of Liberia (Americo-Liberian era)

Republic, independent

Who & why: on 26 July 1847 the Commonwealth declares itself the independent Republic of Liberia, Africa's first republic and the only African state never formally colonized by a European power — a genuine and distinctive milestone. But the republic's politics remain dominated for the whole of this era by the small settler-descended Americo-Liberian elite, governing through the True Whig Party over an indigenous majority who hold little formal political power until well into the 20th century; that imbalance runs unbroken until the 1980 coup ends it by force.

What it represents: a flag designed by a committee of seven Liberian women and adopted at independence: eleven red-and-white stripes for the eleven signers of the Declaration of Independence, a blue canton with one white star, deliberately modeled on the Stars and Stripes given the settlers' own American origins.

1839 – 1847

Commonwealth of Liberia

Commonwealth

Who & why: in 1839 the American Colonization Society reorganizes its scattered coastal settlements into the Commonwealth of Liberia, a single quasi-self-governing unit with an appointed governor (from 1841, Joseph Jenkins Roberts, the first Black man to hold the post) and its own legislature, still nominally under Society oversight. The arrangement proves transitional: settler leaders increasingly chafe at foreign trading tariffs the Commonwealth cannot enforce as a non-sovereign entity, a grievance that helps push the move to full independence in 1847.

What it represents: the Commonwealth continues to fly the 1827 colonial banner — the Society's US-derived stripes with a white cross in the canton — through this entire period, unchanged until independence replaces it outright.

1822 – 1839

American Colonization Society settlement

Colonial settlement

Who & why: in 1822 the American Colonization Society, a US organization whose members ranged from abolitionists who saw resettlement as a path to freedom to slaveholders who wanted free Black Americans removed from the country — a plan many free Black Americans themselves actively opposed as a deportation scheme — begins landing free-born and formerly enslaved settlers at Cape Mesurado. The settlement is named Monrovia, after US President James Monroe, and is governed for its first years directly by white agents of the Society, not by the settlers themselves.

What it represents: the earliest settlement flew the flag of the United States itself; on 9 April 1827 the ACS board adopted a distinct colonial banner — the Society resolved to keep the US flag's stripes but "omit the stars and substitute a white cross," making the design similar enough to signal dependence on the US, yet distinct enough to mark the African colony as its own.

c. 1000 – 1822

Indigenous Kru, Vai, Gola, and neighboring peoples

Fragmented polities

Who & why: for centuries before 1822, the coast and forest of what is now Liberia are home to the Kru, Vai, Gola, Kpelle, Bassa, and other peoples, organized into independent towns, chiefdoms, and trading networks rather than any single unifying state. The Vai develop their own indigenous syllabary in the early 19th century, one of the few writing systems created from scratch in West Africa. None of these communities had any involvement in, or say over, the American Colonization Society's decision to begin settling free-born and formerly enslaved Black Americans on this coast in 1822 — a genuinely different story from most of the rest of this atlas.

What it represents: no securely documented flag unifies these communities, because no single polity ever governed them together; each town and chiefdom used its own local emblems and none survive as a fixed territorial banner. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
