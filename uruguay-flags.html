Flags of Uruguay

The land on the eastern bank of the Uruguay River — the "Banda Oriental" — sat for more than a century on the exact seam between two American empires, and both fought for it accordingly. Portugal pushed a trading post across the water from Buenos Aires in 1680; Spain answered by founding Montevideo forty years later to hold the line; and when Spanish rule finally collapsed, Portuguese Brazil simply annexed the territory outright rather than let it go independent, precisely because whoever held the eastern bank held the mouth of the Río de la Plata.

What makes Uruguay's founding genuinely unusual in this atlas is that it did not become independent by winning its own war of liberation the way most of its neighbors did. After the 1825 Thirty-Three Orientals uprising dragged Argentina and Brazil into a war neither could win outright, Britain stepped in as mediator: the 1828 Treaty of Montevideo, guaranteed by the United Kingdom, created Uruguay as a deliberate buffer state wedged between its two much larger neighbors — independence granted less to satisfy Uruguayan self-determination than to keep Argentina and Brazil from fighting each other over the territory again.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Uruguay, c. 1500s (Charrúa era)–present. This page carries no internal-partition map — there is no genuine internal governing split within Uruguay's own modern borders at any point in its history, so it follows the atlas's single-continuous-timeline template rather than the split-map template used elsewhere. 1830 – present

Oriental Republic of Uruguay (modern)

Republic

Who & why: the 1830 constitution formally establishes the Oriental Republic of Uruguay following the 1828 British-brokered independence settlement, fixing the flag in its final form the same year. The 20th century brings real turbulence — including a 1973–85 civil-military dictatorship — but none of it touches the flag itself, which has flown unaltered through democratic government, dictatorship, and the return to democracy alike.

What it represents: nine alternating white and blue horizontal stripes, one for each of the republic's nine original departments, with a golden Sol de Mayo (Sun of May) in a white canton — the same sun emblem carried on Argentina's flag, a direct nod to the shared revolutionary-era heritage of the Río de la Plata provinces.

1825 – 1828

Independence and the buffer-state settlement

Republic, independence declared

Who & why: the "Treinta y Tres Orientales" (Thirty-Three Orientals), led by Juan Antonio Lavalleja, land on the Banda Oriental in April 1825 and rise against Brazilian rule, triggering a war between Argentina and Brazil over the territory. Neither side can settle it by force, so Britain steps in to broker the December 1828 Treaty of Montevideo, under which both Argentina and Brazil recognize an independent Uruguay — created deliberately as a buffer state between them, a genuinely distinctive founding rationale among this atlas's republics.

What it represents: the independence fighters' own historic banner, sometimes shown as a light-blue, white, and red tricolor carrying the motto "Libertad o Muerte" (Liberty or Death) — the predecessor to the fixed 1830 national design.

1816 – 1825

Cisplatina (Portuguese/Brazilian rule)

Annexed province

Who & why: Portuguese-Brazilian forces invade in 1816 and complete the annexation of the Banda Oriental by 1817, renaming it the "Cisplatine Province" and formally folding it into the United Kingdom of Portugal, Brazil and the Algarves. After Brazil's own 1822 break from Portugal, the province passes intact into the newly independent Empire of Brazil. Brazilian rule over the province is a direct trigger for the Thirty-Three Orientals' 1825 uprising.

What it represents: the province flew under Portuguese colonial authority through 1822, then under the banner of the Brazilian Empire afterward — shown here honestly as two halves of one entry rather than a single continuous flag, since the ruling banner itself changed mid-period even though the annexation did not.

1680s – 1816

Banda Oriental (Spanish colonial, contested with Portugal)

Colony, contested

Who & why: Spain and Portugal dispute this stretch of the eastern riverbank for well over a century. Portugal opens the contest by founding Colônia do Sacramento in 1680, directly across the river from Buenos Aires; Spain answers by founding Montevideo in 1724–30 to secure the eastern bank against further Portuguese encroachment. The territory remains a genuine, long-running frontier flashpoint between the two empires' American claims until Portuguese/Brazilian forces finally seize it outright in 1816.

What it represents: the Cross of Burgundy — a red saltire on a white or cream field — the standard military and civil banner flown across Spain's American colonies, including the contested Banda Oriental garrisons and Montevideo itself.

to 1680s

Charrúa and other indigenous peoples

No unified polity

Who & why: the Charrúa and other indigenous peoples inhabit the territory in small, mobile communities before European contact, with no single ruling authority over the region. Comparatively sparse Spanish and Portuguese settlement follows contact, a direct consequence of the territory's position as a frontier zone between the two empires rather than a core holding of either.

What it represents: no flag or fixed territorial banner is documented for this era or these peoples. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
