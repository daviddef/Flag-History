Flags of Russia

From a Varangian-and-Slavic trade-route principality founded on the Dnieper in 862, through two and a half centuries of Mongol tribute, Muscovy's slow "gathering of the Russian lands," a Tsardom, an Empire that spanned eleven time zones, a five-year civil war fought across those same eleven time zones, seventy years of Communist rule, and a Federation still finding its shape since 1991 — nine political entities, and a white-blue-red tricolor that has been adopted, banned, and readopted twice.

Russia's own borders have moved more than almost any other country in this atlas — from a Kyiv-centered river-trade state to a transcontinental empire to the world's largest country by land area today. This page traces the flags that flew over the lands actually governed from Moscow (and, for two centuries, St. Petersburg); it is the first country in the atlas to extend beyond Europe's western, central, Balkan, Nordic, and Baltic regions already covered.

Scope of this page: the lands governed from Kyiv, then Moscow (and St. Petersburg, 1712–1918), c. 862–present, nine political entities. Kievan Rus' (862–1240) is the shared medieval ancestor also claimed by Ukraine and Belarus — this page frames it the same honest way their own pages do, as a common root rather than an exclusively Russian one. The Soviet Union (1922–1991) was a union of fifteen constituent republics, of which the Russian SFSR was the largest and most dominant, never simply "Russia under a different flag" — see the framing below. Kaliningrad, a real Russian exclave on the Baltic since 1945, is discussed in prose but not drawn on the maps, which focus on the historical mainland. December 1991 – present

Russian Federation

Federal republic Boris Yeltsin Secular state

Who & why: Boris Yeltsin, already elected President of the Russian SFSR in June 1991 and the man who faced down the August 1991 coup attempt from atop a tank outside the White House, becomes the first president of an independent Russia when the USSR formally dissolves on 26 December 1991. The 1990s bring economic shock therapy, a new constitution (1993, after a violent standoff with parliament), and two wars in Chechnya; Vladimir Putin succeeds Yeltsin on the last day of 1999.

What it represents: the white-blue-red tricolor, first used as a Russian merchant ensign under Peter the Great and the country's national flag from 1883 to 1917, is readopted on 22 August 1991 — two days after the coup collapses — deliberately reaching back past the entire Soviet period to the pre-revolutionary state. Kaliningrad, the former East Prussian city of Königsberg annexed by the USSR in 1945 and resettled with Soviet citizens, becomes a genuine Russian exclave in 1991 once Lithuania regains independence, cut off from the rest of the country by two foreign states ever since.

December 1922 – December 1991

Soviet Union (Russian SFSR as founding republic)

Communist state (union republic) Vladimir Lenin State atheism

Who & why, honestly framed: on 30 December 1922, the Russian SFSR, the Ukrainian SSR, the Byelorussian SSR, and the Transcaucasian SFSR sign the treaty founding the USSR — a union of nominally sovereign socialist republics, not a renaming of Russia itself, even though the Russian SFSR was always the largest by far (about three-quarters of Soviet territory and half its population) and Moscow remained both the Russian republic's and the whole Union's capital. Lenin dies in 1924; Stalin's rise, forced collectivization, the famines and purges of the 1930s, victory in the "Great Patriotic War" (1941–45) at colossal cost, and Cold War superpower status all follow under this one flag, ending only with Gorbachev's failed reforms and the USSR's dissolution in December 1991.

What it represents: the plain red RSFSR banner of the civil war years gains the hammer (industrial workers) and sickle (peasantry) emblem beneath a red star by 1923, standardized across all fifteen constituent republics with only minor local variations — the Russian SFSR's own republic-level flag added a thin blue stripe at the hoist from 1954 onward, distinguishing it from the plain all-Union banner shown here.

November 1917 – December 1922

Russian Soviet Federative Socialist Republic (Civil War)

Communist state (civil war) Vladimir Lenin State atheism (from 1918)

Who & why: Lenin's Bolsheviks seize the Winter Palace and depose the Provisional Government on 7–8 November 1917 (25–26 October, Old Style), and the new Russian Soviet Republic is declared days later. What follows is not a clean transfer of power but roughly five years of civil war — Bolshevik "Reds" against a patchwork of monarchist, liberal, and separatist "White" armies, foreign intervention forces from Britain, France, the US, and Japan, and a war with newly independent Poland (1919–21) — that only ends with the last White evacuation from Crimea (1920) and the absorption of the Far Eastern buffer state (1922). See the map above for the war's real, shifting fronts.

What it represents: a plain red field, the first flag decree of April 1918 specifying the republic's Cyrillic initials in gold at the hoist — a deliberate break from the deposed tricolor, and the direct ancestor of the Soviet flag that follows it.

March – November 1917

Russian Provisional Government / Russian Republic

Provisional republic Alexander Kerensky Russian Orthodox (disestablished)

Who & why: Tsar Nicholas II abdicates on 15 March 1917 (2 March, Old Style) after days of strikes and mutiny in Petrograd end 304 years of Romanov rule. Prince Georgy Lvov heads the first Provisional Government; Alexander Kerensky, initially Minister of Justice, becomes Prime Minister in July and formally proclaims the Russian Republic on 1 September 1917. The government's fatal weakness — its refusal to withdraw from a deeply unpopular World War I, shared uneasily with the rival Petrograd Soviet in a period of "dual power" — leaves it unable to survive the Bolshevik coup that comes in November.

What it represents: the existing white-blue-red civil tricolor, simply kept without any crown or imperial eagle — the first time the flag flies over a Russia with no tsar at all, a status quo choice rather than a new design, in office for barely eight months.

1721 – March 1917

Russian Empire

Empire Peter I "the Great" Russian Orthodox (state church, Holy Synod from 1721)

Who & why: the Senate proclaims Peter I "Emperor of All Russia" in October 1721 after victory over Sweden in the Great Northern War, formalizing what had been the Tsardom into an Empire on the European model, with a new capital already rising at St. Petersburg since 1703. Catherine the Great's 18th-century expansion, Napoleon's disastrous 1812 invasion, the 1861 emancipation of the serfs, and a wave of industrialization and revolutionary unrest (1905, then 1917) all unfold under this one imperial banner, by the end spanning from Poland to the Pacific and briefly Alaska.

What it represents: the white-blue-red civil and merchant tricolor, in continuous use since Peter's own 1693/1705 ensign, becomes the empire's official national flag by decree in 1883 (confirmed 1896), typically shown here with the black double-headed eagle — a separate black-yellow-white dynastic flag was also used officially between 1858 and 1883 before the tricolor's final confirmation.

1547 – 1721

Tsardom of Russia

Tsardom Ivan IV "the Terrible" Russian Orthodox (state church)

Who & why: Ivan IV is crowned "Tsar of All Russia" in January 1547, the first Muscovite ruler to formally claim the title (derived from "Caesar") rather than merely Grand Prince, asserting parity with the Byzantine and Mongol imperial traditions Moscow claimed to inherit. His reign brings the conquest of the Khanates of Kazan (1552) and Astrakhan (1556), the brutal Oprichnina terror, and, after the Rurikid dynasty dies out in 1598, the chaotic "Time of Troubles" (1598–1613) that ends only with Mikhail Romanov's election as Tsar, founding the dynasty that rules until 1917.

What it represents: the Tsar's own gold or crimson standard bearing the black double-headed eagle — adopted as Muscovy's state seal under Ivan III in 1497 and elevated to a full royal banner under the early Romanovs — no single flag was flown uniformly over the whole realm in this period; this is the Tsar's personal and military standard, the closest thing to a national symbol that existed.

1480 – 1547

Grand Duchy of Moscow (Muscovy, sovereign)

Grand principality Ivan III "the Great" Russian Orthodox (autocephalous from 1448)

Who & why: Ivan III, already decades into "gathering the Russian lands" by absorbing rival principalities including Novgorod (1478), faces down Khan Akhmat's Golden Horde army at the Ugra River in October 1480 — the two armies stare each other down for weeks before the Horde withdraws without a decisive battle, an anticlimax later remembered as the "Great Stand on the Ugra" that ends Mongol suzerainty for good. Ivan takes the title "Sovereign of All Rus'" and begins using the Byzantine double-headed eagle as a state seal after his 1472 marriage to a Palaiologos princess, niece of the last Byzantine emperor.

What it represents: no state flag survives from this era — Muscovy's rulers used religious icons (the "Not Made by Hands" image of Christ) on campaign banners and dynastic wax seals, not a fixed national flag; the placeholder here matches this atlas's convention for genuinely undocumented periods.

1240 – 1480

Mongol/Tatar suzerainty over the Rus' principalities

Vassal / tributary Alexander Nevsky Russian Orthodox (tolerated by the Horde)

Who & why, honestly framed: the Mongol conquest that burns Kiev in December 1240 does not create a single unified Russian state under a foreign flag — it leaves the surviving Rus' principalities (Vladimir-Suzdal, Novgorod, and, increasingly, the small trading outpost of Moscow) as separate, self-governing tributaries of the Golden Horde, required to pay tribute and secure a khan's patent (a "yarlyk") to rule. Prince Alexander Nevsky, already famous for battlefield victories over Swedish and Teutonic invaders in 1240 and 1242, chooses pragmatic submission to the Horde over resistance, securing the yarlyk for Vladimir in 1252 — a strategy later princes of Moscow, notably Ivan I "Kalita" (granted the title in 1327), use to accumulate wealth and influence as the Horde's own tax collectors, quietly building Moscow into the dominant Rus' power long before 1480 formally ends the tribute.

What it represents: like Poland's 1795–1918 partition entries elsewhere in this atlas, this period is shown as a vassal status rather than genuine statehood — individual princes retained their own seals and banners, but no single flag flew over "Russia" as a subject territory of the Golden Horde, whose own banners this page does not claim as Russian.

862 – 1240

Kievan Rus'

Principality (loose federation) Rurik (traditional founder, 862) Eastern Orthodox (from 988)

Who & why, honestly framed: Kievan Rus' — centered on Kyiv, founded as a Varangian-and-Slavic trade-route state along the Dnieper — is the shared medieval ancestor claimed by three modern nations alike: Ukraine, Belarus, and Russia all trace their origins to this one polity, and this page frames it the same honest way this atlas's Ukraine and Eastern Europe hub pages already do, as a common root rather than an exclusively Russian one. The Primary Chronicle's traditional (semi-legendary) account has the Varangian Rurik "invited" to rule Novgorod in 862; his successor Oleg extends control to Kyiv around 882. Grand Prince Vladimir the Great converts the realm to Christianity in 988; his son Yaroslav the Wise (r. 1019–54) presides over its cultural and legal high point. Rus' had already been fragmenting into competing, often warring principalities for over a century — formalized by the 1097 Council of Liubech — well before the Mongols arrived and ended Kyiv's primacy for good in 1240; the northern principalities of Vladimir-Suzdal and Novgorod, ancestral to the later Muscovite state, are among those competing successors, not simply "Russia already existing under another name."

What it represents: no securely documented state flag survives from this era — rulers used dynastic seal-marks instead, most famously the trident (tryzub) associated with Vladimir the Great's own coinage, which Ukraine revived as its national coat of arms in 1918 and again in 1992; this page uses the same placeholder convention as Ukraine's and Belarus's own pages for the identical undocumented period.

Reading the tags

Political system

Principality / Tsardom / Empire — hereditary monarchy of some form
Vassal / tributary — paid tribute to a foreign power, not fully sovereign
Republic — provisional, federal, or otherwise non-monarchical and non-Communist
Communist state — one-party rule, Marxist-Leninist

Leader — the ruler most associated with that era's opening
