Flags of Burundi

Burundi's own precolonial kingdom, ruled by a Mwami from the same highlands as Rwanda's monarchy, shared German and then Belgian administration with its neighbor but stayed a genuinely separate kingdom and court throughout — and unlike Rwanda, it kept its monarchy at independence in 1962, before a coup replaced it with a republic four years later. Burundi's own history of ethnic violence, including a 1972 mass killing of Hutus, is distinct from Rwanda's, and this page states it plainly, consistent with the rest of this atlas.

Burundi's flag, adopted in 1967, carries three red stars — for the national motto's unity, work, and progress — on a white disc split by a green-and-red saltire.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Burundi, c. 1600–present, five political entities. Burundi shared colonial administration with neighboring Rwanda as the joint territory of Ruanda-Urundi from 1916-1962, but the two were, and remain, distinct kingdoms and countries with their own separate histories — this page is scoped to Burundi alone. Republic of Burundi

Republic, independent

Who & why: Prime Minister Michel Micombero deposes the young Mwami Ntare V in a 1966 coup, ending the monarchy and declaring a republic; a new flag follows in 1967. Burundi's own postcolonial history includes a distinct, separate cycle of Hutu-Tutsi violence from Rwanda's: most seriously, the 1972 mass killings of Hutus by the Tutsi-dominated army and government, a well-documented atrocity following a Hutu-led uprising, with estimates of the dead ranging into the hundreds of thousands. Further civil war follows the 1993 assassination of Melchior Ndadaye, Burundi's first elected Hutu president, lasting until a 2005 peace settlement establishes power-sharing government, under which the country has continued since.

What it represents: a white saltire (diagonal cross) divides the field into red triangles (top and bottom, for the struggle for independence) and green triangles (hoist and fly, for hope), centered on a white disc bearing three red six-pointed stars outlined in green, representing the national motto's three principles — Unity, Work, Progress — and often read as also standing for the country's three main population groups, Hutu, Tutsi, and Twa.

Kingdom of Burundi (independent monarchy)

Kingdom / Sultanate, independent

Who & why: Burundi becomes independent on 1 July 1962 — the same day as Rwanda, the two territories splitting formally from the joint Ruanda-Urundi administration — but unlike Rwanda, Burundi keeps its monarchy, with Mwami Mwambutsa IV as head of state. The brief independent kingdom is marked by growing political instability and ethnic tension, including a failed 1965 coup attempt, before the monarchy is abolished outright by Micombero's 1966 coup.

What it represents: a white field bearing the royal drum (karyenda), the traditional symbol of the Burundian monarchy's authority, used on the kingdom's flag through this short independent-monarchy period.

Ruanda-Urundi (Belgian mandate / UN trust territory)

Colonial rule

Who & why: Belgian forces occupy Rwanda and Burundi during the First World War, taking them from German East Africa; the League of Nations formally awards Belgium the joint mandate of Ruanda-Urundi in 1916/1922, continued as a UN trust territory after 1946. Burundi's own Mwami and court remain in place under Belgian indirect rule throughout, kept administratively distinct from Rwanda's kingdom even though the two share one colonial government. Independence movements press through the 1950s-60s, and Burundi splits from the joint territory to become independent on its own on 1 July 1962, as a kingdom rather than a republic.

What it represents: the Belgian tricolor, flown as the mandate/trust territory's administering power's own flag, typically alongside a colonial badge for official use; no distinct Ruanda-Urundi territorial flag was adopted.

Kingdom of Burundi under German suzerainty

Colonial rule

Who & why: Germany extends its claim over Burundi as part of German East Africa from the late 1890s, ruling indirectly through the existing Mwami and court rather than dismantling the kingdom, much as it does in neighboring Rwanda. German administration is thin and largely limited to a few outposts before the First World War, when Belgian forces occupy the territory from 1916, ending direct German control.

What it represents: the imperial German colonial ensign — the German merchant flag's black-white-red in the canton on a plain field — flown over German East Africa's administrative posts through this period, while the Burundian monarchy continues to fly its own royal regalia locally underneath it.

Kingdom of Burundi (precolonial)

Kingdom / Sultanate, independent

Who & why: a centralized Kingdom of Burundi, ruled by a Mwami through a court structure distinct from — though comparable to — neighboring Rwanda's monarchy, consolidates in the highlands south of Lake Kivu from roughly the 17th century onward. The royal drum (karyenda) serves as the central symbol of the Mwami's sacred authority throughout this period, a tradition strong enough that it is later revived as the flag emblem of the brief independent kingdom in 1962.

What it represents: no national flag is documented for the precolonial kingdom in the modern sense; royal authority was expressed through the karyenda drum and court ritual rather than a territorial banner. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
