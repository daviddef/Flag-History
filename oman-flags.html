Flags of Oman

One of the oldest continuously independent states in the Arab world: an Ibadi Imamate founded in 751 in the interior, Portuguese-held coastal ports from 1507, a Ya'ariba dynasty that expelled them and built the beginnings of a maritime empire, the Al Said dynasty ruling without interruption since 1744, a 19th-century empire that once controlled Zanzibar and stretches of the East African coast, and rapid modernization under Sultan Qaboos from 1970.

Oman's flags carry the region's oldest visual thread here — a plain red field flown by Muscat's own ships for centuries before any emblem was added — and the modern white-red-green tricolor, adopted only in 1970, is a comparatively recent innovation layered onto that much older maritime tradition.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Oman, c. 700–present, six political entries. Part of a larger atlas — see the map and linked pages at the bottom.

Coast and interior, 1507–1624

Oman's own real border, split into the one span where it genuinely was two separately-governed pieces: Portuguese-held coastal forts and trading ports (Muscat, Sohar, Sur, and others along the Gulf of Oman and Arabian Sea coast) against the older Ibadi Imamate's interior. 1970 – present

Sultanate of Oman (modern)

Sultanate, independentSultan Qaboos bin SaidIbadi Islam (state-favored)

Who & why: Sultan Qaboos bin Said deposes his own father in a palace coup on 23 July 1970 and launches a rapid modernization program, opening what had been one of the most isolated countries in the world to schools, hospitals, and paved roads within a generation. The country is officially renamed from "Muscat and Oman" to simply "Oman," and Qaboos rules until his death in 2020, succeeded by his cousin Haitham bin Tariq.

What it represents: a new national flag adopted in 1970 (its canton emblem refined in 1995) places the khanjar — Oman's traditional curved dagger — crossed with swords in the canton, over a white-red-green tricolor, layered onto the older plain red field that had flown over Muscat for centuries before.

1856 – 1970

Sultanate of Muscat and Oman

Sultanate, British-influencedSultan Thuwaini bin SaidIbadi Islam (state-favored)

Who & why: upon Said bin Sultan's death in 1856, his sprawling maritime empire splits between two sons — one inherits Zanzibar and the East African holdings, the other keeps Muscat and the Omani homeland — ending direct Omani rule over Zanzibar for good. The much-reduced sultanate leans increasingly on British treaties and subsidies through the 19th and 20th centuries while the interior Ibadi Imamate periodically reasserts itself, fully reabsorbed into the sultanate only by 1959.

What it represents: the plain red flag, already a maritime tradition of long standing among Muscat's own ships, continues as the sultanate's flag through this entire span, without a national emblem.

1744 – 1856

Al Said Dynasty & the Omani (maritime) Empire

Sultanate / Imamate, expandingAhmad bin Said Al SaidIbadi Islam (state-favored)

Who & why: Ahmad bin Said al-Busaidi, having helped expel a Persian occupying force, founds the Al Said dynasty in 1744 — the same family that still rules Oman today, one of the longest unbroken royal lines in the Arab world. Under Said bin Sultan in the early 19th century, Omani power reaches its peak: a real maritime empire controlling Zanzibar and stretches of the East African coast, with Said relocating his own capital to Zanzibar in 1832 to oversee a lucrative clove and trade economy.

What it represents: the plain red flag, Muscat's own maritime color for generations, flies over both the Omani homeland and the African holdings throughout this era of expansion, no separate emblem yet distinguishing the empire's reach.

1624 – 1744

Ya'ariba Imamate

ImamateNasir bin Murshid Al Ya'arubiIbadi Islam

Who & why: the Ya'ariba dynasty unifies the Ibadi Imamate under Nasir bin Murshid in 1624 and, over the following decades, drives the Portuguese entirely out of Muscat (1650) and the wider Omani coast, building the naval strength that becomes the foundation of Oman's later maritime empire. A succession dispute after 1718 weakens the dynasty and eventually opens the door to Persian intervention, which the young Al Said dynasty ends in 1744.

What it represents: no single specific banner is well attested for this era; plain red, the color already tied to Omani/Muscat shipping in this region for generations, is shown here as an informed reconstruction rather than a documented period specimen.

1507 – 1624

Portuguese-held ports & the interior Imamate

Fragmented / contestedNo single ruler documentedRoman Catholic (ports), Ibadi Islam (interior)

Who & why: Portuguese forces under Afonso de Albuquerque seize Muscat and other coastal ports in 1507 to secure the sea route to India, holding them as fortified trading posts for over a century while the Ibadi Imamate, under the older Nabhani dynasty's fading authority, continues to govern the interior separately. This is a genuinely split, contested span — coast and interior under two different powers — rather than one Oman under one flag.

What it represents: the Portuguese fly their own maritime ensigns over the coastal forts; the interior Imamate's own banner tradition is poorly attested for this specific period. One honest bridging entry rather than an invented shared flag.

c. 700 – 1507

Ibadi Imamate of Oman (founding era)

ImamateJulanda bin Mas'udIbadi Islam

Who & why: Ibadi Islam, a distinct branch tracing to the earliest Kharijite-era disputes over leadership of the Muslim community, takes root in Oman, and the Ibadi Imamate is formally established in 751 — one of the oldest continuously significant political-religious institutions in the Arab world. The Nabhani dynasty comes to dominate the Imamate's temporal authority from around 1154, ruling most of the interior until Portuguese arrival on the coast in 1507.

What it represents: no flag in the modern territorial sense survives from this era; the Imamate used religious and tribal insignia rather than a fixed national banner. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
