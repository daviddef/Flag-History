Flags of Monaco

One family, seven centuries: the House of Grimaldi has ruled Monaco continuously since 1297, when François Grimaldi — "Malizia," the cunning one — talked his way into the fortress disguised as a wandering Franciscan monk, then let his own armed men in behind him.

Monaco's territory and protectors shifted repeatedly across the centuries — Genoese, Spanish, and French influence in turn, a full decade swallowed whole by Revolutionary France, and the loss of most of its historical land in 1861 — but the Grimaldi line itself never broke, making it one of the longest continuously ruling dynasties on earth.

Scope of this page: the lordship, then principality, of Monaco under House Grimaldi, 1297–present, five phases. Monaco's borders shrank sharply in 1861 when Menton and Roquebrune — most of its historical territory — were ceded to France; this page tracks the ruling authority and its flags, not a territorial map, since Monaco's remaining land is now a single dense city-state too small for a meaningful map at this atlas's scale. 1861 – present

Principality of Monaco (French protectorate, modern)

Sovereign principality, French-protected

Who & why: after Sardinia-Piedmont cedes the surrounding county of Nice to France in 1860, Monaco's old protector is gone; the 1861 Franco-Monegasque Treaty establishes a new French protectorate, and in the same settlement Monaco formally cedes Menton and Roquebrune — roughly 95% of its historical territory, lost to local revolt years earlier — leaving the small remaining enclave that is Monaco today. Prince Charles III adopts the current red-over-white flag in 1881; the Grimaldi dynasty continues to rule as constitutional monarchs, with the relationship to France further formalized by treaty in 1918 and updated since, right through to the present day.

What it represents: plain red over white, drawn from the colors of the Grimaldi coat of arms, with no emblem. The design is famously near-identical to Indonesia's flag, differing only in proportions — a real and often-noted coincidence between two unrelated national flags.

1815 – 1860

Restored Grimaldi principality, under Sardinian protection

Grimaldi lordship, dependent

Who & why: the 1814–15 Congress of Vienna restores the Grimaldi family to Monaco after Napoleon's defeat, but places the tiny principality under the protection of the Kingdom of Sardinia-Piedmont rather than restoring full independence. Menton and Roquebrune, together the bulk of Monaco's historical territory, grow increasingly restive under Grimaldi tax policy through this period, eventually declaring themselves free towns in 1848 — a rupture that sets up their formal cession to France in 1861.

What it represents: no distinct national flag is well documented for this narrow window; the Grimaldi family's own heraldic banner — red and white lozenges (fusils), the same arms the family has used since the Middle Ages — stands in for state symbolism, as it does through most of Monaco's pre-1881 history.

1793 – 1814

Annexed by Revolutionary and Napoleonic France

Annexed, no separate flag

Who & why: in 1793, Revolutionary France annexes Monaco outright, abolishing the principality entirely; the ruling Grimaldi family is imprisoned in Paris (Prince Honoré III barely survives), the palace is looted, and Monaco is administered as an ordinary French territory renamed Fort-Hercule. The principality is not restored until Napoleon's fall and the 1814–15 Congress of Vienna settlement.

What it represents: the plain French Republican, then Napoleonic imperial, tricolor flies over Monaco throughout this period — there is no separate Monegasque flag, because there is no separate Monegasque state.

1297 – 1793

Grimaldi lordship (Genoese, Spanish, and French protection)

Grimaldi lordship, dependent

Who & why: in January 1297, François Grimaldi — "Malizia," the cunning one — disguises himself as a Franciscan monk to gain entry to the fortress of Monaco, then lets in his own armed men, seizing the rock for Genoa's Guelph faction. The Grimaldi family secures permanent possession by purchase in 1419 and rules continuously from then on, though rarely fully independently: Monaco falls under Spanish protection after the 1524 Treaty of Burgos, then switches to French protection under the 1641 Treaty of Péronne, when Honoré II first formally secures recognition of his title as sovereign Prince.

What it represents: no distinct Monegasque national flag is well documented across this nearly five-century span; the Grimaldi family's own heraldic arms — red and white lozenges (fusils) — serve in place of one, the same pattern the family still uses in its coat of arms today.
