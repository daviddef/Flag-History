Flags of Niger

Niger's pre-colonial map is genuinely fragmented rather than dynastic: Hausa city-states such as Damagaram (centered on Zinder) controlled the south, the eastern reach of the Songhai Empire and later the Kanem-Bornu sultanate touched its edges near Lake Chad, and Tuareg confederations governed the vast desert north along the trans-Saharan trade routes. No single power ever unified the whole territory before French conquest.

France organized the area as the Military Territory of Niger from 1900, folding it into French West Africa as a full colony by 1922. Independence followed on 3 August 1960. Niger's orange-white-green horizontal tricolor, with its central orange disc, has flown unchanged since 1959 even through the country's frequent post-independence political turbulence — the flag has survived far more governments than most in this atlas.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Niger, from its pre-colonial patchwork of Hausa, Songhai/Kanem-Bornu, and Tuareg polities through French colonization to the modern republic. No single flag unifies the pre-colonial era, since no single power ever governed the whole territory; that period is shown as one honest entry rather than an invented banner. 1960 – present

Republic of Niger (independent)

Republic, independentHamani DioriSecular (no state religion)

Who & why: Niger becomes independent on 3 August 1960 under President Hamani Diori. Since then it has experienced repeated political instability — multiple military coups (including in 1974, 1996, 1999, 2010, and most recently 2023) interspersed with periods of civilian, multiparty rule — alongside recurring Tuareg rebellions in the north. Through all of it, the flag adopted before independence has never changed.

What it represents: a horizontal orange-white-green tricolor adopted 23 November 1959: orange for the Sahara desert of the north, green for the fertile Niger River valley and savanna of the south, white for purity and the Niger River itself, and a central orange disc for the sun.

1958 – 1960

Autonomous Republic of Niger (French Community)

Autonomous republicHamani DioriSecular (French Community)

Who & why: Niger votes "yes" in the September 1958 French Community referendum, becoming a self-governing republic within the Community rather than breaking immediately, as neighboring Guinea had. Full sovereignty follows less than two years later.

What it represents: the orange-white-green tricolor with its central orange disc, adopted 23 November 1959 ahead of full independence and carried forward unchanged.

1900 – 1958

French Niger (military territory, then colony)

ColonyNo single ruler documentedRoman Catholic (French colonial favor)

Who & why: France establishes the Military Territory of Niger in 1900 following brutal punitive campaigns against local resistance, later reorganizing it as a full civilian colony within French West Africa by 1922, with its capital eventually fixed at Niamey. Tuareg communities in the north resist French control longest, with significant uprisings continuing into the 1910s–20s.

What it represents: the French Tricolore flew as the sovereign flag across French West Africa; no distinct territorial flag existed for Niger itself. Shown here as a placeholder Union Flag pattern matching this atlas's convention for unrepresented colonial-era sovereign flags — swap for the Tricolore (blue-white-red vertical bands) when painting.

pre-1900

Hausa city-states, Songhai/Kanem-Bornu fringe, and Tuareg confederations

Fragmented polities

Who & why: no single power governs the territory before colonization. Hausa city-states, above all Damagaram (centered at Zinder from the 18th century), hold the south; the far west and center fall within the historical reach of the Songhai Empire and, after its fall, remain contested among smaller successor states; the east touches the long-lived Kanem-Bornu sultanate near Lake Chad; and Tuareg confederations control the Sahara in the north, governing the trans-Saharan salt and trade routes through their own clan structures.

What it represents: no securely documented flag unifies this era, since no single polity ever governed the whole territory; each state and confederation used its own regalia and standards. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
