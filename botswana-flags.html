Flags of Botswana

Botswana's colonial arrival is unusual in the region: rather than being conquered, three Tswana chiefs travelled to London in 1895 and petitioned Queen Victoria directly to keep their territory a British protectorate rather than hand it to Cecil Rhodes's British South Africa Company. What followed was a genuinely low-key, low-investment colonial administration, and independence in 1966 produced one of postcolonial Africa's most stable and prosperous democracies, underwritten within a year by the discovery of major diamond deposits.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Botswana, from the Tswana chiefdoms through the Bechuanaland Protectorate to the modern republic. No interactive map on this page — deferred to a future phase of the atlas. Part of a larger atlas — see the linked pages at the bottom.

1966 – present

Republic of Botswana (independent)

Republic, independent

Who & why: the Bechuanaland Protectorate becomes independent as Botswana on 30 September 1966 under Seretse Khama, grandson of Khama III and himself the center of a major controversy years earlier over his marriage to a white Englishwoman, Ruth Williams. Major diamond deposits are discovered at Orapa in 1967, just months after independence, and Botswana goes on to become one of the fastest-growing economies in the world through the late 20th century and one of Africa's most consistently stable multiparty democracies.

What it represents: light blue for water and rain (echoed in the national motto and currency, both named "Pula," meaning rain), and the black-and-white stripe for racial harmony and for the zebra, the national animal.

1885 – 1966

Bechuanaland Protectorate

Protectorate

Who & why: Britain declares a protectorate over Bechuanaland in 1885, partly in direct response to the 1895 petition of Bangwato chief Khama III and two other Tswana chiefs (Bathoen I and Sebele I), who travelled to London to secure continued direct Crown protection rather than being absorbed into Cecil Rhodes's chartered British South Africa Company. British administration in Bechuanaland is famously minimal and low-investment throughout, run largely from Mafeking, just outside the protectorate's own borders in South Africa, leaving most day-to-day governance to the Tswana chiefs themselves.

What it represents: the standard British colonial Blue Ensign pattern, defaced with Bechuanaland's own badge, in use throughout the protectorate era until independence.

Tswana chiefdoms (Bangwato, Bakwena, Bangwaketse, and others)

Pre-colonial chiefdoms

Who & why: before British protection, the territory is organized into several distinct Tswana chiefdoms (merafe), each under its own kgosi (chief), including the Bangwato, Bakwena, and Bangwaketse — the same chiefly structures that later negotiate directly with Britain to preserve their protectorate status.

What it represents: no securely documented unified flag survives from this pre-colonial era; each chiefdom expressed authority through its own regalia and symbols rather than a fixed national banner. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
