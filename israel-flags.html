Flags of Israel

From the ancient Israelite and Judahite kingdoms and the Second Temple period, through Roman-era Judea, centuries of diaspora, Crusader Jerusalem, Mamluk and Ottoman rule, and a British Mandate, to the 1948 declaration of the State of Israel — seven entries across roughly three thousand years.

This is the atlas's most politically sensitive page. Every date and event below is drawn from the well-documented historical record, and the account stops at the 1949 armistice agreements — it deliberately does not extend into, or characterize, the Israeli-Palestinian conflict as it has continued since. Where the record touches contested history, it sticks to dates, names, and documents rather than taking a side.

Scope of this page: the land of present-day Israel, antiquity–present, seven political entities. No interactive map or internal-region view is included here — that layer is deferred project-wide to a later phase. Part of a larger atlas — see the linked pages at the bottom.

1948 – present

State of Israel

Republic, independent

Who & why: on 29 November 1947, the UN General Assembly adopted Resolution 181, recommending that Mandatory Palestine be partitioned into a Jewish state, an Arab state, and an internationally administered Jerusalem; the Jewish Agency accepted the plan, the Arab League and the Arab Higher Committee rejected it, and fighting began between Jewish and Arab communities inside Palestine. The British Mandate formally ended on 14 May 1948, and that same day David Ben-Gurion declared the establishment of the State of Israel in Tel Aviv. The next day, Egypt, Transjordan, Syria, Lebanon, and Iraq entered the war. Armistice agreements, negotiated on the island of Rhodes, were signed between Israel and each of its neighbors through 1949.

What it represents: a white field with two horizontal blue stripes and a central blue Star of David, adopted in 1948, based on the pattern of the tallit (Jewish prayer shawl) and on the flag the Zionist movement had itself used since the late 19th century.

1917 – 1948

British Mandate for Palestine

Mandate

Who & why: British forces took the region from Ottoman control in 1917, the same year the British government issued the Balfour Declaration, stating its support for "the establishment in Palestine of a national home for the Jewish people," while also stating that nothing should "prejudice the civil and religious rights of existing non-Jewish communities in Palestine." The League of Nations Mandate for Palestine was formalized at the April 1920 San Remo Conference and administered from Jerusalem. The Mandate ended on 14 May 1948, giving way to the State of Israel above.

What it represents: the British Union Flag flew as the mandatory power's flag over Mandatory Palestine throughout this period.

1517 – 1917

Ottoman Empire

Empire province

Who & why: Ottoman conquest in 1516–17 absorbed the region into the wider provincial system of Ottoman Syria (organized in part as the Sanjak of Jerusalem), without a separate administrative unit corresponding to modern Israel's borders. Four centuries of Ottoman rule followed, ending when British forces took the region during World War I in 1917.

What it represents: the Ottoman state flag, a red field with a white crescent and star, flown across all Ottoman provincial territory.

1291 – 1517

Mamluk Sultanate

Sultanate

Who & why: the Mamluks, ruling from Cairo, took Acre — the last mainland stronghold of the Kingdom of Jerusalem — in 1291, ending Crusader presence in the region, and administered it through provincial governors until the Ottoman conquest of 1516–17.

What it represents: Mamluk heraldry used gold and other emblematic blazons tied to individual sultans and officers rather than one fixed state flag; the design here is an informed reconstruction, not a documented single national banner.

1099 – 1291

Kingdom of Jerusalem (Crusader)

Crusader state

Who & why: the First Crusade took Jerusalem in July 1099, and Godfrey of Bouillon became the new Kingdom of Jerusalem's first ruler. Saladin's Ayyubid forces retook Jerusalem itself in 1187, reducing the kingdom to a shrinking coastal strip; the kingdom's mainland presence ended with the fall of Acre to the Mamluks in 1291.

What it represents: the "Jerusalem cross" — one large cross with four smaller crosses in its quarters, gold on white — is the kingdom's well-documented heraldic banner.

636 – 1099

Early Islamic rule / Fatimid Palestine

Caliphate

Who & why: following the Rashidun conquest of the 630s, the region was organized as Jund Filastin ("the Palestine district"), a military-administrative district centered on Ramla and Jerusalem under the Umayyad and then Abbasid caliphates. Fatimid Egypt took control in the 10th century and held the region, with interruptions, until the First Crusade in 1099. This span is treated here as one honest bridging entry.

What it represents: no single fixed flag is documented across this whole span; plain white (the color most consistently associated with Umayyad banners) is shown here as representative of the earlier part of it, with Fatimid rule later associated with green.

antiquity – 636

Ancient & biblical Israel through Roman Judea

No flag documented

Who & why: this span covers the Israelite and Judahite kingdoms of the biblical period; the First and Second Temple periods in Jerusalem; Roman-era Judea, first as a Herodian client kingdom and then as a direct Roman province; the Jewish revolts against Roman rule in 66–73 CE and 132–135 CE (the latter led by Bar Kokhba); and the centuries of Jewish diaspora that followed those revolts, alongside continued Jewish presence in the land. Byzantine rule followed Rome's, lasting until the Muslim conquest of 636.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from this span; national and civic flags as a concept postdate it entirely. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
