Flags of the United States

No flag in this atlas has a more precisely documented lineage than the Stars and Stripes: a fixed 13 red-and-white stripes since 1818, and a star count that climbed, admission by admission, from 13 to 50 — each change dated to the exact year a new state joined the union. This page traces that count from the British Thirteen Colonies through the Revolution, the Civil War, and the last two admissions, Alaska and Hawaii, in 1959.

The 1776 Declaration of Independence coexisted with chattel slavery for the nation's first 89 years. The Civil War (1861–65) was fought centrally over slavery, and this page states that plainly rather than glossing over it: the Union flag flown throughout the war kept the stars of every seceded state, a deliberate assertion that the secession itself was never recognized as legitimate.

Scope of this page: the lands of the present-day United States, c. 1607–present, nine flag eras defined by star count and constitutional status. No interactive map on this page — that layer is deferred project-wide to a future phase. Part of a larger atlas — see the linked pages at the bottom.

1960 – present

50-star flag

Republic, independentDwight D. EisenhowerNo established state religion

Who & why: Hawaii is admitted as the 50th state on 21 August 1959; per the Flag Act of 1818's standing rule that a new star takes effect the 4th of July following a state's admission, the 50-star flag becomes official on 4 July 1960 — now the longest-serving version of the flag in the nation's history by a wide margin.

What it represents: 50 stars in a staggered 9-row pattern, 13 alternating red-and-white stripes for the original colonies — the design most familiar today.

1912 – 1959

48-star flag

Republic, independentWilliam Howard TaftNo established state religion

Who & why: Arizona's February 1912 admission, the last of the contiguous 48 states, completes the star count. President Taft's 1912 executive order also fixes, for the first time, the flag's precise star arrangement and proportions by law — the flag flown through both World Wars and the Great Depression.

What it represents: 48 stars in a rigid 6-by-8 grid, 13 stripes; the first version with legally standardized star placement.

1865 – 1912

Post-war expansion, 36 to 45 stars

Republic, independentAndrew JohnsonNo established state religion

Who & why: Reconstruction (1865–77) follows the Civil War, restoring the former Confederate states to the union amid the fraught, incomplete work of extending citizenship and voting rights to formerly enslaved Americans. Westward settlement drives eight new states into the union across this period — Nebraska, Colorado, and the six states admitted in 1889–90 alone — each adding its own star the following 4th of July.

What it represents: a fast-changing star count (36, 37, 38, then a jump to 43, then 44, then 45) shown here as one bridging entry, since no single arrangement lasted more than a few years across this span.

1861 – 1865

Civil War, Union flag

Civil warAbraham LincolnNo established state religion

Who & why: the Civil War (1861–65) is fought centrally over slavery, an institution that had coexisted with the nation's founding ideals for its first 89 years. Eleven Southern states secede to form the Confederate States of America; the Union government never recognizes the secession as legitimate, and deliberately keeps every seceded state's star on its own flag throughout the war. The war ends with roughly 620,000 dead and, with the 13th Amendment, the abolition of slavery.

What it represents: 34 stars at the war's outbreak, rising to 35 with West Virginia's 1863 admission (itself formed by counties that broke from seceded Virginia) — the Union's refusal to remove a single star was itself a political statement.

1818 – 1861

Westward expansion, 20 to 33 stars

Republic, independentJames MonroeNo established state religion

Who & why: the Flag Act of 1818, prompted by the awkwardness of adding a stripe for every new state, permanently fixes the stripe count at 13 (for the original colonies) and provides that only a star is added, on the following 4th of July, for each new state. Thirteen states join the union across this period, from Mississippi (1818) to Kansas (1861), doubling the country's size while slavery remains legal and expanding in the South throughout.

What it represents: a fast-changing star count shown here as one honest bridging entry, since no single arrangement lasted more than a few years; official star layout is not standardized until 1912, so period flags show real variation.

1795 – 1818

15-star flag

Republic, independentGeorge WashingtonNo established state religion

Who & why: Vermont (1791) and Kentucky (1792) join the union, and Congress adds both a star and a stripe for each in 1795 — the only version of the flag ever to carry more than 13 stripes. This is the flag Francis Scott Key sees flying over Fort McHenry in 1814, inspiring "The Star-Spangled Banner."

What it represents: 15 stars and 15 alternating red-and-white stripes, before Congress realizes a stripe per state is unsustainable and reverses course in 1818.

1777 – 1795

13-star flag

Republic, independentJohn HancockNo established state religion

Who & why: the Second Continental Congress's Flag Act of 14 June 1777 (still marked as Flag Day) establishes the first official Stars and Stripes, following the July 1776 Declaration of Independence and the Revolutionary War (1775–83) that secures it. No single star arrangement is specified by law — circular, staggered-row, and grid patterns all appear on period flags.

What it represents: 13 stars for the original states, 13 alternating red-and-white stripes — a founding that coexisted with chattel slavery, legal in every one of the original 13 states at independence and not fully abolished nationwide for another 88 years.

c. 1607 – 1777

British Thirteen Colonies

Colonial ruleJames I of EnglandChurch of England / Congregationalism (varied by colony)

Who & why: English (from 1707, British) colonization along the Atlantic seaboard begins with Jamestown in 1607, eventually forming the Thirteen Colonies whose growing resentment of British taxation and rule without representation erupts into the Revolutionary War in 1775. Slavery is present and legal in every colony from the colonial era onward.

What it represents: British colonial flags (the Union Jack and its ensign variants) fly for most of this period; by the Revolution's opening months in late 1775, colonial forces adopt the Grand Union Flag (the Continental Colors) — British Union canton with 13 stripes — as a transitional banner asserting American unity while formal independence was still being debated.
