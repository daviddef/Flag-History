Flags of the Ancient Persian Empires

The first great multi-continental empire in world history: Cyrus the Great unites the Persians and Medes and, by 550 BCE, begins building a realm that will stretch from the Aegean to the Indus. This page follows Persia's ancient, pre-Islamic imperial arc — Achaemenid, then Alexander's conquest and its Seleucid and Parthian aftermath, then the Sasanian Empire that rivals Rome and Byzantium for four centuries — ending at the 651 CE Arab-Islamic conquest that closes the pre-Islamic chapter of Persian statehood for good.

This page hands off to iran-flags.html for Persia's later story — the Islamic caliphates, the Safavid dynasty that refounds a distinctly Persian state in 1501, and the path to the modern Islamic Republic of Iran. Splitting the two avoids duplicating content that Iran's own page already covers well.

Scope of this page: the ancient, pre-Islamic Persian imperial states, c. 550 BCE – 651 CE. Symbols predate the vector flag for virtually this entire span; every emblem below is an informed reconstruction of documented royal and religious symbolism — the Achaemenid faravahar and the Sasanian Derafsh Kaviani battle standard — not a surviving banner. 633 – 651 CE

Arab-Islamic conquest of Persia

Conquered

Who & why: Rashidun Caliphate armies defeat exhausted Sasanian forces at Qadisiyyah (636) and decisively at Nahavand (642), after decades of ruinous warfare between Persia and Byzantium had already weakened both empires. The last Sasanian king, Yazdegerd III, is killed near Merv in 651, ending over four centuries of Sasanian rule and closing the entire pre-Islamic Persian imperial arc.

What it represents: the closing point of this page's arc. Persia's continuing story — conversion to Islam over the following centuries, the Safavid dynasty's 1501 refounding of a distinctly Persian state, and the path to the modern Islamic Republic — is carried by this atlas's own Iran page.

224 – 651 CE

Sasanian Empire

Empire, Persian revival

Who & why: Ardashir I overthrows the last Parthian king in 224 CE, founding the Sasanian dynasty and deliberately reviving Achaemenid-style Persian imperial identity and Zoroastrian state religion. The Sasanians become Rome's (and later Byzantium's) great rival power for four centuries, fighting repeated wars over Mesopotamia, Armenia, and the Levant, reaching a final peak under Khosrow II before mutual exhaustion with Byzantium leaves both empires vulnerable to the rising Arab-Islamic conquests.

What it represents: the Derafsh Kaviani, a legendary battle standard said to originate from a blacksmith's leather apron turned rebel banner, becomes the Sasanian royal and military standard — richly jeweled according to later accounts, captured by Arab forces at Qadisiyyah in 636. Shown here as an informed reconstruction.

247 BCE – 224 CE

Parthian Empire

Empire, successor state

Who & why: the Parthians, an Iranian people from the northeast, break from Seleucid control under Arsaces I around 247 BCE and gradually expand to control the Persian heartland, famously defeating a Roman invasion at Carrhae (53 BCE) with their mounted archers. Parthia governs through a looser, more feudal structure than the centralized Achaemenids, ruling for nearly five centuries until internal rebellion under the Sasanian Ardashir I ends the dynasty in 224 CE.

What it represents: Parthian military identity centers on horse-archer cavalry and the composite bow — the source of the famous "Parthian shot" — along with continued use of some Hellenistic royal symbolism inherited from the Seleucids. Shown here as a reconstructed device.

312 – 247 BCE (Persian core)

Seleucid Empire (Persian territories)

Empire, Hellenistic successor

Who & why: after Alexander's death, his general Seleucus I Nicator secures the largest share of the former empire, including the Persian heartland, founding the Seleucid dynasty from 312 BCE. Seleucid rule over Iran proves harder to hold than the Mediterranean-facing parts of the empire; the Parthians steadily chip away at the eastern territories starting in the mid-3rd century BCE, with Persia itself largely lost to Parthian control by 247 BCE even as the Seleucids persist elsewhere for another two centuries.

What it represents: Seleucid coinage and standards commonly feature the anchor (a dynastic symbol tracing to Seleucus's own signet ring) and Hellenistic sun/star imagery, layered over continued local Persian administrative practice. Shown here as a reconstructed device.

334 – 330 BCE

Alexander's conquest

Conquered

Who & why: Alexander the Great of Macedon invades Achaemenid territory in 334 BCE, winning decisive victories at Issus (333) and Gaugamela (331); the last Achaemenid king, Darius III, is killed by his own satrap in 330 BCE while fleeing, ending the dynasty. Alexander's own empire fragments after his death in 323 BCE into rival successor kingdoms, the most important for Persia's own story being the Seleucid Empire.

What it represents: a hinge point rather than a symbol of its own — Achaemenid administrative structure and much of its royal symbolism is absorbed, adapted, and eventually Hellenized under Alexander's successors.

550 – 334 BCE

Achaemenid Empire

Empire, founding dynasty

Who & why: Cyrus the Great unites the Persians and Medes and, from 550 BCE, builds the first great multi-continental empire in history, stretching at its height from the Aegean and Egypt to the Indus Valley. Cyrus's capture of Babylon (539 BCE) and the Cyrus Cylinder proclamation — often cited as an early human-rights-adjacent document for its treatment of conquered peoples and religious tolerance — set a governing style his successors Darius I and Xerxes I build into a vast, administratively sophisticated empire, organized into satrapies and linked by the Royal Road.

What it represents: the faravahar, a winged disk with a central human figure, is the best-attested Achaemenid royal and Zoroastrian symbol, appearing on monumental reliefs at Persepolis representing divine favor over the king — shown here as an informed reconstruction, one of the most enduring symbols in Persian visual history.
