Flags of Kiribati

Long before any European sail crossed the Gilbert Islands, Micronesian communities navigated between the atolls by star and swell, each island keeping its own leadership and customs with no single flag or unified polity to speak for the whole chain. Britain formalized a protectorate over the group in 1892, folded it into a single colony with the Ellice Islands to the south, and let the Gilberts go their own way alone in 1979 — the newly independent Kiribati taking a Gilbertese rendering of the old colonial name for its own.

Kiribati is a geographic oddity among nations: it is the only country to straddle the equator and every line of longitude at once, so scattered are its 33 atolls across the central Pacific. In 1995 it redrew its own stretch of the International Date Line so the whole country could share a single calendar date — and today it is one of the places most existentially threatened by rising seas, with some of its low-lying atolls projected to become uninhabitable within this century.

Independent Republic of Kiribati

Independent state

Who & why: the Gilbert Islands gain independence from Britain on 12 July 1979, taking the name Kiribati — simply the Gilbertese rendering of "Gilberts." The new nation is a striking geographic outlier: its 33 scattered atolls straddle the equator and every line of longitude on Earth, and in 1995 Kiribati redrew its own stretch of the International Date Line so the whole country could finally share one calendar date. Low-lying and coral-built, it is today counted among the nations most existentially threatened by sea-level rise.

What it represents: the upper half is red, carrying a yellow frigatebird in flight above a yellow rising sun of seventeen rays, one for each of the Gilbert group's inhabited islands at independence; the lower half is blue-and-white wavy stripes standing for the Pacific Ocean that surrounds and connects them.

1892 – 1979

Gilbert Islands (British protectorate and colony)

Colony

Who & why: Britain declares a protectorate over the Gilbert Islands in 1892, formally annexing them as a colony in 1916. The Gilberts are then combined with the Ellice Islands (present-day Tuvalu) to the south into a single administration, the Gilbert and Ellice Islands Colony. The Ellice Islands vote to separate in a 1974–75 referendum and split off in 1975 as their own territory — their own path to independence belongs on Tuvalu's page in this atlas — leaving the Gilberts to proceed on to independence alone in 1979.

What it represents: the British Blue Ensign, with the Union Jack in the canton marking colonial administration and a round badge disc on the fly carrying the colony's own emblem, in the pattern flown across Britain's Pacific possessions of the era.

Indigenous Gilbertese (I-Kiribati) communities

Indigenous nations, pre-contact

Who & why: for centuries before British contact, the Micronesian population of the Gilbert Islands — the I-Kiribati — maintained distinct communities on each atoll, governed locally rather than under any single overarching authority, and sustained a deep tradition of open-ocean wayfinding and navigation between the scattered islands of the chain. No unified Gilbertese polity, and no single flag representing one, existed in this era.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from the pre-contact Gilbert Islands; flags as a form were not part of I-Kiribati tradition before European contact. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
