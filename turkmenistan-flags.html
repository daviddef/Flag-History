Flags of Turkmenistan

From the ancient Silk Road oasis of Merv — briefly one of the largest cities in the world under the Seljuks before the Mongols destroyed it — through centuries of self-governing Turkmen tribal confederations, the last Central Asian people conquered by Russia, Soviet rule, and finally a constitutionally neutral republic pursuing one of the world's most isolationist foreign policies.

Seven flags across a landscape more defined by tribe and oasis than by any single central state, until the 20th century imposed one.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Turkmenistan, antiquity–present, seven political entities. No interactive map or internal-region view is included here — that layer is deferred project-wide to a later phase. Part of a larger atlas — see the linked pages at the bottom.

1991 – present

Republic of Turkmenistan

Republic, independent

Who & why: Turkmenistan declared independence on 27 October 1991. In 1995 it adopted a constitutionally declared status of permanent neutrality, formally recognized by a United Nations General Assembly resolution, and has since pursued one of the world's most famously isolationist foreign policies.

What it represents: a green field with a vertical stripe near the hoist bearing five traditional carpet guls (weaving patterns, one representing each major Turkmen tribe), above a white crescent moon and five stars, adopted in 1992 with minor design adjustments in later years.

1924 – 1991

Turkmen SSR

Soviet republic

Who & why: the Turkmen SSR was created in 1924 in the Soviet "national delimitation" of Central Asia, carved from the former Turkestan ASSR and neighboring territories. It remained a Soviet republic until 1991.

What it represents: the standard Soviet red flag with a gold hammer and sickle and star in the canton, distinguished by a turquoise horizontal stripe near the base — a common pattern among the Central Asian SSR flags.

1917 – 1924

Turkestan ASSR (transitional)

Autonomous republic

Who & why: following the 1917 Russian Revolution and civil war, the Transcaspian region became part of the Turkestan ASSR, a post-revolutionary Soviet transitional administration, before the 1924 national delimitation carved out the Turkmen SSR above.

What it represents: the Soviet red flag with hammer-and-sickle emblem, flown across the transitional Turkestan ASSR administration.

Russian Transcaspian conquest and rule

Empire province

Who & why: the Turkmen were the last of the Central Asian peoples conquered by Russia. The decisive Battle of Geok Tepe in January 1881 broke Teke Turkmen resistance, and the region was organized as the Transcaspian Oblast of Russian Turkestan.

What it represents: the Russian imperial tricolor, flown across all territory under Russian imperial administration including the newly conquered Transcaspian region.

c. 1500s – 1881

Turkmen tribal confederations

Tribal confederations

Who & why: no central Turkmen state existed in this era. Instead, a patchwork of tribal confederations — the Teke, Yomut, Ersari, and others — governed themselves in practice, under the shifting nominal overlordship or influence of the neighboring Khanate of Khiva, the Emirate of Bukhara, and Persia (Safavid and later Qajar). This fragmented, non-unified span is treated here as one honest bridging entry rather than invented precise sub-periods, ending with Russian conquest in 1881.

What it represents: no single flag is documented for a landscape of self-governing tribal confederations rather than one central state; each tribe had its own carpet gul and tamga (clan emblem) instead. Shown here as an informed reconstruction of that tradition, not a documented banner.

Seljuk Merv, Mongol destruction, and successor rule

Empire / Sultanate

Who & why: the Silk Road oasis city of Merv, in present-day Turkmenistan, was a major Seljuk Empire capital in the 11th and 12th centuries, briefly one of the largest cities in the world. The Mongols destroyed Merv catastrophically in 1221, one of history's most devastating urban sacks, killing much of its population. The region was later absorbed into Timur's empire and its successors before Uzbek khanate influence extended over parts of it.

What it represents: no single fixed state flag is documented across this era's shifting Seljuk, Mongol, and Timurid-successor rule; shown here as an honest placeholder.

antiquity – c. 1040

Ancient Turkmen lands and early Merv

No flag documented

Who & why: the Kara-Kum oasis settlements, above all Merv, passed through Achaemenid Persian, Hellenistic (following Alexander's conquest), Parthian, and Sasanian-era rule before the early Islamic conquest of the 7th–8th century brought Merv into the caliphate as a major regional capital, a status it held into the Seljuk era above.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from this span; national flags as a concept postdate it entirely. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
