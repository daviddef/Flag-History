Flags of Haiti

Haiti's 1 January 1804 independence is a real, historically major first: the Haitian Revolution is the only successful large-scale slave revolt in history to found a sovereign nation, and Haiti became the second independent nation in the Americas, just decades after the United States. That founding act — won against Napoleonic France's own expeditionary army — makes everything on this page, however politically messy afterward, sit on genuinely exceptional ground.

What follows independence is honestly tangled: an empire, a kingdom-versus-republic civil split, a long reunified republic interrupted by a second empire, a 20th-century dictatorship that reached back to revolutionary colors, and the restored republic Haiti flies today. Six entries trace it, newest first.

Scope of this page: the western third of Hispaniola, c. 1492–present, six political entities. Haiti's own 1822–1844 occupation and unification of the eastern two-thirds of the island (the future Dominican Republic) doesn't get its own card here — it's Haiti ruling out, not a change to Haiti's own flag, so it's covered honestly in the prose of the entry it falls within instead. The brief Second Empire of Faustin I (1849–1859) gets the same treatment. Part of a larger atlas — see the linked pages at the bottom.

1986 – present

Restored Republic of Haiti

Republic

Who & why: Jean-Claude "Baby Doc" Duvalier flees the country on 7 February 1986, ending the 29-year Duvalier dictatorship. Among the first acts of the new order is restoring the traditional blue-and-red flag in place of the black-and-red banner the regime had imposed. Haiti's politics remain turbulent in the decades since — coups, foreign interventions, the 2010 earthquake, gang violence in the 2020s — but the flag itself has not changed again.

What it represents: the restored vertical blue-and-red bicolor with the central coat of arms — a palm tree, cannons and cannonballs, a Phrygian cap, and the motto "L'Union Fait La Force" ("Union Makes Strength") — Haiti's flag today.

1964 – 1986

Duvalier dictatorship (black-and-red flag)

Dictatorship

Who & why: François "Papa Doc" Duvalier, president from 1957, entrenches a repressive one-party regime backed by the paramilitary Tonton Macoute, declaring himself president-for-life in 1964. His son Jean-Claude "Baby Doc" succeeds him in 1971 and continues the same system. In 1964 Papa Doc changes the national flag's colors from blue-and-red to black-and-red.

What it represents: the change invokes Jean-Jacques Dessalines' short-lived 1804–06 Empire flag, recast as a nationalist and noirisme symbol tying the regime to Haiti's Black revolutionary founding rather than to the 19th- and 20th-century blue-and-red republic.

1820 – 1964

Reunified Republic of Haiti (blue-and-red bicolor)

Republic

Who & why: President Jean-Pierre Boyer reunifies Christophe's northern Kingdom and Pétion's southern Republic in 1820, then in February 1822 also occupies and unifies the Spanish-speaking eastern two-thirds of Hispaniola under Port-au-Prince, ruling the whole island until the Dominicans' 1844 independence breaks it apart again. This long stretch is not free of further interruption: the Second Empire of Haiti under Emperor Faustin I briefly revives a black-and-red flag between 1849 and 1859, before the 1859 revolution restores blue-and-red for good.

What it represents: the vertical blue-and-red bicolor with the central coat of arms becomes the settled national flag through this entire span, aside from that one black-and-red interruption under Faustin I — the same basic design later revived in 1986 and flown today.

1803 – 1820

Revolution, Empire, and civil split

Revolution / civil split

Who & why: this entry deliberately bridges a genuinely messy 17-year span rather than inventing false sub-boundaries. The Haitian Revolution, begun in 1791 under Toussaint Louverture (who dies in French captivity in 1803), reaches its climax when Jean-Jacques Dessalines tears the white from the French tricolor at the Congress of Arcahaie on 18 May 1803 — still celebrated as Haiti's Flag Day — creating a blue-and-red flag. Independence is declared 1 January 1804, renaming Saint-Domingue "Haiti": the second independent nation in the Americas and the only nation ever founded by a successful large-scale slave revolt. Dessalines proclaims himself Emperor Jacques I later in 1804, and the flag becomes black-and-red under the short-lived First Empire. After his assassination in 1806, the country splits: Henri Christophe's northern Kingdom of Haiti flies its own crowned-arms flag, while Alexandre Pétion's southern Republic of Haiti reverts to blue-and-red. The two are reunited only in 1820.

What it represents: the flag shown here is the original 1803 Arcahaie bicolor at the start of this span — blue and red, no coat of arms yet — standing in for a decade and a half that genuinely saw an empire, a kingdom, and a republic, in that order.

1697 – 1803

French Saint-Domingue

French colony

Who & why: the 1697 Treaty of Ryswick formally cedes the western third of Hispaniola — settled by French buccaneers and planters based on Tortuga since the 1620s–40s — to France as Saint-Domingue. By the 1780s it is the richest colony in the world, its sugar and coffee wealth built entirely on the brutal slave labor of enslaved Africans. That wealth, and that cruelty, are what set up the 1791 revolt.

What it represents: the plain white French royal and colonial ensign, bearing gold Bourbon fleurs-de-lis, the standard French flag of the period before the Revolution introduced the tricolor.

1492 – 1697

Spanish Hispaniola (western part)

Spanish colony

Who & why: Columbus's first permanent settlement in the Americas is founded on this island in 1492–93, and Spain claims the whole of Hispaniola. Its grip on the thinly-populated western third weakens steadily over the 17th century as French buccaneers and planters, based on Tortuga, move in from the 1620s–40s onward, a presence formally ceded to France in 1697.

What it represents: the Cross of Burgundy — a red diagonal saltire on white — the standard Spanish colonial and maritime banner, the same one flown at the same time over the eastern (future Dominican) side of the island, since Hispaniola was one Spanish possession until this split.
