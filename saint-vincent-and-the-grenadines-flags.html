Flags of Saint Vincent and the Grenadines

Saint Vincent's deepest history belongs to the Garifuna, a people of mixed West African and indigenous Kalinago descent who formed on this island through more than a century of intermarriage and shared resistance to European settlement. Britain's push to seize their land for sugar plantations after 1763 triggered two Carib Wars, and after the second of them the British rounded up roughly five thousand Garifuna, held them on the nearby islet of Balliceaux, and shipped the survivors into exile at Roatán off the coast of Honduras in 1797 — from where Garifuna communities spread along the coasts of Belize, Guatemala, Honduras, and Nicaragua, a diaspora that still marks Garifuna Settlement Day every April.

Five flags trace that arc: an indigenous era with nothing honestly claimed as a flag, a treaty-protected neutral period, a genuinely messy span of French interlude and Carib Wars under a nominally British claim, uncontested British colonial rule after the deportation, and finally the independent state of 1979.

Scope of this page: unlike the seven-country hub above, Saint Vincent and the Grenadines has no interactive traced map in this atlas — it is one of six Eastern Caribbean ministates profiled on their own pages without a GeoJSON map. The 1763–1797 span below is deliberately kept as a single bridging entry: the Anglo-French contest for the island and the two Carib Wars did not align neatly with the treaty dates, and this atlas prefers an honest, explained mess to invented precision.

1979 – present

Independent Saint Vincent and the Grenadines

Independent state

Who & why: Saint Vincent and the Grenadines gains independence from Britain on 27 October 1979, keeping the British monarch as head of state within the Commonwealth. The current flag was adopted in 1985, replacing a busier 1979 original; its three green diamonds, arranged in a bent line, are read locally as a stylized “V” for Vincent and as the “Gems of the Antilles.”

What it represents: blue for the sky and sea, gold for the warmth of the people and the Grenadines' sand, and green for the island's lush vegetation and agriculture — with the diamonds set on the widened center gold band.

British Saint Vincent (colonial period)

British colony

Who & why: following the Second Carib War and the 1797 mass deportation of roughly 5,000 Garifuna to Roatán, British control of Saint Vincent goes largely uncontested. Sugar, and later arrowroot — for which the island became a major world producer — are grown on enslaved and, after 1834, indentured labor; the island is part of the short-lived West Indies Federation from 1958 to 1962, late in this span, before moving toward self-government and eventual independence.

What it represents: the standard British colonial Blue Ensign, defaced on the fly with a badge depicting Saint Vincent's patron figure of Justice, flown over the colony through this entire era.

1763 – 1797

British Saint Vincent and the Carib Wars

British colony (Carib Wars)

Who & why: the 1763 Treaty of Paris cedes Saint Vincent to Britain outright, ending its earlier “neutral island” status. Britain's attempt to seize Garifuna land for sugar plantations sparks the First Carib War (1772–73) — before French forces recapture the island in 1779 during the American Revolutionary War, holding it until the 1783 Treaty of Paris restores it to Britain for good. The far larger Second Carib War (1795–96) follows: Garifuna leader Joseph Chatoyer, still remembered today as Saint Vincent's national hero, is killed in battle, and British victory leads directly to the 1797 deportation covered in the entry above. This flag is shown as the dominant claimant's colors for the whole span, not as documentation that British control was ever unbroken here — it plainly was not.

What it represents: the same British colonial ensign flown (intermittently, and contested) across a period that genuinely does not reduce to one clean flag or one clean set of dates.

1719/1748 – 1763

Anglo-French neutral island and Garifuna consolidation

Kalinago / Garifuna, neutral territory

Who & why: following the pattern of neighboring Dominica, France and Britain formally agreed, from 1660 and reaffirmed in 1748, to leave Saint Vincent as neutral territory under Kalinago and Garifuna control rather than fight over it directly. French planters and traders nonetheless settled informally and intermarried, contributing to the Garifuna ethnogenesis — the blending of Kalinago and West African ancestry, the latter partly from shipwrecked or escaped enslaved Africans who found refuge among the Kalinago from the 1600s onward — that gives the island its distinctive population by the time European rivalry intensifies again in the mid-1700s.

What it represents: no securely documented single flag survives for this self-governing indigenous period; shown honestly as a placeholder rather than invented.

Kalinago Saint Vincent, pre-Garifuna

Kalinago / pre-colonial

Who & why: Columbus sights the island in 1498, but it remains firmly under Kalinago control for over two centuries after contact, with no lasting European settlement. The specific Afro-indigenous Garifuna identity gradually forms over the course of the 17th century through contact and intermixture with shipwrecked and escaped enslaved Africans, setting up the consolidation described in the entry above.

What it represents: no documented flag survives from this earliest period; shown honestly as a placeholder rather than invented.
