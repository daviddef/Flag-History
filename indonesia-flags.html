Flags of Indonesia

From the maritime trading empire of Srivijaya, through Java's great Majapahit Empire, three and a half centuries of Dutch commercial and colonial rule, brutal Japanese wartime occupation, and a proclaimed 1945 independence that had to be won again over four more years of armed and diplomatic struggle — eight flags across the world's largest archipelagic nation.

Indonesia's own red-and-white national colors are not a modern invention: they echo the striped red-and-white war banner traditionally associated with the Majapahit Empire, deliberately revived by nationalists in the twentieth century to root the new republic in a pre-colonial past.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Indonesia, c. 650–present, eight political entities. Pre-colonial Indonesia was never a single unbroken state — Srivijaya and Majapahit were the two dominant maritime powers, but neither held the entire archipelago continuously, and a fragmented sultanate era separates them from Dutch arrival. 1949 – present

Republic of Indonesia (sovereignty recognized)

Republic, independent

Who & why: after four years of guerrilla warfare and international diplomatic pressure following the 1945 proclamation, the Netherlands formally transfers sovereignty at the Hague Round Table Conference on 27 December 1949. Indonesia goes on to absorb Dutch New Guinea in 1963 and East Timor in 1975–76 (the latter relinquished in 1999–2002), and transitions from Suharto's three-decade authoritarian "New Order" to multiparty democracy after his 1998 resignation.

What it represents: the same red-white bicolor flown since the 1945 proclamation, now internationally recognized rather than merely declared.

1945 – 1949

Proclaimed Republic of Indonesia (National Revolution)

Republic, contested

Who & why: Sukarno and Mohammad Hatta proclaim independence on 17 August 1945, two days after Japan's surrender, before the Dutch can reoccupy the archipelago. The Netherlands refuses to accept the proclamation and launches two large "police actions" (1947, 1948–49) to reassert colonial control; the republic survives through guerrilla resistance and mounting international pressure, particularly from the United States and the United Nations, until the Dutch relent.

What it represents: the red-white flag, raised at the moment of proclamation in Jakarta, deliberately revives the red-white war banner attributed to the Majapahit Empire — a symbolic claim to pre-colonial legitimacy.

1942 – 1945

Japanese military occupation

Occupation (Japan)

Who & why: Japanese forces overrun the Dutch East Indies in early 1942, ending Dutch rule within months and interning Dutch civilians and soldiers. Japan initially courts Indonesian nationalists, permitting limited use of the red-white flag and the Indonesian language late in the occupation as the war turns, and it is Japan's own August 1945 surrender that creates the opening Sukarno and Hatta use to proclaim independence.

What it represents: the Japanese naval and military ensign, flown over the occupied archipelago; no genuine Indonesian state flag flew during this period.

1800 – 1942

Dutch East Indies

Colonial (Netherlands)

Who & why: the bankrupt Dutch East India Company (VOC) is dissolved on 31 December 1799 and its territories nationalized as a formal Dutch colony on 1 January 1800. The colonial state expands over the nineteenth century into direct control of nearly the entire archipelago, run under the exploitative "Cultivation System" from the 1830s and later a nominally more liberal "Ethical Policy," while a broad nationalist movement builds through the early twentieth century.

What it represents: the plain Dutch tricolor, the colonial state's own flag, with no distinct East Indies national symbol.

1602 – 1800

Dutch East India Company (VOC) rule

Colonial (Dutch company)

Who & why: the Dutch East India Company, chartered in 1602 with quasi-sovereign powers to wage war and administer territory, gradually displaces Portuguese traders and local sultanates from the spice trade, establishing Batavia (modern Jakarta) in 1619 as its administrative and military headquarters. Company rule is commercial rather than fully territorial at first, tightening its grip archipelago by archipelago over two centuries before collapsing under debt and mismanagement in 1799.

What it represents: the Dutch tricolor bearing the Company's own interlocking VOC monogram, flown over trading posts and forts rather than a unified colony.

1527 – 1602

Fragmented sultanates (post-Majapahit)

Fragmented sultanates

Who & why: Majapahit's authority collapses in the early sixteenth century as Islam spreads through the archipelago via trade networks, and power fragments among rival coastal sultanates — Demak, later Mataram, on Java; Aceh in northern Sumatra; Banten in the west — none of which reunifies the whole archipelago before Dutch traders arrive.

What it represents: no single flag can honestly represent this genuinely fragmented span; each sultanate kept its own banners and none achieved archipelago-wide reach, so this entry is shown deliberately blank rather than inventing a false unifying symbol.

Gula Kelapa — the nine-striped red-white war banner attributed to Majapahit
c. 1293 – 1527

Majapahit Empire

Empire

Who & why: founded in 1293 by Raden Wijaya on the ruins of a failed Mongol invasion, Majapahit becomes the most powerful pre-colonial state in Indonesian history, claiming influence over much of the archipelago and parts of the wider Malay world at its height under Hayam Wuruk and his minister Gajah Mada in the mid-1300s. Its later fifteenth- and early sixteenth-century decline, hastened by succession conflicts and the rise of Islamic coastal trading states, opens the door to the fragmented sultanate era that follows.

What it represents: the Gula Kelapa ("sugar and coconut") banner, nine alternating red and white stripes, is the war flag traditionally attributed to Majapahit in Javanese chronicles — and the direct historical inspiration for the plain red-white flag Indonesia adopted in 1945.

c. 650 – 1293

Srivijaya

Empire

Who & why: centered on Sumatra near modern Palembang, Srivijaya rises in the mid-600s as a Buddhist maritime and trading empire, controlling the Strait of Malacca and much of the surrounding archipelago's sea trade for over six centuries. Its influence fades from the eleventh century under pressure from South Indian Chola raids and rising regional rivals, and its remaining territory is absorbed into the ascendant Majapahit sphere by the late 1200s.

What it represents: no securely documented flag or banner survives from Srivijaya; its power was expressed through temple inscriptions, tribute relationships, and naval control of trade routes rather than a fixed territorial symbol. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
