Flags of the Austrian Lands

From a Babenberg margraviate on the Danube frontier, through five and a half centuries as the seat of the Habsburg dynasty, an empire, a forced absorption into someone else's, and back to a small neutral republic — twelve flags, and one color scheme that outlasted every regime that tried to replace it.

Red-white-red is older than the Habsburgs and newer than their empire: legend traces it to Duke Leopold V's blood-soaked tunic at the 1191 Siege of Acre, and the 1918 republic revived it specifically to skip over the black-and-gold imperial flag the monarchy had flown since 1804.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Austria, c. 976–present, twelve political entities. Part of a larger atlas — see the map and linked pages at the bottom.

1955 – present

Republic of Austria

Sovereign state

Who & why: the 15 May 1955 State Treaty ends a decade of four-power occupation in exchange for Austria's permanent, constitutionally enshrined neutrality — the last Allied troops leave that October. Neutrality kept Austria out of NATO and the Warsaw Pact alike throughout the Cold War; it joined the EU in 1995 and today treats the neutrality clause as flexible rather than absolute.

What it represents: the same red-white-red first attested on Duke Leopold V's seal after the Third Crusade — legend holds his white surcoat was soaked crimson with blood except where a sword-belt had shielded a white stripe. The state flag adds a federal eagle (single-headed, holding a hammer, sickle, and broken chains, crowned with a mural crown) that the plain civil flag shown here omits.

1945 – 1955

Allied-Occupied Austria

Allied occupation

Who & why: the Moscow Declaration of 1943 had already promised to treat Austria as Hitler's "first victim" rather than a defeated enemy, and the Second Republic is declared the same month Vienna falls, April 1945. Even so, the country is split into American, British, French, and Soviet occupation zones (Vienna itself divided into sectors, like Berlin) for a full decade before the State Treaty ends it.

What it represents: the same red-white-red tricolor the republic had used since 1918/1919, reasserted immediately as a statement of continuity and distinctness from Nazi Germany — unlike Germany, Austria's occupation never produces a permanent partition, and all four zones are reunified at once in 1955.

1938 – 1945

Anschluss: the Ostmark

Occupation / annexation

Who & why: German troops cross the border on 12 March 1938; the Anschluss is proclaimed the next day and ratified by a rigged April referendum claiming over 99% approval. Austria is renamed "Ostmark" and administratively dissolved into Reichsgaue — Burgenland itself is abolished as a unit and split between neighboring Gaue. Austria ceases to exist as a state, not merely as an independent one, until 1945.

What it represents: no separate Austrian symbol is permitted; the swastika banner of the Reich flies over Vienna exactly as it does over Berlin. The Anschluss is the only period in this timeline when no red-white-red flag of any kind is flown anywhere in the country.

1934 – 1938

Federal State of Austria

Corporatist state

Who & why: Chancellor Engelbert Dollfuss suspends parliament in March 1933, crushes the Social Democrats in a brief civil war that February, and imposes a one-party corporatist constitution in May 1934 modeled partly on Mussolini's Italy. Dollfuss is assassinated by Austrian Nazis that July; his successor Kurt Schuschnigg holds the line, unsuccessfully, until the 1938 Anschluss.

What it represents: the republic's own red-white-red tricolor is kept, now paired with a redesigned federal eagle. The ruling Fatherland Front's own party symbol, the Kruckenkreuz (crutch cross), appears on party materials and propaganda but never replaces the state's own tricolor flag.

1919 – 1934

First Republic of Austria

Republic

Who & why: the September 1919 Treaty of Saint-Germain formally renames the state from "German-Austria" to simply "Austria," bars the word "German" from the name, and explicitly forbids union with Germany without League of Nations consent — a prohibition Austria's own parliament had voted to defy. Vienna, a Social Democratic stronghold ringed by conservative rural provinces, becomes its own federal state in 1922, a political fault line that runs through the whole interwar period.

What it represents: red-white-red, adopted the moment the monarchy fell, explicitly reaching back past 1804's imperial black-and-gold to the older, pre-imperial Austrian colors — a small act of political symbolism distancing the new republic from the Habsburgs even while keeping their oldest heraldic device.

1918 – 1919

State of German-Austria

Republic (unrecognized union)

Who & why: as Austria-Hungary disintegrates in October-November 1918, German-speaking deputies to the old imperial parliament declare the Republic of German-Austria on 12 November, its constitution explicitly naming it "a constituent part of the German Republic." The claimed territory includes German-speaking areas of Bohemia and South Tyrol that the new state never actually controls, and the Allies reject the union with Germany outright the following year.

What it represents: red-white-red is chosen immediately, the first government in centuries to fly it without an imperial eagle or Habsburg crown — a republic asserting continuity with the medieval Austrian lands while trying, unsuccessfully, to merge into a different nation entirely.

1867 – 1918

Austria-Hungary (Cisleithania)

Dynastic empire

Who & why: defeat by Prussia in 1866 forces Emperor Franz Joseph into the 1867 Ausgleich, splitting his realm into two co-equal halves under one monarch: Cisleithania (Austria and its Alpine and Bohemian crownlands) governed from Vienna, Transleithania (Hungary, including Burgenland's own territory) governed from Budapest. The assassination of Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo in 1914 sets off the war that ends the whole arrangement.

What it represents: the black Habsburg double-headed eagle on imperial gold, the dynasty's own house colors, distinct from the red-white-red used for the Austrian lands since medieval times — the empire's flag was always about the House of Habsburg-Lorraine first, the Austrian lands second.

1804 – 1867

Empire of Austria

Dynastic empire

Who & why: facing Napoleon's rise and anticipating the Holy Roman Empire's collapse, Francis II preemptively proclaims himself hereditary Emperor Francis I of Austria in 1804, two years before formally dissolving the thousand-year-old Empire in 1806. The new Austrian Empire is the first time the Habsburg lands are unified under one consistent, modern imperial title rather than a patchwork of separate crowns.

What it represents: the same black eagle on gold, now flying over a Habsburg realm that is a fully sovereign empire in its own right rather than an elected Holy Roman title. The Congress of Vienna in 1815, hosted by Austria's own chancellor Metternich, makes Vienna the arbiter of European affairs for a generation.

1453 – 1804

Archduchy of Austria

Habsburg crownland

Who & why: Archduke Rudolf IV's fabricated 1358–59 Privilegium Maius claims special archducal status for Austria, elevating it above ordinary duchies within the Empire; Emperor Frederick III finally confirms the title formally in 1453. For most of these three and a half centuries, "Archduke of Austria" is simply one of many titles held by whichever Habsburg also wears the Holy Roman imperial crown.

What it represents: the medieval red-white-red banner continues as the Austrian lands' own flag throughout the Habsburgs' rise to become the dominant dynasty of Central Europe — distinct from the Empire's own black-and-gold, and distinct again from whatever arms a given Habsburg ruler's other territories carried.

1282 – 1453

Duchy of Austria (Habsburg)

Duchy

Who & why: the Babenberg line dies out in 1246; after a contested interregnum, King Rudolf I of Habsburg defeats King Ottokar II of Bohemia at the 1278 Battle on the Marchfeld and enfeoffs his own sons with the Duchy of Austria in 1282 — the Habsburg dynasty's first territorial foothold, centuries before it became a byword for Central European empire.

What it represents: the new Habsburg dukes keep the existing Babenberg-era red-white-red banner rather than replacing it with their own ancestral colors, a choice that quietly outlasts every later change of imperial title.

1156 – 1282

Duchy of Austria (Babenberg)

Duchy

Who & why: the 1156 Privilegium Minus elevates the Babenberg margraviate to a full duchy under Henry II "Jasomirgott," who moves his seat to Vienna the same year. Duke Leopold V (r. 1177–1194) is the family's most famous member outside Austria itself — he captured Richard the Lionheart on his return from the Third Crusade in 1192, holding him for ransom.

What it represents: the red-white-red banner is traditionally dated to Leopold V's own Crusade service — a striking, easily painted design that becomes, and remains, the enduring symbol of the Austrian lands themselves, independent of whichever dynasty happens to rule them.

976 – 1156

Margraviate of Austria

March (frontier province)

Who & why: Holy Roman Emperor Otto II grants the March of Austria (Ostarrîchi, first attested in a document of 996 — the root of the modern name "Österreich") to Leopold I of Babenberg in 976, establishing a frontier march against Magyar raids along the Danube. The Babenbergs hold it continuously as loyal imperial vassals for the next two centuries.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from this pre-heraldic era, before fixed armorial devices became standard practice; the Babenberg margraves used personal and ecclesiastical banners, not a fixed territorial one. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
