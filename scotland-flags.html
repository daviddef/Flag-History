Flags of Scotland

One of the oldest national flags still in use anywhere on earth: the Saltire's own origin is wrapped in a battlefield legend nearly twelve centuries old, but its documented life as a national badge runs, unbroken and largely unchanged, through an independent medieval kingdom, a king who inherited a second crown without giving up the first, a forced merger of parliaments, and three centuries of devolution debates that end, in 1999, with a Scottish Parliament sitting again.

Unlike many of the flags in this atlas, the Saltire was never replaced — only, at times, overshadowed by a Union Flag flown alongside or above it. This page tracks both: Scotland's own blue-and-white cross, and the wider constitutional arrangements that determined which flag actually flew where.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Scotland, c. 843–present, six political entities. The Royal Standard (Lion Rampant) appears here as the monarch's personal banner, distinct from the Saltire as the national/civic flag — both are shown where relevant. No internal governing split exists within Scotland's own modern borders at any point covered here, so this page carries no internal-partition map. 1999 – present

Scotland (devolved nation, Scottish Parliament restored)

Part of the United Kingdom

Who & why: the Scotland Act 1998, following a decisive September 1997 referendum, re-establishes a Scottish Parliament in Edinburgh, which convenes in 1999 for the first time since 1707 — with devolved powers over health, education, justice, and much else, though foreign affairs, defence, and the core of the constitution remain reserved to Westminster. A further independence referendum in September 2014 sees Scotland vote 55–45 to stay in the United Kingdom.

What it represents: the Saltire, already Scotland's undisputed national flag for centuries, takes on renewed civic prominence after devolution — flown over the new Parliament building and government buildings, alongside the Union Flag as the state flag of the UK as a whole.

1707 – 1999

Scotland (in the united Kingdom of Great Britain / UK)

Part of the United Kingdom

Who & why: the Acts of Union, in force from 1 May 1707, dissolve the separate Scottish and English Parliaments into one Parliament of Great Britain at Westminster — a real political merger, not just the shared crown of a century earlier. Scotland keeps its own legal system, established Presbyterian church, and education system by treaty guarantee, but the Union Flag becomes the state flag of the merged kingdom (later the United Kingdom, from 1801). The Saltire continues to be flown as Scotland's own national and civic flag throughout, though without the same official status as the state flag.

What it represents: the Union Flag, first devised in 1606 for James VI/I and updated in 1801 with the addition of St Patrick's cross, is the flag of the state Scotland belongs to for this entire span; the Saltire persists underneath it as the nation's own symbol.

1603 – 1707

Kingdom of Scotland (Union of the Crowns)

Kingdom, independent

Who & why: when Elizabeth I dies childless in March 1603, the English succession passes to her cousin James VI of Scotland, who becomes James I of England as well — the Union of the Crowns. One person now wears both crowns, but Scotland and England remain legally separate kingdoms: separate parliaments, separate law, separate established churches. James's attempt to formally merge them as a single “Great Britain” is rejected by both parliaments in 1606–07. In 1606 James also orders a new combined flag — the first Union Flag — for use mainly at sea, without replacing Scotland's own Saltire on land.

What it represents: the Saltire remains Scotland's own flag throughout this period, flown domestically even as the shared monarch's new Union Flag appears on ships and in some official contexts.

1249 – 1603

Kingdom of Scotland (independent medieval & early modern kingdom)

Kingdom, independent

Who & why: the Lion Rampant is first securely attested as the royal arms under Alexander II and Alexander III in the mid-13th century. This span covers the Wars of Independence — the 1296–1328 struggle against English overlordship led first by William Wallace and then Robert the Bruce, whose victory at Bannockburn (1314) and the 1328 Treaty of Edinburgh–Northampton confirm Scottish sovereignty — through the Stewart/Stuart dynasty's long reign, right up to James VI's 1603 succession to the English throne. Scotland remains a fully independent kingdom with its own parliament and law throughout.

What it represents: the Royal Standard (Lion Rampant, a red lion within a red double tressure on gold) is the monarch's personal banner, legally restricted to royal use even today; the Saltire, meanwhile, is in growing use nationally across this same period as the country's own flag — a 1385 ordinance requires Scottish soldiers to wear a white St Andrew's cross as a field sign, the earliest solid documentary evidence of the saltire as a national badge.

843 – 1249

Kingdom of Alba / early Kingdom of Scotland

Kingdom, independent

Who & why: Kenneth MacAlpin unites the Picts and Scots (Dál Riata) into the Kingdom of Alba around 843, the traditional starting point for a continuous Scottish crown; the kingdom is consolidated and expanded over the following centuries under Malcolm II, Duncan I, and Malcolm III, bringing Lothian and Strathclyde firmly under one crown by the 11th century. This entry closes with the accession of Alexander III in 1249, after whom royal heraldry becomes securely attested.

What it represents: Scottish tradition traces the Saltire to a legendary 832 battle near Athelstaneford, where King Óengus II, facing a larger Anglo-Saxon force, is said to have seen a cross of white clouds against the blue sky and taken it as a sign of St Andrew's favor before his victory. No contemporary record confirms the story — the earliest surviving accounts date from centuries later — but it is the traditional origin still cited for Scotland's flag and is shown here as the legend, not as a documented 9th-century banner.

c. 500 – 843

Fragmented Picts, Scots, Britons, and Angles

Fragmented kingdoms

Who & why: before Kenneth MacAlpin's unification, what is now Scotland is divided among several distinct peoples and kingdoms: the Picts in the north and east, the Gaelic Scots of Dál Riata in the west (traditionally settled from Ireland from around the 5th–6th century), Britons in the southwest kingdom of Strathclyde, and Angles in the southeast (Bernicia, later Northumbria). No single crown rules all of them, and centuries of shifting alliances and wars — including Pictish-Scottish dynastic intermarriage — eventually set up Kenneth MacAlpin's union of the Picts and Scots around 843.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from this pre-heraldic era; early medieval Britain used carved symbol stones, war banners, and religious imagery rather than fixed territorial flags, and none can be attributed with confidence to a single one of these peoples. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
