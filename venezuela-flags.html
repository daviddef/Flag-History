Flags of Venezuela

Venezuela's coast was a Spanish colonial backwater for most of the empire's run — a captaincy general administered first from Santo Domingo and later Bogotá, with none of the gold or silver wealth that made Peru or Mexico crown priorities. That changed abruptly on 19 April 1810, when Caracas became the first city in Spanish America to depose its royal governor and set up an independent junta, seven years before Bolívar's own decisive victories, making Venezuela the true starting point of the wars that would eventually free the whole continent.

Caracas is also, not incidentally, Simón Bolívar's own birthplace. Venezuela's independence, its brief union with Colombia and Ecuador in Gran Colombia, and its final split into a separate republic in 1830 all trace back to the same man and the same decade.

Scope of this page: the territory of present-day Venezuela, c. 1500–present, five political entities. No internal governing partition exists within Venezuela's own modern borders after independence, so this page carries no partition map. Part of a larger atlas — see the linked pages at the bottom.

1830 – present

Republic of Venezuela (modern, separate state)

Republic, independent

Who & why: regional resistance to rule from distant Bogotá, led by General José Antonio Páez, pulls Venezuela out of Gran Colombia in 1830, the same year Bolívar dies; it re-forms as its own separate republic. Venezuela cycles through 19th-century caudillo rule and instability, a long 20th-century oil boom that made it briefly one of Latin America's wealthiest states, and since 1999 Hugo Chávez's "Bolivarian Revolution" and its successor government under Nicolás Maduro, alongside a severe economic and migration crisis in the 2010s–20s.

What it represents: the same yellow-blue-red tricolor inherited from Gran Colombia, with a star added for each founding province — seven originally, rising to eight in 2006 under Chávez to mark the historic province of Guayana — arranged in an arc.

1819 – 1830

Gran Colombia

Union republic

Who & why: Bolívar's 1819 victory at Boyacá, fought to liberate New Granada, sets up the Congress of Angostura's proclamation of Gran Colombia that same year, uniting Venezuela, New Granada (Colombia), and Ecuador — plus, briefly, Panama — into a single republic. Venezuela's own liberation follows with Bolívar's 1821 victory at Carabobo. The union struggles with the same distances and regional rivalries that undid Spain's own empire, and collapses within the decade.

What it represents: the yellow-blue-red tricolor originally devised by Venezuelan-born Francisco de Miranda for his own 1806 independence expedition, adopted as Gran Colombia's flag and the shared ancestor of Venezuela's, Colombia's, and Ecuador's later national flags.

1810 – 1819

First and Second Republics of Venezuela (independence war)

Independence war

Who & why: Caracas deposes its Spanish governor on 19 April 1810, setting up a junta that formally declares full independence on 5 July 1811 — the First Republic, the first in Spanish America. A devastating 1812 earthquake and royalist counterattack crush it within the year; Bolívar's 1813 "Admirable Campaign" restores a Second Republic, which also falls to royalist forces by 1814. Years of brutal back-and-forth warfare follow until Bolívar's decisive 1821 victory at the Battle of Carabobo secures Venezuela for good.

What it represents: the yellow-blue-red tricolor Francisco de Miranda first raised in 1806 during his own failed independence expedition, adopted by the 1811 First Republic and flown, with interruptions, through the war years that follow.

1777 – 1810

Captaincy General of Venezuela (Spanish colonial rule)

Colonial territory

Who & why: after two and a half centuries administered piecemeal from Santo Domingo and later the Viceroyalty of New Granada, Spain consolidates its Venezuelan provinces into a single Captaincy General of Venezuela in 1777, seated at Caracas, giving the territory its own coherent administrative identity for the first time. The colony's economy runs on cacao exports and a rigid caste system; by the 1800s a native-born Creole elite — including the young Simón Bolívar's own family, wealthy cacao planters — grows increasingly resentful of rule from Madrid, setting up the 1810 break.

What it represents: Spain flew no single fixed colonial flag across its American territories; the red saltire (cross of Burgundy) on white was the common Spanish military and naval ensign of the era, shown here schematically alongside the captaincy seal's gold sun to represent Spanish crown authority, not a specific Venezuelan banner.

c. 1500 – 1777

Indigenous coastal peoples and early Spanish provinces

Fragmented / early colonial

Who & why: before and during early Spanish contact, the Venezuelan coast and interior are home to scattered indigenous groups — Caquetío, Cumanagoto, and others — with no single unifying state. Spanish colonization begins slowly and unevenly from the 1520s, with Coro and Cumaná among the earliest settlements (Cumaná, founded 1521, is among the oldest continuously inhabited European-founded settlements in South America), administered as loosely connected provinces rather than one coherent colony until the 1777 captaincy-general reform.

What it represents: no securely documented territorial flag survives from this fragmented indigenous and early-contact era. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
