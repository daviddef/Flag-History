Flags of Libya

Libya's modern flag history is defined by two remarkable bookends: in 1951 it became the first country ever brought to independence directly by the United Nations, and in 2011 it became the only country in this atlas whose national revolution was fought, in part, to bring back a flag — the tricolor of the 1951 kingdom, hauled out of exile as the banner of the uprising against Muammar Gaddafi. Between Ottoman rule, Italian colonization, a brief Allied military administration, a monarchy, and Gaddafi's 42-year Jamahiriya, this page traces seven eras.

Gaddafi's 1969 coup eventually produced the only national flag in modern history to be a single plain color with no design of any kind — a solid green field flown from 1977 to 2011, representing his political philosophy rather than any traditional heraldic device.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Libya, c. 900–present, seven political entities. Part of a larger atlas — see the map and linked pages at the bottom.

This page doesn't split internally, even though Libya's own history would support it. Tripolitania, Cyrenaica, and Fezzan were governed separately for long stretches — including the 1943–51 Allied administration, when Britain ran the first two regions and France ran Fezzan under entirely different flags. But no single Libyan flag existed for any of those separately-run pieces; the division was administrative, not symbolic, so a map split would invent borders no flag ever marked. The story below flags that exception honestly instead.

2011 – present

Libya (post-revolution)

Republic, independentMustafa Abdul JalilSunni Islam (constitutional state religion)

Who & why: protests beginning in February 2011, part of the wider Arab Spring, escalate into a full civil war; NATO intervenes under a UN mandate, and Gaddafi is captured and killed near Sirte in October 2011, ending his 42-year rule. Libya has since struggled with fragmented governance and recurring conflict between rival authorities, a genuine and difficult aftermath stated here factually.

What it represents: revolutionaries deliberately readopt the 1951 kingdom's red-black-green tricolor with its white crescent and star as their banner from the earliest days of the uprising, and it becomes Libya's official flag again upon the fall of Gaddafi's government.

1977 – 2011

Great Socialist People's Libyan Arab Jamahiriya

Personalist ruleMuammar GaddafiSunni Islam (constitutional state religion)

Who & why: Muammar Gaddafi seizes power in a September 1969 coup against King Idris, and by 1977 declares Libya a "Jamahiriya" (state of the masses) under his idiosyncratic "Third International Theory," ruling for 42 years until the 2011 revolution ends his government.

What it represents: a plain green field, adopted in 1977 as a deliberate break from all traditional flag design and a reference to Gaddafi's political manifesto, the Green Book — the only national flag in modern history to carry no emblem, pattern, or additional color whatsoever.

1969 – 1977

Libyan Arab Republic / Federation of Arab Republics

Personalist ruleMuammar GaddafiSunni Islam (state religion)

Who & why: Gaddafi's Revolutionary Command Council replaces the monarchy's flag with a red-white-black tricolor (mirroring Egypt's own republican flag) after the 1969 coup, then briefly joins the 1972–77 Federation of Arab Republics, a loose and short-lived pan-Arab union with Egypt and Syria that shared a common hawk-emblem flag before dissolving.

What it represents: a period of rapid, symbolically pan-Arab flag changes bridging the monarchy's fall and Gaddafi's later, more idiosyncratic green banner — shown here as one honest bridging entry rather than several thinly-documented sub-flags.

1951 – 1969

Kingdom of Libya

Kingdom, independentIdris IIslam (constitutional state religion)

Who & why: the United Nations General Assembly resolves in 1949 to grant Libya independence, and on 24 December 1951 it becomes fully sovereign under King Idris — the first country ever brought to independence by the UN, unifying Tripolitania, Cyrenaica, and Fezzan into a single federal (later unitary) kingdom. Oil discoveries in the late 1950s transform its economy before Gaddafi's 1969 coup ends the monarchy.

What it represents: a new tricolor designed for independence, based on the flag of the wartime Cyrenaican resistance, with a white crescent and star recalling the Sanussi order's own banner — the flag Libya's 2011 revolutionaries would later revive.

1943 – 1951

Allied Military Administration

Occupation / administrationNo single ruler documentedNo official state religion (Sunni Islam predominant)

Who & why: Allied forces expel Italy from Libya in 1943; Britain administers Tripolitania and Cyrenaica while France separately administers the southern Fezzan region, an honestly split arrangement that persists until UN-sponsored independence talks conclude in 1951.

What it represents: no single Libyan flag exists in this transitional period — British and French administrative authority is exercised under their own respective flags region by region, shown here honestly as a divided administration rather than one invented banner.

1911 – 1943

Italian Libya

Colonial ruleVictor Emmanuel IIINo official state religion imposed (Sunni Islam predominant)

Who & why: Italy invades Ottoman Tripolitania and Cyrenaica in 1911, formally annexing the territory in 1912 and consolidating it under the name "Libya" from 1934 — the first use of that name for the unified territory in modern times. Italian rule includes brutal suppression of Sanussi-led resistance in Cyrenaica through the 1920s–30s before Allied forces end it in 1943.

What it represents: the Italian Tricolor, flown as the colony's sole official flag throughout Italian rule.

c. 900 – 1911

Ottoman Tripolitania, Cyrenaica & Fezzan

Ottoman ruleNo single ruler documentedSunni Islam (established, across this Islamic-era span)

Who & why: earlier medieval rule by various Berber and Arab dynasties, and a brief 16th-century period under the Knights of Malta and Spain at Tripoli, gives way to direct Ottoman conquest in 1551. From 1711 to 1835 the local Karamanli dynasty rules as a largely autonomous Ottoman vassal before direct Ottoman control is reimposed until the 1911 Italian invasion.

What it represents: the standard Ottoman red field with white crescent and star, flown across this territory’s long Ottoman period, including through the semi-autonomous Karamanli years.

Reading the tags

Ottoman rule — direct or vassal, under the Sultan
Colonial rule / occupation — governed by a foreign power
Kingdom / Republic, independent — sovereign under its own government
Personalist rule — single-leader regime, revolutionary legitimacy
Leader — the named ruler most associated with the era's start
State religion — the official/established faith, not the population's
