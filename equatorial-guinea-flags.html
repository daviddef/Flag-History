Flags of Equatorial Guinea

From the indigenous Bubi people of Bioko island and Fang communities of the Rio Muni mainland, through a nominal Portuguese claim and then nearly two centuries of Spanish rule — the only part of sub-Saharan Africa Spain ever colonized, the reason Spanish remains the country's official language today — to independence in 1968 and, within a year, one of postcolonial Africa's most brutal and enduring dictatorships.

The country's flag itself has stayed remarkably constant since independence: green-white-red horizontal bands with a blue hoist triangle and the national coat of arms, tweaked only in its shield's details as governments changed, never wholesale.

Scope of this page: the territory of present-day Equatorial Guinea (Bioko island, Annobón, and the Rio Muni mainland), precolonial societies to present, four political-flag eras. Spain's own colonial-era national flag varied in its coat of arms as Spanish governments changed (constitutional monarchy, Second Republic, Francoist dictatorship) but kept the same red-yellow-red field throughout the colonial period, so it is shown here as one entry rather than several. Republic of Equatorial Guinea

Republic, independent

Who & why: independence from Spain arrives on 12 October 1968 under President Francisco Macías Nguema, whose rule quickly turns into one of postcolonial Africa's most extreme dictatorships — mass killings, forced labor, and the flight of roughly a third of the population into exile before his own nephew, Teodoro Obiang Nguema Mbasogo, overthrows and executes him in a 1979 coup. State factually: Obiang has ruled ever since, making his one of the longest tenures of any head of state in the world, with oil wealth discovered in the 1990s doing little to change the country's governance. The flag, adopted at independence, has survived both regimes essentially unchanged, its shield redesigned in detail (a variant silk-cotton tree emblem) without altering the overall design.

What it represents: green for the mainland's vegetation, white for peace, red for the blood of those who died winning independence, and the blue hoist triangle for the sea linking the mainland and island territories; the central coat of arms bears a silk-cotton tree under which early treaties with Spain were reputedly signed.

Spanish Guinea

Colonial territory

Who & why: the 1778 Treaty of El Pardo transfers Bioko island (then Fernando Póo), Annobón, and claims on the nearby mainland from Portugal to Spain in exchange for territory in South America, but Spain does not establish effective control for over a century — early attempts falter against disease and resistance, and Britain leases Bioko as an anti-slave-trade naval base for decades in between. Spain reasserts and expands direct administration from the 1850s–1900s, formally organizing Spanish Guinea (Bioko, Annobón, and the Rio Muni mainland, home to the Fang) as a colony in the early 20th century, run as a cocoa and timber economy under forced-labor practices that persist into the Franco era. Independence movements gain little traction under Spain's own Francoist dictatorship, and independence itself, in 1968, arrives more from decolonization pressure at the United Nations than from strong local nationalist momentum.

What it represents: Spain's own civil flag, the red-yellow-red field fixed since 1785 and flown over the colony throughout Spanish rule; the central coat of arms varied with the government in Madrid (constitutional monarchy, the 1931–39 Second Republic's differently arranged tricolor, and Franco's eagle-bearing arms from 1938) but is omitted here as a plain civil ensign.

Portuguese claim (Bioko and Annobón)

Colonial territory

Who & why: Portuguese navigators reach Bioko island around 1471 and Annobón slightly later, claiming both under the general Portuguese sphere established by the 1494 Treaty of Tordesillas; the Rio Muni mainland is not meaningfully claimed by either Iberian crown during this period. Portuguese presence on the islands themselves remains thin, focused on sugar plantations that decline by the 17th century, with the indigenous Bubi population of Bioko's interior remaining largely autonomous and unconquered throughout.

What it represents: Portugal's own national flag of the era, flown nominally over its island claims; effective Portuguese governance of the interior was minimal for most of this span.

Bubi and Fang precolonial societies

Fragmented societies

Who & why: before European contact, Bioko island is home to the Bubi people, who migrated from the mainland centuries earlier and organized into independent clan-based chiefdoms, while the Rio Muni mainland is home to Fang and other Bantu-speaking peoples with their own decentralized lineage-based societies. Neither maintained any single overarching state, and Bubi communities in particular remained largely independent of outside control well into the colonial era.

What it represents: no unified flag existed for either the island or mainland populations before colonization. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
