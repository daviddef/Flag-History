Flags of Seychelles

Uninhabited until French settlers arrived in 1756, the Seychelles archipelago was built as a plantation economy on enslaved and later indentured labor under two colonial powers, changed hands repeatedly during the Napoleonic Wars without ever really leaving French administration until the final British capture held, and reached independence in 1976 only to be overtaken within a year by a one-party socialist state that lasted two decades before a peaceful return to multiparty democracy.

Five flags trace this lineage — French, then British, then three distinct Seychellois national flags across the past half-century, more flag changes for its size than almost anywhere else in this atlas.

Scope of this page: the granitic and outer islands of Seychelles, from uninhabited French settlement in 1756 to present, five political-flag eras. No indigenous population predates 1756, so this page carries no precolonial "no flag documented" entry — the lineage begins directly with French colonization. 1996 – present

Third Republic of Seychelles

Republic, multipartyFrance-Albert RenéSecular (no state religion)

Who & why: under growing domestic and international pressure, France-Albert René's government agrees to end one-party rule, and a new 1993 constitution restores multiparty elections, which René wins; a further constitutional revision brings the current flag in 1996. René remains president until voluntarily stepping down in 2004, and Seychelles has held competitive multiparty elections continuously since, developing into one of Africa's wealthiest states per capita on the strength of tourism and, more recently, fisheries and offshore finance.

What it represents: five oblique bands fanning out from the lower hoist — blue for sky and sea, yellow for the sun, red for the people's unity and future, white for social justice and harmony, and green for the land and natural environment — a deliberately dynamic design symbolizing a nation moving forward into a new era.

1977 – 1996

One-party socialist republic

One-party stateFrance-Albert RenéSecular (no state religion)

Who & why: just over a year after independence, Prime Minister France-Albert René seizes power in a bloodless coup on 5 June 1977 while President James Mancham is abroad, and establishes a one-party socialist state aligned loosely with the Non-Aligned Movement and, at times, the Eastern Bloc. René's Seychelles People's Progressive Front nationalizes land and key industries and survives several coup and mercenary-invasion attempts, most notably a failed 1981 raid by South African mercenaries posing as tourists, before economic pressure and the end of the Cold War push René toward accepting multiparty reform in the early 1990s.

What it represents: the ruling party's own colors — red for the socialist revolution, green for agriculture and the land — with a gold palm tree and star in the canton standing for the nation and its aspirations under the new one-party order.

1976 – 1977

Independence, first republic

Republic, independentJames ManchamSecular (no state religion)

Who & why: Seychelles becomes independent from Britain on 29 June 1976 as a republic within the Commonwealth, with James Mancham, leader of the pro-Western Seychelles Democratic Party, as its first president in a coalition arrangement with René's more left-leaning party. The arrangement proves short-lived: René ousts Mancham within a year, on 5 June 1977, ending this brief first-republic era.

What it represents: green for the land, white for peace and purity, and red for the people's dynamism, with the national coat of arms — featuring a giant tortoise, a symbol closely associated with the islands — centered on the white band.

1810 – 1976

British colonial rule

Colonial territoryNo single ruler documentedAnglican (favored), Catholic majority

Who & why: Britain seizes Seychelles from France for the final time in 1810 during the Napoleonic Wars, with French planters' property and the French-derived Seychellois Creole culture and language left largely intact under the terms of capitulation; the 1814 Treaty of Paris formalizes British possession. Seychelles is administered as a dependency of Mauritius until becoming its own separate British Crown Colony in 1903. The plantation economy, now centered on coconut, vanilla, and cinnamon, continues to rely on the descendants of enslaved and, after abolition, indentured laborers well into the 20th century. A local Legislative Council is introduced in the 1940s–60s, and Britain grants independence in 1976.

What it represents: the standard British colonial pattern — a defaced Blue Ensign bearing the territory's own badge — flown as the colonial flag; the specific Seychelles badge is not securely attested before the 1903 separation from Mauritius.

1756 – 1810

French colonial rule

Colonial territoryNicolas MorpheyRoman Catholic (colonial establishment)

Who & why: France formally claims the previously uninhabited archipelago in 1756, naming it for Jean Moreau de Séchelles, and begins settlement with enslaved African labor to build a plantation economy from the 1770s onward. During the Napoleonic Wars, the islands' resourceful civil administrator, Jean-Baptiste Quéau de Quincy, adopts a policy of capitulating to any passing British warship while quietly continuing French civil administration underneath — Seychelles changes hands on paper repeatedly between 1794 and 1810 without its day-to-day French character ever really breaking, until Britain's final capture in 1810 makes the change permanent.

What it represents: France's own royal ensign, plain white bearing the Bourbon fleur-de-lis, flew for most of this period; the Revolutionary Tricolor arrives from 1794 onward as France's flag changes at home, though Quincy's improvised local administration makes the practical colors flown day to day somewhat uncertain in the final years before 1810.
