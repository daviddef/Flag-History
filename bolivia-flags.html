Flags of Bolivia

Bolivia is one of a very small number of countries in the world named directly after a real person: on independence in 1825, the new republic takes the name of Simón Bolívar, who himself declines its presidency in favor of his lieutenant Antonio José de Sucre. The territory's history runs far deeper than that founding moment — through the Tiwanaku civilization around Lake Titicaca, the Inca Empire's southeastern Qullasuyu province, and nearly two and a half centuries of Spanish rule as “Upper Peru” (Alto Perú), built on the silver wealth of Potosí and administered for most of that span under the Viceroyalty of Peru before a late-colonial transfer to the Viceroyalty of Río de la Plata.

Bolivia's modern history also carries a defining, still-sensitive loss: the War of the Pacific (1879–84), in which Bolivia and its ally Peru were defeated by Chile, and Bolivia surrendered its entire Pacific coastline, becoming permanently landlocked. That loss is stated here factually and directly, as the atlas project's standards require, because it is central to how Bolivia understands its own history.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Bolivia, c. 1400s (Inca-era Qullasuyu) – present, five political entries. No internal governing split exists within Bolivia's own modern borders at any point covered here, so like some other single-lineage pages in this atlas, this one carries no internal-partition map. 1825 – present

Republic / Plurinational State of Bolivia

Republic

Who & why: Simón Bolívar declines the presidency of the new republic, and Antonio José de Sucre becomes its first president in 1825; the country is named “Bolivia” directly after Bolívar, one of very few nations in the world named after a real person. The War of the Pacific (1879–84) against Chile, fought alongside allied Peru, ends in defeat: Bolivia loses its entire Pacific coastline and becomes landlocked, a defining and still keenly felt national loss. The country's official name changes to the “Plurinational State of Bolivia” in 2009 under a new constitution, without altering the flag itself.

What it represents: the horizontal red-yellow-green triband, with colors fixed in 1851 and current proportions and details set in 1888, has remained stable ever since. The plain civil flag shown here omits the national coat of arms that sits centered on the state and war-ensign versions.

1809 – 1825

War of independence (Upper Peru)

Revolutionary war

Who & why: Upper Peru sees some of the earliest revolutionary stirrings anywhere in Spanish America — the 1809 Chuquisaca and La Paz uprisings are among the first in the region — but the territory remains under royalist control longer than most of its neighbors, owing to its remoteness and the strength of royalist forces garrisoned there. Independence is only secured in 1825, after Antonio José de Sucre's forces, continuing Bolívar's broader campaign of liberation, defeat the last royalist holdouts.

What it represents: no single fixed flag governs this transitional period; insurgent and royalist forces in Upper Peru fought under a variety of banners, including early versions of the United Provinces of Río de la Plata's flag and improvised local standards. Shown here honestly as undocumented rather than invented.

1776 – 1809

Upper Peru / Charcas, Viceroyalty of Río de la Plata

Colony

Who & why: in 1776 the territory, administered as the Audiencia of Charcas, is transferred from the Viceroyalty of Peru to the newly created Viceroyalty of the Río de la Plata, reflecting Buenos Aires's growing importance in Atlantic trade. Potosí's silver mines had already made this one of the wealthiest, most heavily populated parts of the Spanish empire for two centuries before that administrative shift.

What it represents: the Cross of Burgundy (a red saltire on white), the common Spanish colonial and military banner of the period, shown here in simplified straight-line form rather than its traditional jagged, knotted-branch rendering.

1559 – 1776

Audiencia of Charcas, Viceroyalty of Peru

Colony

Who & why: the Real Audiencia of Charcas is established in 1559, governing “Upper Peru” as part of the Viceroyalty of Peru. Potosí, founded around its famous silver mountain (Cerro Rico), discovered in 1545, becomes one of the largest and wealthiest cities in the world during this period, its silver output critical to the Spanish crown's finances.

What it represents: the same Cross of Burgundy colonial banner used across the Spanish empire, flown here over Charcas through more than two centuries of Viceroyalty of Peru administration.

c. 1400s – 1559

Inca Qullasuyu and the earlier Tiwanaku civilization

No unified polity

Who & why: the Tiwanaku civilization flourishes around Lake Titicaca from roughly 300–1000 CE, one of the most significant pre-Inca Andean civilizations. The region is later incorporated into the Inca Empire as its southeastern Qullasuyu province, one of the empire's four quarters, before Spanish conquest in the 1530s–40s brings the territory under colonial rule, culminating in the Audiencia of Charcas's establishment in 1559.

What it represents: no securely documented fixed flag survives from either the Tiwanaku or Inca-Qullasuyu era. The modern multicolored checkerboard “Wiphala” flag, widely associated with Andean indigenous identity today, is a modern indigenous political symbol first documented in the 20th century, not a surviving ancient banner — shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
