Flags of Ecuador

Quito sat at the seat of a northern province of the Inca Empire — but only just: the highland kingdoms around it were folded into Cusco's rule mere decades before the Spanish arrived, a conquest far more recent and far less settled than the Inca heartland to the south in Peru. Spain replaced that brief Inca rule with its own, founding Spanish Quito in 1534 and formally organizing the territory as the Real Audiencia of Quito in 1563, a colonial judicial district that governed the highland and coastal lands of modern Ecuador for two and a half centuries.

Independence came in stages: a short-lived 1809 junta, a decade of renewed rebellion, and finally Antonio José de Sucre's decisive 1822 victory at the Battle of Pichincha, fought on the slopes directly above Quito, which secured the territory for Simón Bolívar's Gran Colombia — joining Venezuela and Colombia as the union's third pillar. When Gran Colombia collapsed in 1830, Ecuador split away in the same wave as Venezuela, adopting a flag directly descended from Francisco de Miranda's 1806 yellow-blue-red tricolor — the same shared lineage carried by its two former union partners to this day.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Ecuador, c. 1400s (late Inca era)–present, five political entries. No genuine internal governing split exists within Ecuador's own modern borders at any point in its history, so this page carries no internal-partition map. 1830 – present

Republic of Ecuador (modern)

Republic

Who & why: as Gran Colombia disintegrates under regional pressure, Ecuador separates in May 1830 under General Juan José Flores, who becomes its first president. The flag's yellow-blue-red horizontal triband descends directly from Francisco de Miranda's 1806 tricolor — the very same ancestor claimed by Colombia's and Venezuela's flags, a visible thread still running across all three former Gran Colombia states. This civil design has remained Ecuador's national flag essentially unchanged ever since.

What it represents: the plain civil triband shown here; state and war versions center the national coat of arms — a condor perched above Mount Chimborazo — on the flag's middle band, a version also flown widely as an unofficial national symbol.

1822 – 1830

Gran Colombia

Federation

Who & why: Antonio José de Sucre's decisive victory at the Battle of Pichincha on 24 May 1822, fought on the slopes directly above Quito, drives out the last royalist forces and secures the territory of the former Audiencia of Quito for Simón Bolívar's Gran Colombia, which it formally joins as a southern department. Regional resentment of rule centered on distant Bogotá builds steadily, and by 1830 the union fractures, with Ecuador seceding alongside Venezuela.

What it represents: the yellow-blue-red tricolor devised by Francisco de Miranda in 1806 and adopted by Bolívar's independence forces, flown as the common banner of the federation joining Venezuela, Colombia (New Granada), and Ecuador (Quito) under one government.

1809 – 1822

War of independence (Quito juntas)

Revolutionary government

Who & why: Quito stages one of the earliest Spanish American independence attempts on 10 August 1809, when a local junta deposes the Spanish authorities — an event sometimes called "the first cry of independence in the Americas." Royalist forces suppress it within months, but rebellion resurfaces repeatedly over the following decade, until Sucre's 1822 victory at Pichincha finally secures the territory for the independence cause.

What it represents: no single fixed flag is documented across this fractured, decade-long struggle; various local juntas and rebel forces used improvised banners, often drawing on Spanish colonial heraldry. Shown here as an informed reconstruction of the period's device-bearing banners, not a confirmed official flag.

1563 – 1809

Real Audiencia de Quito (Spanish colonial)

Colony

Who & why: Spain establishes the Real Audiencia of Quito in 1563 as a judicial and administrative district, at various points subordinate to the Viceroyalty of Peru and later the Viceroyalty of New Granada, governing the highland and coastal territory that becomes modern Ecuador. Quito grows into a significant colonial religious and artistic center over these two and a half centuries, producing the distinctive "Quito School" of devotional art.

What it represents: the Cross of Burgundy and Spanish royal colors, flown across Spain's American colonies rather than a flag specific to Quito; the Audiencia had no flag of its own, governed instead under Spain's general colonial banners and royal arms.

to 1563

Indigenous highland kingdoms and Inca conquest

No unified polity

Who & why: before the late 1400s, the northern highlands around what becomes Quito are home to the Quitu, Cara (Caranqui), and other indigenous kingdoms, loosely grouped by later chroniclers as the "Kingdom of Quito," with no single ruling authority over the whole territory. The Inca Empire conquers this region only late, under Huayna Capac in the final decades before European contact — a notably recent and contested incorporation compared to the far older Inca heartland around Cusco in Peru. Francisco Pizarro's Spanish forces arrive in the 1530s, and Sebastián de Belalcázar founds Spanish Quito in 1534, leading directly into the 1563 establishment of the Real Audiencia.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from either the pre-Inca indigenous kingdoms or the brief period of Inca rule; Andean polities used woven textile and standard-bearing traditions (such as the Inca's own unancha) rather than fixed territorial flags in the European sense, and none can be attributed specifically to this northern province with confidence. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
