Flags of Brunei

From a fifteenth- and sixteenth-century empire that ruled much of Borneo and the southern Philippines, through a long decline that reduced it to a fraction of its former territory, seven decades as a British protectorate, and full independence in 1984 — six flags tracing the shrinking and then stabilizing fortunes of one of the last remaining absolute monarchies anywhere in the world.

Brunei's yellow-white-black flag with its red crest, adopted in 1959, has flown continuously since — through the transition from protectorate to full sovereignty in 1984 without any change to its design.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Brunei, 15th century–present, six political entries. Japanese wartime occupation (1942–45) interrupts the British protectorate era and is shown as its own separate entry, splitting that protectorate into two dated spans. Sultanate of Brunei (full independence)

Sultanate, absolute

Who & why: Brunei gains full independence from Britain on 1 January 1984, the last of Britain's Southeast Asian territories to do so, under Sultan Hassanal Bolkiah, who has now reigned since 1967. Substantial oil and gas reserves fund one of the highest per-capita incomes in the region, and the sultan retains full executive power as one of the world's last true absolute monarchs, having formally implemented sharia-based criminal law from 2014 onward.

What it represents: the same flag adopted for self-government in 1959 continues unchanged into full sovereignty — yellow for the sultan, white and black diagonal bands for the chief minister and other senior officials, and the national crest in red at center.

British protectorate (self-governing)

Sultanate, protectorate

Who & why: a 1959 constitution grants Brunei internal self-government while Britain retains control of defense and foreign affairs; Sultan Omar Ali Saifuddien III declines to join the new Federation of Malaysia in 1963, in part over precedence and revenue-sharing disputes, keeping Brunei on a separate path toward eventual full independence.

What it represents: the 1959 flag adds the national crest — a crescent, wings, umbrella, and hands — in red to the earlier plain yellow-white-black design, marking the shift to self-government.

British protectorate (post-war)

Protectorate (Britain)

Who & why: British administration resumes after Japan's surrender, and the sultanate continues under the residential system, its sultan retaining internal authority under British supervision, while post-war reconstruction and growing oil revenues (from fields discovered in the 1920s) begin reshaping the small territory's economy.

What it represents: the plain yellow field with diagonal white and black bands, without the later crest — yellow for the sultan, the other two colors for his senior officials.

1942 – 1945

Japanese occupation

Occupation (Japan)

Who & why: Japan invades Borneo in December 1941 and occupies Brunei through the rest of the war, valuing its oil fields; the sultanate's own administration is left largely in place as a local instrument of Japanese rule until Australian forces liberate the territory in mid-1945.

What it represents: the Japanese naval and military ensign, flown over the occupied sultanate; no distinct Bruneian flag flew during this period.

British protectorate (established)

Protectorate (Britain)

Who & why: facing continued territorial losses to the neighboring Brooke family's Sarawak and to British North Borneo, Sultan Hashim Jalilul Alam Aqamaddin accepts British protection in 1888 to halt the sultanate's decline; a British Resident is installed from 1906, advising the sultan on all matters except local custom and Islamic religious affairs, a system that endures for over half a century.

What it represents: plain yellow, the color exclusively associated with Brunei's royalty since well before this era, serves as the sultanate's own flag through the earlier protectorate years, before the 1959 constitution adds the white, black, and crest.

Bruneian Empire and its decline

Empire / Sultanate

Who & why: the Bruneian Empire reaches its peak in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries under sultans such as Bolkiah, controlling much of Borneo's coastline and extending influence into the southern Philippines, including Manila before Spanish conquest. A long decline follows from internal succession disputes, piracy, and growing European pressure; the empire cedes Sarawak to the Brooke family (self-styled "White Rajahs") from 1841 and loses North Borneo to a British chartered company by the 1880s, shrinking to a small remnant before accepting British protection in 1888.

What it represents: no single flag is well documented across this long span of rise and decline; plain yellow, the color exclusively reserved for Brunei's royalty across its known history, is shown here as a reasonable representative color rather than a specific attested banner.
