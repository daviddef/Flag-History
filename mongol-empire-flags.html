Flags of the Mongol Empire

The largest contiguous land empire ever assembled, built in barely two generations: Temüjin unifies the fractious Mongol tribes in 1206 and, as Genghis Khan, launches conquests that within decades reach from the Pacific to the doorstep of Europe. His successors push the empire to its greatest extent, then split it — by succession dispute rather than defeat — into four khanates, each of which runs its own separate course toward absorption into the settled civilizations it had conquered.

Because it never settled on a single fixed national banner the way later states did, this page leans on the empire's one well-documented pre-heraldic emblem, the Sülde spirit-banner, and on the flags of the successor khanates once they begin minting and administering in the styles of their conquered territories.

Scope of this page: the unified Mongol Empire, 1206–1260s, and its four successor khanates through their own separate declines, c. 1260s–16th century. This empire's territory at its peak spanned, in modern terms, Mongolia, China, Russia, and most of Central Asia, Iran, and the Caucasus — far more ground than any single country page in this atlas can carry, which is exactly why it gets a page of its own rather than remaining scattered as background mentions across roughly twenty of this atlas's country pages. See Mongolia for the modern country's own later national history, China for the Yuan Dynasty's own Chinese heartland, Russia, and Central Asia hub for how those regions' own pages pick up the story on their side. 14th – 16th century

Dissolution of the khanates

Fragmented successor states

Who & why: each khanate ends on its own schedule. The Yuan dynasty in China is overthrown by the ethnic-Han Ming in 1368, its remnant court retreating to the steppe as the Northern Yuan. The Ilkhanate in Persia effectively collapses after 1335 with no agreed successor, fracturing into rival Persianate dynasties (eventually feeding into the Timurid and Safavid states). The Chagatai Khanate in Central Asia splits and re-splits from the 1340s onward, its western half absorbed into Timur's own empire by the 1370s. The Golden Horde in Russia and the Pontic-Caspian steppe fragments more slowly, splintering into the Khanates of Kazan, Astrakhan, Crimea, and Sibir through the 15th century as the Russian principalities it had once taxed — Moscow foremost among them — grow strong enough to stop paying tribute and eventually conquer the fragments in turn.

What it represents: no single flag marks this era; it is shown here as a plain gold field (the color most associated with Mongol imperial authority) labeled honestly as a dissolution rather than invented as a banner.

1271 – 1368

Yuan dynasty (China)

Khanate, imperial dynasty

Who & why: Kublai Khan, having secured the Mongol throne after the 1260s succession war with his brother Ariq Böke, formally declares the Yuan dynasty in 1271, adopting a Chinese-style dynastic name and administration while ruling as Great Khan. The Yuan completes the conquest of Song China by 1279, the only period in history a foreign dynasty rules all of China. Kublai's failed invasions of Japan (1274, 1281) mark the practical limit of Mongol naval expansion. The dynasty weakens through the 14th century under succession crises, plague, and the rebellions that produce the Ming in 1368.

What it represents: the Yuan court adopted Chinese imperial conventions for its banners; red and gold, the colors of Chinese sovereignty, are shown here as a reconstruction — no single fixed Yuan state flag in the modern sense survives.

1256 – 1335

Ilkhanate (Persia & the Middle East)

Khanate

Who & why: Hülegü, another grandson of Genghis Khan, is sent west to extend Mongol rule over Persia and Mesopotamia, sacking Baghdad in 1258 and ending the Abbasid Caliphate before his advance is checked by the Mamluks at Ain Jalut in 1260. The Ilkhanate rules as a nominally subordinate khanate under the Great Khan in China, governing a mostly Persian and Muslim population; Ghazan Khan's conversion to Islam in 1295 marks the ruling elite's own gradual absorption into the culture it conquered. The line ends without an agreed heir after 1335, and Persia fractures into competing successor dynasties.

What it represents: no fixed Ilkhanate state flag survives; the green-and-gold shown here follows the wider Persianate and Islamic royal banner conventions the khanate itself increasingly adopted, marked as a reconstruction.

1225/1260s – 1340s

Chagatai Khanate (Central Asia)

Khanate

Who & why: allotted to Chagatai, Genghis Khan's second son, as his share of the empire in the 1220s, this khanate governs the Central Asian heartland — Transoxiana, the Tarim Basin, and Semirechye — and becomes a fully separate polity after the empire's formal 1260s division. It remains more steppe-nomadic and less administratively centralized than its sibling khanates, and splits into eastern and western halves by the 1340s; the western half is absorbed into Timur's rising empire by the 1370s, while a weakened eastern remnant (Moghulistan) persists for a time further east.

What it represents: no fixed Chagatai state flag survives; the blue-and-gold shown here follows the wider Mongol steppe convention of Tengri-blue fields with gold devices, marked as a reconstruction.

1240s/1260s – 1502

Golden Horde (Russia & the western steppe)

Khanate

Who & why: founded by Batu Khan, grandson of Genghis Khan, whose westward campaigns of the 1230s–40s devastate the Rus' principalities and push raiding parties as far as Hungary and Poland before Möngke Khan's death pulls the Mongol leadership back east. The Horde settles into ruling the Pontic-Caspian steppe and holding the Russian principalities as tributaries (the "Mongol/Tatar Yoke") rather than annexing them outright, extracting tribute for roughly two and a half centuries. Its cohesion frays from the 15th century as constituent khanates — Kazan, Astrakhan, Crimea, Sibir — break away, and Moscow's own 1480 refusal of further tribute marks the effective end of Mongol suzerainty over Russia, with the rump Great Horde finally destroyed in 1502.

What it represents: "Golden Horde" (Altan Ord) likely derives from the golden tent of the khan's ordu (camp/headquarters); no fixed state flag survives from the period, so the white-and-black shown here follows steppe banner convention, marked as a reconstruction.

1206 – 1260s

Mongol Empire (unified, under Genghis and his immediate successors)

Empire, unified

Who & why: in 1206, a kurultai (grand council) on the Onon River confirms Temüjin as Genghis Khan, ruler of all the Mongol and Turkic tribes of the steppe he has spent two decades subduing and uniting. What follows is the fastest and largest campaign of territorial conquest in recorded history: the Khwarazmian Empire falls by 1221, northern China's Jin dynasty by 1234 under Ögedei, and Mongol armies reach Kyiv, Poland, and Hungary by 1241–42, withdrawing only after Ögedei's death forces a succession-driven retreat. The empire reaches its greatest contiguous extent under Möngke Khan (r. 1251–59), stretching from the Sea of Japan to the Danube. Möngke's death in 1259 triggers the Toluid Civil War between his brothers Kublai and Ariq Böke, and by the mid-1260s the empire has split, in practice, into four increasingly separate khanates that never fully reunite under one throne again.

What it represents: the empire had no single national flag in the later heraldic sense; its most consistently attested emblem is the Sülde, a horsehair standard mounted on a spear, believed to house the spirit of the ruler or the state and carried into battle and through the imperial court. Shown here as an honest, informed reconstruction of the type, not a documented fixed-design banner.

before 1206

Fragmented Mongol and Turkic steppe tribes

Fragmented tribes

Who & why: before 1206 the Mongolian steppe is home to competing, frequently warring tribal confederations — Mongols, Keraites, Naimans, Merkits, Tatars — with no single ruler over them all. Temüjin, born into a minor noble Mongol lineage around 1162, spends roughly two decades building alliances, defeating rivals, and absorbing tribes into his own following before his elevation as Genghis Khan.

What it represents: no securely documented flag or banner can be attributed to any single steppe tribe of this era with confidence; shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
