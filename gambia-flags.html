Flags of The Gambia

The Gambia is a geographic oddity before it is anything else: a narrow ribbon of territory hugging both banks of the Gambia River, surrounded on three sides by Senegal, with the border famously said to trace roughly how far a 19th-century British gunboat's cannon could fire from the water. That shape — imperial rivalry frozen into a national outline — runs through this whole lineage, from the western fringe of the Mali Empire's reach along the same river, through fragmented Mandinka and Wolof river kingdoms, a British river-colony administered first from Sierra Leone, independence in 1965, and a brief, unusual confederation with the country that surrounds it.

Only one flag has ever flown as Gambia's own national banner: the red-blue-green horizontal bands adopted at independence on 18 February 1965, unchanged through the move to a republic in 1970 and through the Senegambia Confederation of the 1980s.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day The Gambia, c. 1235–present, six political entities. Because Gambia's own national flag has never changed since its 1965 adoption, later entries track political-system changes (dominion to republic; the Senegambia Confederation) rather than flag changes — the confederation altered Gambia's diplomatic and defense arrangements but never its flag, so that episode is folded into the surrounding entry's narrative rather than shown as a separate banner. 1970 – present

Republic of The Gambia

Republic, independent

Who & why: Gambia becomes a republic within the Commonwealth on 24 April 1970, following a referendum, with Prime Minister Dawda Jawara becoming president. From 1982 to 1989 Gambia enters the Senegambia Confederation with Senegal — the country that surrounds it on three landward sides — a real attempt at integrating defense, security, and economic policy after Senegalese troops helped put down a 1981 coup attempt; the confederation dissolves in 1989 without ever merging the two states, and Gambia's own flag and sovereignty are unaffected throughout. A 1994 military coup under Yahya Jammeh is later reversed by a peaceful transfer of power in 2017 after he loses an election and eventually steps down.

What it represents: the same 1965 flag continues to fly, unaltered by the change to a republic or by the confederation years — red for the sun and the savanna to the north and south of the river, blue for the Gambia River itself, green for the forests and farmland, and the two thin white fringes for unity and peace between Gambia's peoples.

1965 – 1970

Independent Gambia (Commonwealth realm)

Commonwealth realm

Who & why: The Gambia gains independence from Britain on 18 February 1965, the smallest country in mainland Africa, still hemmed in on three sides by Senegal. Initially it remains a Commonwealth realm with Queen Elizabeth II as head of state, represented locally by a governor-general, with Dawda Jawara as prime minister. That arrangement lasts five years before the 1970 referendum converts the country into a republic.

What it represents: the newly designed national flag replaces the colonial Blue Ensign outright at independence — deliberately avoiding both the British Union Jack and any color associated with a single political party, with blue chosen specifically to mark the Gambia River running the length of the country.

1888 – 1965

Gambia Colony and Protectorate

Colony and protectorate

Who & why: Britain separates Gambia from the Sierra Leone administration in 1888, creating a distinct colony (the narrow riverine settlement, run directly) and protectorate (the wider hinterland along both banks, governed through indirect rule over existing chiefs) — formalizing the odd, river-hugging shape that gunboat-range diplomacy with France had already sketched out. The territory remains Britain's smallest, most geographically constrained African possession right up to independence in 1965.

What it represents: the standard British colonial Blue Ensign, now carrying Gambia's own distinguishing badge in the fly — a device built around the Gambia River and its oil-palm groves, the colony's principal trade good, set against the same navy field and Union Jack canton used across the empire.

1816 – 1888

British settlement of Bathurst (administered from Sierra Leone)

Colonial settlement

Who & why: in 1816 Britain establishes a fortified trading post, Bathurst (today's Banjul), on an island at the mouth of the Gambia River, chiefly to suppress the Atlantic slave trade and secure river-borne trade in groundnuts and other goods against French rivals based in Senegal. For most of this period the settlement is administered as part of the British West African Settlements out of Freetown, Sierra Leone, rather than as its own separate colony — the narrow enclave along the river is already taking the shape that French Senegal's surrounding claims will later lock in.

What it represents: no Gambia-specific colonial badge yet exists; the settlement flies the generic British Blue Ensign of the period — navy field, Union Jack canton, no distinguishing device in the fly — shared with other undifferentiated British possessions until a proper colony is organized in 1888.

c. 1600 – 1816

Fragmented Mandinka and Wolof river kingdoms

Fragmented kingdoms

Who & why: as Mali's imperial authority over the river fades by the early 1600s, the Gambia River corridor splits into a string of small, independent Mandinka and Wolof kingdoms — Niumi, Baddibu, Niani, Wuli, Kombo, and others — each controlling its own stretch of bank and levying tolls on river trade. No single ruler ever again unites the river valley before colonization; European trading companies (Portuguese, then increasingly British and French) deal separately with each riverbank kingdom for slaves, ivory, and later groundnuts, playing them against one another for access and favorable terms.

What it represents: as with Mali's own western reach, no securely documented territorial flag survives for any of these river kingdoms; authority was marked by royal regalia, drums, and personal banners of individual rulers rather than fixed state flags. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

c. 1235 – c. 1600

Mali Empire's western reach along the Gambia River

Imperial periphery

Who & why: Sundiata Keita founds the Mali Empire around 1235, and over the following century Mali's authority extends west to the Gambia River valley, which becomes a tributary periphery of the empire rather than a core province — local Mandinka rulers along the river acknowledge Mali's overlordship and trade links (gold, salt, kola) while retaining considerable local autonomy. Mali's own power peaks under Mansa Musa in the 1320s–30s and then fragments slowly over the following two centuries, with its grip on the distant Gambia periphery loosening well before the imperial core in the Niger valley finally collapses.

What it represents: no flag is documented for Mali's own imperial court, let alone for its western riverine periphery; medieval West African empires marked authority through regalia, praise-singing griots, and symbolic objects rather than territorial banners. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
