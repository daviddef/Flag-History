Flags of Lithuania

Europe's last pagan realm, holding out against the Northern Crusades for a full century after its neighbors fell — then, within two more centuries, ruling a grand duchy so vast it touched the Black Sea. Partition, three foreign occupations in five years, and a human chain of two million people later, the yellow-green-red tricolor a democratic council chose in 1918 is exactly the flag flying over Vilnius today.

Lithuania's colors were fixed by the Council of Lithuania in 1918 — yellow for ripening wheat and prosperity, green for forests and hope, red for the land's own courage and the blood spent defending it — and restored unchanged in 1990, the clearest possible statement that independence was being resumed, not invented.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Lithuania, from the pagan tribal era to the present, nine political entities. Part of a larger atlas — see the map and linked pages at the bottom.

1990 – present

Republic of Lithuania

Sovereign state

Who & why: the Supreme Council declares independence restored on 11 March 1990, the first Soviet republic to do so — well before the USSR's own collapse and at real risk: Soviet troops and tanks stormed Vilnius's TV tower on 13 January 1991, killing fourteen unarmed civilians defending it. Moscow does not concede, and international recognition (including, finally, the Soviet Union's own) arrives only after the failed August 1991 coup, in the same September week as Latvia and Estonia. Lithuania joins NATO and the EU in 2004.

What it represents: the identical yellow-green-red tricolor first adopted by the Council of Lithuania in 1918, restored without alteration — a deliberate statement that this is a resumption of statehood, not a new founding.

1944 – 1990

Lithuanian SSR (second Soviet period)

Communist state

Who & why: the Red Army reoccupies Lithuania through the summer and autumn of 1944 as the Wehrmacht retreats; Moscow treats the 1941–44 German interruption as a mere gap, not a lapse of the 1940 annexation. Mass deportations to Siberia resume, and the "Forest Brothers" partisan resistance fights on into the early 1950s. János-Kádár-style "goulash" liberalization never really arrives here; the Singing Revolution of 1987–91, capped by the 675-kilometer human chain of the 23 August 1989 Baltic Way, finally breaks Soviet control.

What it represents: the standard Soviet republic banner, red with the gold hammer, sickle and star; a 1953 redesign added the thin green-and-white stripe near the bottom edge, the only visual nod to a distinct Lithuanian identity permitted under Moscow's rule.

1941 – 1944

Nazi occupation (Reichskommissariat Ostland)

Fascist occupation

Who & why: Operation Barbarossa reaches Lithuania within its first days in June 1941; a short-lived, unrecognized Provisional Government in Kaunas is pushed aside within weeks as Germany folds the country into the Reichskommissariat Ostland, a civilian occupation zone administered from Riga alongside Latvia, Estonia, and Belarus. The occupation brings the near-total destruction of Lithuania's Jewish population, one of the highest proportional death tolls of the Holocaust anywhere in Europe.

What it represents: a German occupation authority flag rather than any Lithuanian national symbol — shown here in stylized form rather than reproducing the regime's actual imagery. No Lithuanian government of any kind existed during these three years.

1940 – 1941

Lithuanian SSR (Soviet annexation)

Communist state

Who & why: the secret protocols of the August 1939 Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact place Lithuania in the Soviet sphere; a base agreement is forced on the country that October, and the Red Army occupies it outright in June 1940. A rigged "People's Seimas" election follows within weeks, and the new assembly duly petitions to join the USSR — formal annexation is complete by early August 1940. Mass deportations to Siberia begin within the year, hitting tens of thousands of Lithuanians before the German invasion interrupts Soviet rule less than twelve months later.

What it represents: the plain Soviet republic banner as it existed before the 1953 redesign — red field with a gold hammer, sickle and star, no distinguishing stripe of any kind, visually indistinguishable from most other Soviet republics of the moment.

1918 – 1940

Republic of Lithuania (interwar)

Republic

Who & why: the Council of Lithuania declares independence on 16 February 1918 as the German-occupied east collapses toward the war's end. A separate war of independence follows through 1919–20 against Bolshevik, Bermontian, and Polish forces — Poland seizes Vilnius itself in 1920, forcing the young republic to govern from Kaunas as a "temporary capital" for the next two decades. Democratic government gives way to Antanas Smetona's authoritarian rule after a 1926 coup, though the state itself remains internationally recognized and independent until the 1940 Soviet occupation.

What it represents: a brand-new tricolor, adopted by the Council in April 1918 specifically because it had no direct precedent in the old Grand Duchy's own heraldry — a deliberate break asserting a modern, popular Lithuanian nation rather than a revived dynastic state.

1795 – 1918

Russian Empire rule

Occupied territory

Who & why: the 1795 Third Partition of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth erases both partners outright, absorbing the Grand Duchy into the Russian Empire as the Vilna, Kaunas, and Suwałki governorates. Two major uprisings, in 1831 and 1863, are crushed; the tsarist response to the second includes a decades-long ban on printing the Lithuanian language in Latin script, defeated in practice by book smugglers running Lithuanian-language texts across the Prussian border. World War I brings German occupation from 1915, opening the path to the 1918 declaration.

What it represents: the standard Russian imperial civil tricolor, flown across the whole empire with no distinct Lithuanian symbol permitted — 123 years during which no Lithuanian flag of any kind exists.

1569 – 1795

Grand Duchy of Lithuania (Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth)

Grand duchy (Commonwealth)

Who & why: the 1569 Union of Lublin, prompted by the pressure of the Livonian War, converts the two-century-old personal union of crowns into the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth — a single elected monarchy and parliament (the Sejm), though Lithuania keeps its own army, treasury, laws, and administration throughout. The Commonwealth becomes one of Europe's largest and, for its era, most religiously tolerant states before a long 18th-century decline ends in the three partitions of 1772, 1793, and 1795.

What it represents: Lithuania's own Vytis — the mounted, sword-bearing knight, in use as the Grand Duchy's arms since at least the 14th century — continues to fly as the state's own banner within the Commonwealth, distinct from the Polish Eagle even after the 1569 union of governments.

1253 – 1569

Grand Duchy of Lithuania (independent)

Grand duchy

Who & why: Duke Mindaugas unifies the Lithuanian tribes and is crowned King of Lithuania by papal authority in 1253, the only king the country ever has — the title lapses after his 1263 assassination, and his successors rule instead as grand dukes. Under Gediminas and Algirdas in the 14th century, the grand duchy expands explosively into the power vacuum left by the Mongol-shattered Kievan lands, at its height stretching from the Baltic all the way to the Black Sea, one of the largest states in Europe and, for most of this period, the continent's last openly pagan realm. The 1386 Union of Krewo brings Grand Duke Jogaila the Polish crown and Catholic baptism — a personal union of monarchs, not governments — while the grand duchy itself stays entirely self-ruled until 1569.

What it represents: the Vytis (Lithuanian: "the chase" or "pursuer") — a white armored knight on a white horse, sword raised, on a red field — attested from the 14th century and still Lithuania's national coat of arms today, among the oldest state symbols in continuous use anywhere in Europe.

c. 1150 – 1253

Pagan Lithuanian tribal lands

Tribal lands

Who & why: before Mindaugas's unification, Lithuanian-speaking Baltic tribes hold the forested south of the Baltic coast, organized around local dukes with no central authority. The Northern Crusades, launched by the Livonian Brothers of the Sword and later the Teutonic Order, conquer Latvia and Estonia to the north through the 13th century, but Grand Duke Traidenis's crushing 1236 victory over the Sword-Brothers at Saule shows the crusaders they picked the wrong target here — the beginning of a century and a half of successful Lithuanian resistance that makes it the last major pagan state in Europe.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from this pre-heraldic, pre-Christian era; the tribes are recorded fighting under duke-specific war banners and totemic standards rather than a fixed territorial flag. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
